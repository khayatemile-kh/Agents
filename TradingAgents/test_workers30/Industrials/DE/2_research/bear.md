## **CRITICAL FLAW #2: THE DEBT-TO-EQUITY RATIO IS NOT "NORMAL"—IT'S A STRUCTURAL VULNERABILITY**

### **The Bull's Sleight of Hand on Leverage**

The bull acknowledges the 376.02 D/E ratio but then performs verbal gymnastics to rationalize it:

> "A significant portion of DE's debt is actually **debt issued by the captive finance subsidiary**, not the core manufacturing business."

This is precisely the problem, and I'll explain why the bull has this **completely backwards.**

#### **How Captive Finance Actually Works (And Why It's Dangerous)**

Yes, John Deere Financial does issue debt to finance customer purchases. But here's what the bull is omitting:

1. **The Captive Finance Debt is NOT Segregated**: When you consolidate financial statements (which equity analysts do), John Deere Financial's debt appears on DE's balance sheet alongside the manufacturing operations' debt. **The bondholders and creditors see one legal entity at risk.**

2. **Interconnected Risk**: If the manufacturing business hits a rough patch, John Deere Financial suffers too. Why?
   - Customer defaults rise in a recession (farmers/contractors can't pay financing)
   - Loan loss reserves must increase
   - Interest income falls as receivables quality deteriorates
   - **The parent company (manufacturing) must backstop the finance subsidiary with capital**

3. **The Math Reveals the Real Risk**: 
   - The bull cites $4.52B in free cash flow as "robust debt service capacity"
   - But if we work backwards from a 376 D/E ratio and assume equity of ~$33B (rough estimate), **total debt could be $124B+**
   - Interest expense on $124B of debt at current rates (4-5% average) = $5-6B annually
   - **Free cash flow of $4.52B does NOT cover this**, especially when capex, dividends, and working capital are factored in

4. **The Company Must Continue Rolling Debt**: With debt service approaching or exceeding free cash flow, **DE must continuously refinance and issue new debt.** This is dangerous because:
   - If credit markets tighten (which they often do in recessions), refinancing becomes difficult
   - Rates on new issuances would rise, compressing margins
   - Covenant violations become possible if EBITDA falls

#### **Why This Matters Right Now**

The market data shows that the Fed is expected to keep rates elevated through 2026. For a company with a debt burden of this magnitude, **sustained 4-5% rates are margin-compressing.** The bull argues this is already "priced in," but I'd counter:

- **If rates were truly priced in, the stock wouldn't be trading at 38x TTM P/E**
- **If rates were truly priced in, guidance wouldn't show 26.4% EPS growth** (because higher rates would suppress both volumes and margins)

The bull is asking you to believe two contradictory things simultaneously: rates are high (correctly noting this), yet growth will surge to 26.4% anyway. **Pick one.**
