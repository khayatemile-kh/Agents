## Let Me Start With What the Aggressive Analyst Gets Right But Oversells

The negative FCF isn't just "accounting noise" or a "temporary timing artifact," despite what the conservative analyst argues. Here's why the distinction matters more than the conservative analyst admits:

Yes, utilities are capital-intensive and regulatory environments typically allow rate recovery for mandated capex. That's all true. But—and this is critical—the *timing and certainty* of that rate recovery is what separates a healthy utility from a stressed one. Duke Energy's debt increased 5.6% year-over-year while revenue grew roughly 1%. That's not the ratio you want to see. The conservative analyst says this is "how the entire sector operates," and technically they're right. But that doesn't mean it's sustainable *indefinitely*, and it definitely doesn't mean it's equally sustainable across all utilities.

Here's the data point both analysts are glossing over: Duke's interest expense grew 6.7%—that's faster than debt growth itself. Do you know what that means? Either debt is being issued at higher coupon rates (refinancing stress), or the company is rolling over older debt at higher rates (and facing more refinancing ahead). The conservative analyst notes that ROE is 9.86%, which is within the regulatory band of 8-10%. But here's the wrinkle: if interest rates stay elevated, future rate cases might not recover *all* of Duke's increased financing costs. The regulator might allow an 8-10% ROE on equity, but if debt servicing costs rise faster than the regulator allows rates to rise, there's compression.

This isn't theoretical. It's happening *right now* in the regulatory environment. Federal rates are stable or potentially higher for longer, according to the macro report (89% probability of no Fed cuts in 2026). That environment is genuinely tough for a company with high debt that's refinancing at the margins.

So the aggressive analyst is right to worry about FCF. Where they go wrong is the timeline and the action. They want to sell *now* because FCF is negative *today*. But here's the problem: if you sell today at $120 because you're worried about refinancing stress in Q4 2026 or Q1 2027, you're making a binary bet that things won't improve. And they might.

But they're not acknowledging what happens if the monitoring reveals bad news.
