## I. THE MACRO ARGUMENT DOESN'T JUSTIFY THE RISK PREMIUM

The bull wants to frame stable rates as uniformly positive for NEE. **It's not.** Yes, 89% probability of no Fed rate cuts is "stable," but stable doesn't mean supportive for valuations at this level.

**Here's what the bull glossed over:**

**Rates are already elevated.** The 2.4% 10-Year Treasury yield the bull referenced isn't some baseline from which we get relief—it's already historically high relative to 2010-2020 norms. NEE's cost of capital to refinance the **$110.2B debt pile** isn't getting cheaper; it's locking in at elevated levels.

Consider this reality: NEE just issued **$6.9B in debt in Q2 2026 alone**. If rates stay at current elevated levels, that refinancing cost persists for *years* on the company's $10.3B annual capex program. The bull says this is "sustainable" because capex generates 7-10% returns. But here's the problem: **regulated utility returns are contractually set by state regulators at 9-10%, not "up to 10."** The company can't arbitrage its way out of leverage with exceptional returns—it's locked into mediocre single-digit spreads between cost of capital (~5-6%) and returns on equity (~9-10%).

**The recession insurance argument is overstated.** Yes, only 8% recession risk is priced in. But that's *as of September 1, 2026*. The world changes fast. A China shock, a credit event, or an earnings recession can flip sentiment within weeks. And here's the kicker: **even without a full recession, earnings growth can stall.** If hyperscaler capex cycles flatten (which they will, once the AI infrastructure build is complete), NEE's growth evaporates. The bull is betting on the data center tailwind lasting 10-15 years. I'm betting the *intensity* of that tailwind peaks in 2027-2028 and normalizes thereafter.

**The real macro risk: demand destruction in regulated utilities.** As renewable adoption accelerates and distributed energy resources (DERs) proliferate, regulated utilities like NEE face **structural demand headwinds**. Rooftop solar, battery storage, and microgrids reduce electricity consumption from the grid. NEE's regulated tariff model assumes steady or growing throughput. If throughput declines (even slightly), the regulated returns don't compress—the *rate base* gets squeezed, and shareholders absorb the hit. This isn't theoretical; it's happening in California, Hawaii, and Massachusetts *right now*.

## II. THE DATA CENTER BACKLOG IS REAL BUT NOT A PANACEA—IT'S A TRAP

The bull's treatment of the 35.1 GW backlog is where the argument becomes dangerously optimistic. Let me be specific about why this backlog overstates NEE's real growth opportunity.

**First, the backlog is NOT equal to revenue.** The 35.1 GW is *contracted capacity*, but it converts to earnings over 10-15 years, not 2-3 years. The bull says capex of $150B+ "flows through earnings for 10-15 years"—true, but that capex is being deployed *today*, while the earnings accrue *gradually*. This is a classic timing mismatch. NEE's leverage is rising *now* (1.62x D/E, up from ~1.45x in 2024), but the earnings benefit is deferred.

**Second, the regulatory moat argument is backwards.** The bull claims NEE's regulated model "insulates" it from data center moratorium risk because "rate recovery mechanisms" guarantee returns. This is wrong.

Here's the regulatory reality: If a state imposes a data center moratorium, regulators *can* deny rate recovery for stranded capex. It happens. Look at what happened with coal plant retirements—utilities spent billions on coal infrastructure, and regulators disallowed recovery when policy shifted. The same risk applies to data center power plants. If Virginia or Texas (where much of NEE's data center backlog sits) decides data centers are straining the grid, they can:

1. **Deny certificates of public convenience and necessity (CPCN)** for new data center interconnections
2. **Impose lengthy environmental reviews** that delay projects by 2-3 years
3. **Require utilities to prioritize residential/commercial load** over data center load during scarcity

The 71% probability of *at least one state* moratorium isn't a minor edge case—it's a baseline scenario. And once one state moves, others follow. Virginia's data center moratorium ripples to North Carolina, Tennessee, and beyond.

**Third, the capex intensity is unsustainable without demand.** The bull assumes 35.1 GW converts to stable, long-term revenue. But here's the industry dynamic: once the AI infrastructure wave matures (likely 2028-2030), hyperscaler capex will normalize to *replacement level*, not growth level. At that point, NEE's $10.3B annual capex program (which is elevated relative to historical $7-8B levels) will compress. The debt taken on to fund this capex spike will *remain*, but the incremental earnings will *disappear*.

**The Fervo comparison backfires.** The bull cited Fervo's 396 MW Google deal as validation of "real capex." Correct—Fervo is real. But notice what happened: **Fervo's stock spiked 28% on a single contract**. This means the market was *underpricing* Fervo's ability to win deals. Now apply this logic to NEE: if the market was *overpricing* NEE's ability to execute on 35.1 GW (which NEE's flat stock price and 6.9% drawdown suggests), then the backlog might deliver less upside than the bull assumes.
