## The Negative Working Capital "Feature" Masquerades as a Liability

The aggressive analyst frames negative working capital as a SaaS-like competitive advantage—that Equifax collects cash before delivering services, creating a virtuous float. I need to be direct: this is misleading.

Yes, recurring revenue businesses often have negative working capital. But there's a critical distinction the aggressive analyst glosses over: **Equifax's negative working capital is problematic *because of the debt structure*.**

A healthy SaaS company with negative working capital typically has low debt (because cash upfront funds growth) and can operate with that feature indefinitely. Equifax is the opposite: heavily leveraged, with $5.3 billion in net debt, carrying that negative working capital position while servicing massive debt obligations.

Here's what happens in a stress scenario: If customer acquisition slows, or if clients prepay less aggressively (which happens in credit stress environments), that negative working capital buffer evaporates. The company suddenly faces a cash flow cliff—not because the underlying business is broken, but because the financial structure can't absorb working capital swings. With $5.47 billion in total debt, that's not theoretical. That's material.

The aggressive analyst points to $947.5 million in free cash flow and argues that proves working capital isn't problematic. But FCF already includes working capital changes. If working capital deteriorates, FCF deteriorates. We don't have a baseline "good" FCF that tells us how much buffer remains. We have one data point at a single moment in time.

## The Macro "Tailwind" is Actually the Exact Opposite

The aggressive analyst inverts the consumer debt stress narrative and claims rising delinquencies create *more* demand for Equifax's risk assessment services. Let me be blunt: this argument confuses a cyclical uptick with structural support.

Yes, in a mid-to-late credit cycle, lenders increase their screening activity as credit standards tighten. I don't dispute that. But the aggressive analyst misses the *duration* of that boost. It lasts until one of two things happens:

**Either (1) lending volumes collapse** as lenders retreat to fortress balance sheets, or **(2) the regulatory environment responds to delinquency stress** by imposing stricter origination requirements, which reduces lending volumes while paradoxically reducing *per-loan* credit inquiry volumes (fewer loans = fewer inquiries, even if per-loan intensity increases).

The macro data supports scenario (2): we're seeing record consumer debt ($2.6 trillion in Canada alone) *and* rising delinquencies. That's not a stable equilibrium. That's a credit cycle approaching inflection. Equifax is positioned to benefit in the quarter or two *before* that inflection hits, not after.

And crucially, the aggressive analyst's entire macro thesis depends on the Fed holding rates at 89% probability. But **that doesn't mean rates stay elevated forever.** The moment inflation signals weaken (and with record debt levels, the odds of deflationary pressure are non-trivial), the Fed will cut. When that happens, lending volumes expand, yes—but guess what also happens? Competition increases, margins compress, and risk assessment becomes commoditized more rapidly.

The 89% Fed hold probability is a short-term anchor, not a long-term moat. The aggressive analyst is trading a 6-month macro setup as if it's a multi-year secular trend.
