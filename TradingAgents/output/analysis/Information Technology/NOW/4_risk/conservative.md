## The Real Problem: You Cannot Value Software Multiples in a Deteriorating Liquidity Environment

Let me walk through the logic that the aggressive and neutral camps seem to be missing entirely.

ServiceNow trades at 27.3x forward PE. The aggressive analyst acknowledges this is "reasonable" for a SaaS company with NOW's growth profile. The neutral camp will likely make the same argument. Both are making a valuation assessment in a vacuum.

But valuation is not conducted in a vacuum. Valuation is contextual. And here is the context that matters: **the company's balance sheet has deteriorated so severely that its financing cost structure is about to change materially.**

When a company has $8.45 billion in debt, has issued $2.08 billion in commercial paper that rolls over every 90 days, and is operating in a "higher-for-longer" rate environment with 89% probability of NO Fed cuts through 2026, the cost of capital rises. This is not a technical market-timing question. This is a corporate finance question.

Do you understand what happens when refinancing costs rise for a leveraged software company?

The company faces a choice:

1. **Raise rates on debt refinancing** (increasing interest expense, reducing net income, compressing margins further)
2. **Reduce capex and R&D** (slowing innovation, weakening competitive position)
3. **Raise equity** (diluting existing shareholders)
4. **Cut dividends** (signaling financial stress to the market)
5. **Sell assets** (admitting the acquisition strategy has failed)

All five of these outcomes are *negative* for equity holders. And they are all becoming more likely as refinancing windows approach.

The aggressive analyst seems to think that by trimming 20-30% now, they dodge this bullet. But what happens to the remaining 70-80%? It still faces the same structural financing risk. You don't de-risk a balance sheet problem by selling 30% of a position. You de-risk it by acknowledging that the valuation multiple you paid was based on a financial structure that no longer exists.

## Here's What the Aggressive Analyst Got Wrong About Working Capital

The aggressive analyst stated correctly that working capital collapsed to negative $3.64 billion. But I think they underestimated what this actually means.

Negative working capital is not inherently a crisis for a SaaS company. Many SaaS companies operate with negative working capital because they collect cash from customers upfront (deferred revenue) and pay their suppliers 30-60 days later. That's a feature of the business model, not a bug.

**But the deterioration of negative $2.09 billion in a single quarter is the problem.** That level of deterioration suggests one of two things:

**First possibility:** The company is funding large acquisition-related payments. The $6.02 billion debt surge suggests this. When you acquire a company, you often take on debt, pay the purchase price in cash, and the combined entity suddenly shows higher debt and lower cash. This is typical M&A behavior.

**But here's the critical question:** Are these acquisitions accretive to earnings? Are they expanding the TAM? Or are they defensive—attempts to buy capability because NOW can't develop it organically?

Looking at the operating margin collapse from 13.4% to 4.1%, I would argue these acquisitions are *not yet accretive*, and the integration costs are substantial. This suggests management made an aggressive bet that has not paid off—at least not yet.

**Second possibility:** The company's cash generation is slowing, and negative working capital is expanding because of operational stress, not just balance sheet mechanics. If customer retention is weakening, or if churn is accelerating, then accounts receivable would decline and current liabilities would rise. The deterioration we're seeing could signal underlying business weakness masked by topline revenue growth.

The aggressive analyst dismissed this risk because "NOW has $5.15 billion in free cash flow." But free cash flow is trailing. It tells you what happened in the past. The deterioration *right now* tells you what might happen next.
