That's not a healthy balance sheet. That's a company using leverage to fund shareholder returns. When you reduce equity through massive buybacks ($809 million in H1 2026 alone) while maintaining or increasing debt, you mechanically inflate your ROE because you're dividing the same or growing earnings by a shrinking equity base. It's a financial engineering artifact, not genuine operational excellence.

A 10% earnings decline on this balance sheet structure could lead to covenant concerns, credit rating downgrades, or forced capital raising at unfavorable terms. That's not a "capital-efficient profit machine." That's a company extracting every dollar for shareholders today while building structural fragility for tomorrow.

The aggressive analyst wants us to ignore this because the buybacks and debt reduction look good on the quarterly release. ## THE MACRO ARGUMENT CUTS BOTH WAYS—AND NOT THE WAY THEY THINK

The aggressive analyst claims the macro setup is a "tailwind" because recession odds are 8% and the Fed is holding rates steady. They argue this supports discretionary spending on collectibles and fashion. But I need to point out they're cherry-picking the macro narrative to fit a bullish thesis.

Let's think about what 8% recession odds actually means. It means there's a *92% probability* of no recession, which sounds benign. But here's the trap: prediction markets are forward-looking and incorporate current momentum. If we're nine months away from the end of 2026, and the market is assigning 8% recession probability, that actually reflects a significant volume of tail risk concern. In September 2019, recession odds were similarly low. In January 2020, they were still modest. Markets don't price in recessions until they're obvious.

Moreover, the "higher-for-longer rates actually help" argument is economically backward. Yes, lower *expected* rate cuts theoretically increase the present value of cash flows. But higher realized rates mean:

1. **Consumer financing gets tighter.** Discretionary purchases on credit—exactly eBay's core business—get more expensive. Higher APRs on financing for collectibles and fashion purchases means lower transaction volumes and smaller check sizes.

2. **Credit spreads widen in stress scenarios.** eBay's $7.13 billion debt load becomes more expensive to refinance if risk sentiment deteriorates. The company is currently managing a moderate debt load with modest margins. If credit spreads widen 200 basis points (not unprecedented during volatility), their refinancing costs spike materially.

3. **Consumer discretionary retraction is typically first.** In economic slowdowns, people cut back on collectibles, vintage fashion, and hobby purchases before they cut back on essentials. eBay's Depop and second-hand categories are exactly where consumer retrenchment hits hardest.

The aggressive analyst is treating macro as supportive because recession odds are low *today*. But if those odds re-price sharply—and they can, very quickly—eBay has exactly the wrong characteristics to weather it: leverage, discretionary-focused business model, modest organic growth, and slowing momentum.
