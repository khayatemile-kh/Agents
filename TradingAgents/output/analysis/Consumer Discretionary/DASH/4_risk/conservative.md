## **THE GROSS MARGIN EXPANSION ARGUMENT OBSCURES THE REAL PROFITABILITY CRISIS**

The aggressive analyst wants us to celebrate the 190 basis point gross margin expansion to 52.7% as proof the core business is strengthening. But this is selective data reading that ignores the elephant in the room: **despite gross margin expansion, net income collapsed 29.8% year-over-year.**

Think about what this actually means operationally. The company improved its ability to extract margin from the delivery transaction itself—that's real and positive. But simultaneously, the company deployed that margin windfall into a 37.7% increase in operating expenses. We're not looking at a temporary investment phase; we're looking at a company that's spending incremental margin gains faster than it can generate them.

Here's the mathematical reality the aggressive case glosses over: If gross margins expanded 190 basis points and revenue grew 35.6%, gross profit should have grown proportionally—and it did, growing from $1,668M to $2,347M, a 40.7% increase. But operating income? It fell from $163M to $158M. **Operating income actually declined while the company was harvesting margin expansion.** That's not "strategic investment phase"—that's operational dysfunction.

The aggressive analyst projects that OpEx growth will "begin decelerating" to 15-20% annualized. On what basis? The company just demonstrated it will consume margin gains by spending 37.7% more on operating expenses. There's no evidence in the trailing data that this spending intensity is moderating. In fact, R&D spending jumped 52.4%—that's acceleration, not moderation.

We're being asked to trust that management will suddenly exhibit spending discipline they haven't demonstrated yet. That's not analysis; that's faith. And faith is exactly what conservative risk management is designed to eliminate.

## **THE RETAIL PARTNERSHIP NARRATIVE IS PREMATURE AND UNVALIDATED**

Now, let's address the competitive moat argument, because this is where I see the most dangerous conflation of optionality with certainty. The aggressive analyst treats the Kohl's, Gap, and Carter's partnerships as proof of embedded competitive advantage. But let me ask the critical question: **where is the revenue evidence that these partnerships are accretive?**

The partnerships were announced in late August. Q2 2026 results were reported before any meaningful contribution from these deals. We have exactly zero quarters of actual earnings data showing whether these partnerships drive incremental margin or simply cannibalize existing revenue at lower unit economics.

Here's the risk dynamic the aggressive case doesn't adequately weight: **retail partnerships create operational complexity and negotiating leverage favors the retailer, not the logistics platform.** Kohl's, Gap, and Carter's didn't choose DASH altruistically. They chose DASH because DASH offered terms more favorable than Amazon or Uber. That means margin compression on retail delivery versus food delivery. The company is trading profitable food delivery volume for lower-margin retail logistics to chase top-line growth.

Is that value-destructive? Maybe not. But it's not the moat-strengthening narrative we're being sold. It's exposure diversification, which is fine, but it doesn't justify maintaining a 27.76x forward multiple on a company that's diluting its margin profile by design.

And here's what really concerns me: **the back-to-school catalyst is a temporary demand surge that won't repeat at this intensity for twelve months.** The aggressive analyst literally says "we have exactly 30-60 days of favorable seasonality remaining." That's the bull case. We're supposed to hold through Q3 earnings on a bet that temporary seasonality proves something about normalized profitability. But seasonal demand normalizes. When October arrives and back-to-school demand evaporates, we'll see what the underlying retail delivery business actually generates. That's when margin reality becomes apparent.

Holding through that cycle to validate a thesis based on temporary demand is exactly the kind of leverage to narrative that destroys capital.
