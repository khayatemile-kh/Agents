## **PART 1: DECONSTRUCTING THE "STRUCTURAL DEMAND TAILWIND" ARGUMENT**

### **The LEAP Engine Growth Story Has a Dangerous Counternarrative**

The bull correctly notes that LEAP deliveries are up 41% year-over-year. This is true. But let me ask a critical question: **What happens when you're growing at 41% and STILL can't meet demand?**

Here's what the bull *isn't* telling you:

**1. LEAP Growth is Already Priced In—Dangerously So**

The market has incorporated this 41% growth into a 38.86x P/E multiple. Let's do the math:

- Current Stock Price: $331.09
- Forward EPS: $9.07
- Forward P/E: 36.31x
- Historical Average Aerospace P/E: 18-22x

The bull argues this is justified by 12%+ earnings growth. But here's the uncomfortable truth: **To justify a 36x P/E multiple, you don't need 12% growth—you need perpetual 7-8% growth forever.** 

And that's the problem.

**LEAP's growth will inevitably decelerate.** Here's why:

- Aircraft backlogs are finite. Boeing and Airbus combined have ~10 years of backlog *at current production rates*. But production rates are *increasing*, which compresses the backlog timeline.
- Once backlogs normalize to 5-7 years (still healthy, but not exceptional), growth decelerates from 15-20% to 7-10%.
- When that happens, a 36x P/E becomes unsustainable, and the stock gets re-rated downward—potentially to 20-25x, implying 35-40% downside.

**The bull is betting on perpetual 15%+ growth. The market is increasingly uncertain about that assumption.**

### **Commercial Aviation Demand is Cyclical, Not Structural**

The bull presents global aviation recovery as a "structural tailwind." I would argue it's a *cyclical upturn that's nearing maturity*.

**Consider the timeline:**
- Post-COVID recovery began mid-2020
- Peak capacity and booking occurred 2023-2024
- Demand normalization began 2025-2026
- Historical cycle suggests peak is 2-3 years post-recovery, then normalization

**Current leading indicators suggest demand is plateauing:**

According to IATA data trends, while absolute travel volumes remain elevated, *growth rates* are decelerating:
- 2021-2022: +50% YoY growth (recovery phase)
- 2023-2024: +10-15% YoY growth (normalization)
- 2025-2026: +5-8% YoY growth (maturation)

As growth rates compress, LEAP engine delivery growth will compress. The 41% we're seeing today is the *peak* of the cycle, not the new baseline. **The bull is extrapolating peak cycle growth as permanent.**

### **Deferred Revenue Isn't as Safe as the Bull Claims**

The bull highlights $19.2 billion in deferred revenue as "nearly 40% of annual revenue already committed and waiting to be recognized."

This sounds bulletproof until you examine the fine print:

**1. Deferred Revenue is Subject to Performance Risk**

Aerospace programs are notorious for delays, cost overruns, and schedule slippage. Examples abound:
- Boeing 737 MAX: 18-month grounding caused cascading revenue recognition delays
- Airbus A380: Years of production delays
- GEK800 military engines: Still in early stages; defense timelines are notoriously unpredictable

If GE's production encounters supply chain disruptions (still a persistent risk post-COVID), quality issues, or engineering challenges, deferred revenue gets *reclassified*, not eliminated. The $19.2B doesn't vanish—but the *timing* of revenue recognition gets pushed out, pressuring EPS in near-term quarters.

**2. Deferred Revenue Carries Margin Risk**

Here's the critical detail: Deferred revenue is recognized at *contractual prices*, many of which were locked in 2-3 years ago. If GE's input costs (labor, materials, energy) have inflated faster than contract escalation clauses allow, margins compress *as revenue is recognized*.

GE's gross margin is currently 31.1%—which is healthy. But aerospace programs typically see margin compression over the production lifecycle as learning curves are exhausted and input costs rise. **By the time $19.2B in deferred revenue is fully recognized, margins may have deteriorated to 25-28%, negating much of the revenue growth benefit.**

## **PART 2: THE COMPETITIVE POSITIONING ARGUMENT IS DANGEROUSLY OVERSIMPLIFIED**

### **GEK800 Isn't the Game-Changer the Bull Claims**

The bull presents the GEK800's F143 designation as opening a "$2-3B revenue opportunity over 5 years." Let me be direct: **This is speculative, and the upside is far smaller than implied.**

Here's why:

**1. The Defense Engine Market is Highly Mature**

The military defense engine market is dominated by:
- **Pratt & Whitney** (F119, F135 fighters; military transport)
- **General Electric** (F110, F414 fighters; F117 transport)
- **Rolls-Royce** (fighter and transport engines)

These are entrenched OEM relationships with 30-40 year production timelines (F-16 fighter has been in production since 1974; F/A-18 since 1984). Contracts are *extremely* difficult to win.

**2. GEK800 is a Hypersonic Platform Engine—Tiny TAM**

The bull claims hypersonic testing opens a new revenue stream. But let's examine the actual market:

- Hypersonic weapons testing platforms: ~20-50 test flights globally over 5 years
- GEK800's addressable market: Perhaps 100-200 engines over 5 years, not thousands
- Revenue potential: $100-200M annually at peak (not $400-600M implied by "2-3B over 5 years")

Compare this to LEAP's addressable market (10,000+ commercial engines delivered annually). **GEK800 is rounding error relative to commercial aviation.**

**3. The Bull Ignores Competitive Threats**

Pratt & Whitney has been aggressively pursuing military engine diversification. RTX (parent company) has deeper defense relationships and government connections. GE's entry into hypersonic is a niche play, not a transformational advantage.
