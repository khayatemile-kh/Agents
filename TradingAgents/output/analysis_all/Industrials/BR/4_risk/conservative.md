Notice the pattern? Q2s are abnormally fat—24.6% and 24.1% in consecutive years. This isn't "operational excellence"; this is **seasonal revenue concentration or timing-driven accounting**. The aggressive analyst cites a $226 price target from RBC (25.3% upside) as if it's a legally binding promise from the universe. Let me explain why analyst price targets are neither predictive nor actionable for risk management purposes:

If RBC's model assumed even one rate cut by now, and we're still in a restrictive policy regime, discount rates should be higher, which *lowers* DCF valuation, not raises it.

4. **How sensitive is the model to margin compression?** If RBC's $226 target assumed Q2 FY26's 24.6% operating margin was repeatable, and it turns out to be a one-time event followed by reversion to the 14% range, the model is worth substantially less.

**The aggressive analyst treats the $226 target as a catalyst.** I treat it as a *risk factor*. If the target proves optimistic—which the margin quality concerns suggest it might—we're not looking at mean reversion upside; we're looking at downside to prior support.

## The MACD Convergence: A Textbook Inflection, But Not in the Direction the Aggressive Analyst Wants

This is where I need to push back on the technical framing with equal force.

The aggressive analyst argues that MACD convergence at elevated levels is a "coiled spring" setup. I would argue they're misinterpreting a classic momentum-divergence pattern that precedes *reversals*, not breakouts.

**Let's look at what MACD convergence actually tells us in context:**

The MACD histogram has *narrowed from 1.27 to 0.00 over 8 trading days* while price has *pulled back 2.7%*. In technical analysis, this is called a **bearish divergence**—momentum is weakening while price is still elevated. This is the exact setup that often precedes corrective moves, not continuation moves.

Here's the distinction the aggressive analyst glosses over:

- **Bullish convergence:** MACD and signal line converge *near the zero line* after a deep pullback, then both turn positive together. That's a "coiled spring" setting up a new leg up. We're NOT seeing that. MACD is elevated (+7.22), but it's converging because it's *falling toward* the signal line, not rising away from it.

- **Bearish convergence:** MACD and signal line converge at elevated levels *as momentum decelerates*. The histogram narrows because MACD is rolling over, not because both are about to explode higher. **This is what we're seeing.**

The aggressive analyst also cites RSI at 63.44 as "healthy, not exhausted." But RSI at 63 on a 2.7% pullback isn't particularly healthy—it suggests the pullback lacked conviction, which is actually *more bearish*, not less. If the pullback had conviction (price breaking decisively below moving averages, volume surging), RSI would have dropped more aggressively, potentially to the 40s, and we'd have a true capitulation washout from which to build. Instead, we have weak hands shaking out, which is often followed by more price discovery lower.

**The real technical tell:** Price has tested the 10 EMA intraday but closed above it, and ATR has contracted. The aggressive analyst reads this as "coiled spring." But here's the alternative narrative: **this is a very orderly, low-volatility pullback that's setting up for a break lower, not higher.** When institutional capital rotates out in a controlled manner (declining volume, modest intraday tests of support), it's often the prelude to a breakdown, not a setup.

The safest technical posture right now? **Wait for price to close decisively above $185.45 (the August 24 high) on expanding volume.** Until then, the MACD convergence should be interpreted as a warning, not an opportunity.
