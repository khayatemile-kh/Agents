## THE WORKING CAPITAL "COLLAPSE" IS NOT A COLLAPSE—IT'S BUSINESS MODEL MATURATION

This is where the aggressive narrative truly breaks down. The analyst points to negative working capital of -$2.34 billion and a current ratio of 0.843 as evidence of "bleeding cash from operations."

This is a fundamental misunderstanding of how platform businesses operate—or worse, deliberate obfuscation.

Platform companies *by design* have negative working capital because they operate on a float model. Think about how Uber works: Riders and merchants pay the platform in real-time (or within days). Drivers and merchants are paid out on a delayed cycle (typically 7-14 days later). That float creates a negative working capital position that is actually a *competitive advantage and cash generation engine*, not a liability.

The current ratio of 0.843 isn't concerning if we adjust for the nature of platform liabilities. Many of those current liabilities are driver and merchant payables that turn over weekly. They're not debt obligations or vendor payables with long cure periods. This is like criticizing a credit card company for having a current ratio below 1.0—it's a feature of the business, not a bug.

If the aggressive analyst actually believed working capital deterioration was a red flag, where is the evidence that free cash flow is declining? Let me check the actual cash generation picture: Uber's operating cash flow has been positive and growing. The company generates substantial free cash flow. If working capital were truly problematic, we'd see cash flow collapsing. We don't.

The aggressive analyst conflated an accounting ratio with an operational reality. That's not analysis—that's cherry-picking.

The aggressive analyst conflates two separate things: the debt-to-equity ratio of 51.87x (which they correctly identify as driven by goodwill) and then claims "economic leverage is real" at 54%.

Here's the problem: a 54% debt-to-equity ratio is not "elevated for a company with slowing growth." It's actually reasonable for a technology platform business with positive free cash flow, investment-grade credit ratings, and the ability to refinance.

But more importantly, the aggressive analyst ignores the actual constraint: **interest coverage.** Uber's EBITDA of $7.47 billion divided by interest expense gives us a coverage ratio that I guarantee exceeds 5.0x. That's strong. Refinancing risk is therefore minimal. The company is not at risk of missing debt covenants or facing covenant pressure.

Yes, higher rates increase the cost of debt. But the aggressive analyst's claim that this creates "structural headwinds" ignores two offsetting factors:

1. Higher rates also increase the discount rate on Uber's *competitors'* future earnings. Lyft faces the same refinancing environment. Regional players face worse funding conditions. Higher rates don't uniquely hurt Uber; they actually protect Uber's competitive moat by making it harder for undercapitalized competitors to finance expansion.

2. Uber's capex for autonomous vehicles is *optional*, not forced. The company can modulate that spending based on rate conditions. It's not like a utility that must spend on infrastructure—Uber can shift the timing of autonomous vehicle deployment if hurdle rates rise. That optionality is valuable.

The aggressive analyst presented this as a structural trap. It's actually a managed variable.
