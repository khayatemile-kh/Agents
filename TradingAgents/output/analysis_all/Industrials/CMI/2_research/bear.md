## **PART 1: DECONSTRUCTING THE BULL'S THREE PILLARS**

### **PILLAR 1 CRITIQUE: THE $750B "MEGA-TREND" IS REAL, BUT CMI'S PIECE OF IT IS MICROSCOPIC AND UNPROVEN**

The bull is absolutely right that hyperscalers are spending $750B on AI infrastructure. I don't dispute that. **But here's what they're glossing over:**

#### **The $750B Is Already Spoken For**

Of that $750B annual capex:
- **GPU & chip manufacturing** (NVIDIA, TSMC, Samsung): ~$200-250B
- **Real estate & construction** (site development, cooling systems): ~$150-200B
- **Networking & fiber** (connectivity infrastructure): ~$100-150B
- **Power distribution & grid upgrades** (from utilities, not equipment vendors): ~$100-150B
- **Systems integration & software** (Microsoft, Google in-house): ~$50-100B

Where does CMI's BESS business fit? It's competing in the **remaining slice**: on-site backup power, battery storage, and load management systems. That's maybe **$50-100B globally, across all vendors**—not $750B.

Now, CMI just won "its largest BESS contract ever." The bull presents this as validation. **But what's the contract size?** The data doesn't specify. Is it $50M? $100M? $200M? Without knowing the scale, we can't assess whether this is a meaningful revenue contribution or a rounding error.

Let me put this in perspective:
- **CMI's TTM revenue: $34.71B**
- **Even a $500M BESS contract** = 1.4% of revenue
- **To justify the current valuation assumptions (76% EPS growth)**, CMI would need BESS contracts in the **multi-billion dollar range annually**

The bull says CMI is entering from "a position of strength" with 130 years of track record. **I say that's a liability, not an asset.** CMI is a legacy industrial company trying to compete in the fastest-moving sector in technology. Let me explain why that matters.

#### **CMI Doesn't Actually Have the Defensible Moat the Bull Claims**

The bull argues: *"Data center operators care about systems integration, not just batteries. CMI brings diesel generators, fuel cells, and reliability."*

**This is where the argument breaks down completely.** Here's why:

1. **Specialized battery companies ARE winning in data centers.** Eos Energy, Form Energy, and others are landing major data center contracts. Why? Because their **battery technology is dramatically superior** to retrofit solutions. They're not just vendors—they're bringing innovation that CMI can't match with 50-year-old diesel generator technology.

2. **The "systems integration" moat is disappearing.** Data center operators have massive in-house engineering teams. They don't need CMI to integrate diesel + battery + controls. They're buying best-of-breed components and integrating them themselves. Microsoft, Google, and Meta are *designing their own power systems*. They're not buying from CMI—they're using CMI as a *commodity supplier* if they use CMI at all.

3. **Tesla Energy is already eating CMI's lunch.** Tesla has deployed massive scale in energy storage, better margins, and direct relationships with data center operators. CMI's entry is *late*, not *first-mover.*

4. **The barrier to entry is low.** Battery technology is not proprietary anymore. Any industrial company can partner with a battery supplier (Tesla, LG, CATL) and slap their brand on it. CMI's "reliability" advantage evaporates when the battery component is commoditized.

**Verdict on Pillar 1:** The mega-trend is real, but CMI's addressable portion is **far smaller than implied, unproven, and facing entrenched competitors with better technology.** This isn't a $750B opportunity—it's a **low-single-digit percentage of a $75B market, heavily contested.**

### **PILLAR 2 CRITIQUE: THE VALUATION "OPPORTUNITY" IS BUILT ON FICTION**

Now let's talk about the valuation, because this is where the bull makes a critical logical error.

#### **The Forward EPS Growth Is Speculative, Not "Demonstrated"**

The bull claims: *"CMI just demonstrated execution by landing the largest BESS contract ever. Q2 2026 net income was up 42.6% QoQ."*

**Let me expose the sleight of hand here:**

The 42.6% QoQ net income growth from Q1 to Q2 is NOT evidence of sustainable BESS traction. Here's why:

1. **Q1 2026 was an anomaly.** Look at the data: Q1 net income was $654M, which is depressed. Q2 bounced to $932M. This is **within normal quarterly volatility for an industrial company**, not evidence of a structural inflection.

2. **The BESS contract hasn't contributed material revenue yet.** Contracts announced in August 2026 don't generate Q2 revenue. So the Q2 earnings beat has **nothing to do with the BESS win**. The bull is conflating timing—they're claiming causation where there's correlation.

3. **Where is the forward guidance?** If CMI just landed the largest contract ever and it's transformational, why hasn't management issued explicit BESS revenue guidance? Answer: because they can't, because they don't know the ramp rate, execution timeline, or customer capex schedules.

#### **Forward P/E of 15.97x Assumes 76% EPS Growth—That's Not "Reasonable," That's a Bet**

Let me directly challenge the bull's comparison to peers:

| Company | Forward P/E | Expected EPS Growth | Assessment |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|-----------|
| **CMI** | 15.97x | +76% (implied) | Assumes flawless BESS execution |
| **Eaton (ETN)** | 18-20x | +12-15% | Proven growth; modest premium justified |
| **Caterpillar (CAT)** | 16-17x | +8-12% | Cyclical; valuation appropriate |

**The bull says CMI is "cheaper than peers on a growth-adjusted basis."** Here's the problem: **CMI's growth is *assumed*, while Eaton's and CAT's growth is *demonstrated with order books.***

Eaton's data center segment revenue is *already running* at elevated levels (+65% YoY). That's visible in their quarterly reports. CAT has *visible order backlogs* growing. CMI has an **announced contract** with no revenue contribution yet, no visibility into future contracts, and no management guidance on BESS profitability margins.

**This is not a valuation inefficiency—it's a valuation trap.** When a company's forward valuation rests entirely on a new product line that hasn't achieved revenue scale, and when comparable companies with *proven* growth are only trading at a modest premium, it's usually because the market is correctly pricing execution risk.

#### **The "Intrinsic Value Double-Digits Above Market" Claim Is Meaningless Without Visibility Into Assumptions**

The bull cites Simply Wall St. and Trefis flagging CMI as "cheap." **But these valuations are only as good as the assumptions feeding them.**

If the DCF model assumes:
- BESS TAM of $50B globally by 2030
- CMI capturing 15-20% market share
- 30%+ EBITDA margins on BESS (speculation)
- Sustained 10%+ organic growth thereafter

...then yes, the model says it's undervalued. **But if any of these assumptions are optimistic by 30-40% (which is normal in inflection stories), the "intrinsic value" collapses by half or more.**

**Verdict on Pillar 2:** The forward valuation isn't compelling—it's *dangerous.* It's priced for perfection on a new product line with zero execution track record, facing entrenched competitors, and with management providing zero visibility.
