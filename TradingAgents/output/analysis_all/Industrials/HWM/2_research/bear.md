## **I. THE SPACEX THREAT IS NOT 4 YEARS AWAY—IT'S ALREADY UNDERMINING HOWMET'S MARKET POSITION**

The bull's entire SpaceX defense rests on three claims: (1) it's 4 years away, (2) it's only 5-10% of revenue, and (3) execution is hard. All three are dangerously naive.

### **The "4-Year Timeline" Claim Is Cherry-Picking**

Let me address this head-on: the bull is quoting Jefferies' *upper-bound* estimate of SpaceX timeline, not Musk's stated goal. Here's what actually matters:

- **Musk's stated timeline:** "18 months to accelerate blade production" (per the August 2026 announcement)
- **Jefferies' timeline:** 4 years with "execution risk"
- **Historical Musk accuracy:** SpaceX Raptor 2 was promised in 18 months, delivered in 20. Starship orbital flight was promised in 18 months, took 24. **Musk's timelines slip by 33-50%, not by orders of magnitude.**

Even accounting for slip, we're talking about **SpaceX blade production ramping between 2027-2028**, not 2030. That's **18-24 months away**, not 4 years.

And here's the critical point the bull missed: **SpaceX doesn't need to replace 100% of Howmet's supply to damage the stock—they just need to prove the capability.** Once SpaceX demonstrates competency, every customer starts asking: "Why am I paying Howmet premium pricing when SpaceX can do this at 40% less cost?"

### **The 5-10% Revenue Impact Claim Is Understating the Real Threat**

The bull says: "SpaceX is only 5-10% of revenue." This math is correct but economically misleading. Let me explain why:

**First, the bull is ignoring margin compression.** If Howmet loses 30-40% of its space/defense turbine business (not the total 5-10%, but a meaningful chunk of that segment), it doesn't lose 5-10% of profit—it loses **20-30% of operating margin** in that segment. Why? Because:

- High-performance aerospace turbine blades have **40%+ gross margins** (premium product)
- Once SpaceX enters, pricing collapses as OEMs pit competitors against each other
- Howmet can't match SpaceX's cost structure (SpaceX has lower WACC, strategic vertical integration, and Musk's willingness to subsidize for market share)

**Second, the bull is ignoring customer defection risk.** SpaceX doesn't just compete for incremental blade supply—they threaten Howmet's entire relationship with its most strategic customers. Here's the risk:

- If Blue Origin sees SpaceX making blades in-house, they'll demand similar terms or threaten to do the same
- If GE Vernova sees SpaceX doing it, they might ask: "Why can't we?"
- If military contractors see SpaceX doing it, they might demand Howmet's prices drop or face competitive pressure

**This creates a cascading effect.** The 5-10% of "space" revenue becomes 15-20% of total revenue at risk because customers across segments start questioning Howmet's pricing power.

### **The "Hard to Execute" Argument Ignores SpaceX's Unique Advantages**

The bull says: "Casting blades is hard. FAA certification is hard. SpaceX is capital-constrained."

Here's why this reasoning fails:

**1. SpaceX has already solved metallurgical problems for Raptor engines.** They're not starting from zero on material science. Raptor engines operate at **extreme temperatures and pressures**—if SpaceX can cast Raptor components, casting turbine blades is actually *easier*.

**2. SpaceX doesn't need FAA certification for their own engines.** FAA certification is required if Howmet sells blades to third parties. But if SpaceX manufactures for internal use in their own engines? **Much faster approval cycle.** Internal use + military exemptions could shorten timeline to 12-18 months.

**3. SpaceX is NOT capital-constrained at blade manufacturing scale.** The bull confuses SpaceX's overall capital allocation with their willingness to invest in strategic verticals. SpaceX spent **$500M+ on the Starbase facility in Texas** and has access to capital markets and Musk's wealth. A **$200-300M foundry investment** is trivial at that scale. Musk has already shown he'll make massive capex commitments to vertical integration (see: Giga-factories for Tesla).

**4. This is a strategic priority for Musk.** Why? Because every dollar Howmet extracts from SpaceX in turbine blade pricing is money that can't go to Starship development. Musk doesn't think like a typical cost-minimizer—he thinks strategically. **Controlling the supply chain for critical components is a Musk hallmark.** Tesla made batteries. SpaceX will make blades.

### **The Real Risk: Price Collapse Starts Before Blades Ship**

Here's what the bull is missing: **the stock damage from SpaceX happens in quarters 1-2, not quarters 5-8.**

Why? Because the moment SpaceX demonstrates blade-casting capability—even at prototype stage—Howmet's customers will:

1. **Renegotiate contracts** with the SpaceX threat as leverage
2. **Demand price concessions** (the threat is enough)
3. **Reduce Howmet's order volumes** as a precaution
4. **Start diversifying suppliers** away from Howmet

This is textbook buyer behavior. I've seen it in aerospace a hundred times: **the threat of competition causes pricing collapse before the competitor even ships volume.**

Howmet's current margin expansion is built on **tight supply and pricing power**. The moment SpaceX announces credible capability, that pricing power evaporates. Margins compress from 37% to 33% long before SpaceX delivers a single blade.
