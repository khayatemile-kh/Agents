## **I. THE SPACEX "THREAT" IS ACTUALLY A MARKET VALIDATION**

This is where I think the market got it completely backwards.

### **The Real Story: Capacity Shortage, Not Displacement**

When Elon Musk announces SpaceX will manufacture gas turbine blades in-house, what is he actually saying? He's saying: **"We can't get turbine blades fast enough from current suppliers."**

That's not a threat to Howmet. **That's a demand signal.**

Look at what Jefferies said—and this is critical: SpaceX's internal turbine manufacturing is **4 years away**, despite Musk's claims of acceleration. Even with Elon's track record of aggressive timelines, we're talking about a 2030 event at the earliest. Why? Because:

- **Casting turbine blades is hard.** Howmet didn't become the market leader by accident. There are material science challenges, quality control requirements, and FAA certification hurdles that don't disappear just because you're SpaceX.
- **Capital intensity is brutal.** Building a foundry and casting operation requires massive capex, process validation, and multi-year ramp. Musk's time estimates have a consistent pattern of optimism bias.
- **SpaceX is capital-constrained on Starship.** With Starlink capex needs, Starship development, and other projects, would SpaceX really divert resources to blade casting when they could negotiate better terms with Howmet instead?

### **What This Really Means for Howmet's Business**

SpaceX manufacturing blades in-house affects maybe 12-15% of Howmet's addressable market—the high-performance aerospace segment. Let's put this in context:

**Howmet's revenue sources include:**
- Commercial aviation turbines (GE, Rolls-Royce, CFM) — **60%+ of business**
- Military/defense applications — **20%+**
- Industrial power generation (Vernova, Siemens, GE) — **10%+**
- Space/emerging (SpaceX, Blue Origin, others) — **5-10%**

Even if SpaceX captures 100% of their own blade needs by 2030, that's maybe **$200-300M annual impact** on a company running $9.1B in revenues. That's a 2-3% headwind *in 2030+*, not an existential threat.

**Meanwhile, the market that's driving Howmet's current growth remains completely intact:**
- **GE Vernova** needs turbine blades for renewable power plants (structural tailwind for decades)
- **Rolls-Royce and CFM** are ramping commercial aviation supply chains (post-COVID recovery still in early innings)
- **Defense spending** is elevated and bipartisan
- **Military modernization** across NATO and allies continues

### **The Oligopoly Moat Remains Intact**

Here's something the market glossed over: turbine blade manufacturing is **not a commodity business**. There are only a handful of companies in the world that can make high-performance blades at scale:

1. **Howmet** (dominant)
2. **Precision Castparts** (Berkshire subsidiary)
3. **Consolidated Precision Products**
4. Doncasters
5. A few regional players

This is a **moat**, not a commodity pool. Howmet doesn't compete on price alone—it competes on:
- **Material science expertise** (directional solidification, single-crystal casting)
- **Supply chain reliability** (just-in-time delivery to OEMs)
- **Quality/certification** (decades of FAA/EASA relationships)
- **Capacity availability** (which is currently tight!)

SpaceX adding a foundry doesn't erase these advantages. If anything, it *validates* that capacity is so tight that a $200B+ company decided it was worth the capex to build in-house. That's a compliment to the market structure, not a death knell.
