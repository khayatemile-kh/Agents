## **2. The Balance Sheet "Loaded Gun" Narrative Ignores How Airlines Actually Work**

This is where I need to be surgical because the Aggressive Analyst is using language designed to make a normal airline balance sheet sound like a doomsday machine.

Yes, DAL has a current ratio of 0.42x. The Aggressive Analyst says this "means DAL has only $0.42 in current assets for every $1.00 in current liabilities" and then adds that "the airline industry model does rely on advance ticket sales, but let's not conflate operational efficiency with financial fragility."

Okay, let me actually explain what's happening on the balance sheet instead of using scary language.

The low current ratio exists *because of the airline business model itself*. Airlines collect cash from customers *today* for flights happening *tomorrow* (or months later). That's why current liabilities are bloated—they include air traffic liability (the obligation to fly passengers who've already paid). Meanwhile, current assets are held down because the company is constantly spending cash operationally.

This is not fragility. This is *normal*. And every major airline operates with a current ratio below 1.0x. American Airlines, United, Southwest—they all run sub-1.0x current ratios. If current ratio below 1.0x = financial fragility, then the entire airline industry is fragile. But it's not—it's just how the business model works.

Now, working capital of negative $19.37B. The Aggressive Analyst treats this like a structural weakness. But here's what that actually represents: it's the net present value of *future revenue* that's already been collected. When a customer buys a ticket 6 months in advance, that $500 shows up as a liability *today* and becomes revenue *tomorrow*. That's not a fragility indicator—it's the nature of the business.

What actually matters for covenant compliance isn't working capital or current ratio—it's **EBITDA** and **debt service coverage**. Let me look at the data:

- TTM EBITDA: $7.51 billion
- Total Debt: $19.98 billion
- Debt/EBITDA: 2.66x

That's *reasonable* for an airline. That's not excessive. It's in line with industry norms. Southwest has similar multiples. United has higher multiples. AAL has *worse* multiples (which is why they're struggling).

The Aggressive Analyst is talking about "covenant stress" and "liquidity stress." But they're not showing me the actual covenant language. They're not showing me the debt maturity schedule. They're not showing me quarterly cash burn rates. They're just using scary language and hoping I don't ask for specifics.

Here's the reality: DAL generated $7.51B in EBITDA last twelve months. With $19.98B in debt at average rates around 4-5%, that's roughly $800-1000M in annual interest expense. The company is *easily* covering that with EBITDA. They're generating positive free cash flow. Net debt actually *declined* from $11.73B to $9.29B year-over-year—meaning they're *paying down* debt, not accumulating it.

Where is the covenant stress? I don't see it. The Aggressive Analyst is conjuring a scenario where "Q3 or Q4 operates anything like Q1 (even a mild miss)" triggers covenant discussions.
