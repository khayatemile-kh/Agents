## **THE EARNINGS DECELERATION PROBLEM IS REAL, BUT NOT DISQUALIFYING**

Let me address the elephant in the room. Conservative, you ask a very fair question: if WEC deployed $4.40 billion in capex in 2025 at promised 9-10% returns, where's the earnings growth? The answer matters, and I think you're right to push on this.

But here's what I think you're missing: capex cycles don't work on annual basis. When a utility deploys infrastructure capex, it doesn't all hit the rate base on day one. There's a construction cycle, a regulatory approval cycle, and then a ramp-up cycle where revenues gradually increase as infrastructure becomes operational. WEC's 2025 capex of $4.40 billion is likely still in construction phase. The earnings accretion from that capex should flow through 2026-2027. 

So the 0.4% YoY TTM metric reflects mostly older capex that's already deployed. The Q2 2026 EPS of $0.91, up 19.5% year-over-year, might actually be the leading indicator here—it shows that when 2025 capex starts flowing through operationally, earnings are responding. The TTM metric is being depressed by stale Q4 2025 and earlier data.

**That said**, this is an assumption. If 2026 TTM earnings growth doesn't accelerate to 3-5% by Q4 2026, then the conservative thesis gains credibility. The capex isn't working. That's the data point to watch—not the current TTM, but the trajectory over the next two quarters.

Aggressive, your faith that "every dollar gets embedded in rate base" is theoretically sound, but it's also underestimating regulatory risk. Conservative is right that regulators aren't guaranteed to rubber-stamp returns. They scrutinize leverage, execution efficiency, and cost overruns. WEC's net debt to capitalization at 67.4% is at the upper range. If regulators start pushing back on returns because they see over-leverage, then your 9-10% thesis gets compressed to 8% or 7.5%. That's material to the earnings outlook.

## **THE DEBT STORY IS CONCERNING BUT NOT CATASTROPHIC**

Here's where I side more with the Conservative thesis, but with an important caveat.

Yes, debt increased 33% from 2022 to Q2 2026. That's real. And yes, the company is refinancing existing debt in a higher rate environment. But let's put this in context: **WEC's operating cash flow (OCF) is approximately $2.8-3.0 billion annually.** With $22.98 billion of debt, that's a gross debt-to-OCF ratio of about 7.7-8x. For a utility with regulated revenues, that's elevated but not crisis-level. For comparison, some leveraged utilities run 9-10x.

The more important metric is this: **can WEC service its debt and the dividend while deploying capex?** Let's look at the cash flow waterfall. WEC generates roughly $3 billion in OCF. Interest expense on $22.98 billion of debt at current rates (probably averaging 4-4.5%) is about $900-1,000 million annually. The dividend, with a 3.6% yield on a $34.45 billion market cap, is about $1.24 billion annually. That leaves roughly $800 million for capex from operations. But WEC is deploying $4.40 billion annually.

So where's the gap funding coming from? It's coming from new debt issuance. WEC is using debt capital markets to fund the gap between OCF plus dividend and capex. That's actually normal for utilities with growth capex—they're not expected to self-fund entirely from operations. But it does mean the debt will grow until capex moderates or earnings grow.

**Here's the critical question**: will regulatory returns on the deployed capex grow earnings fast enough to eventually generate sufficient OCF to self-fund? Or will this become a perpetual debt-issuance story where leverage keeps ratcheting up?

I think the answer is "it depends on execution." If WEC deploys that $37.5 billion capex efficiently and regulators approve normalized returns (even if at 8-8.5% rather than 9-10%), then OCF should grow to $3.5-4.0 billion range by 2028-2029, which would be sufficient. But if capex execution stumbles or regulators push back on returns due to leverage concerns, then you have a tightening cash flow story, and the dividend becomes vulnerable.

That's not a catastrophe scenario, but it's a real risk that justifies at least some caution.
