## THE BEAR'S CORE THESIS: THREE STRUCTURAL FAILURES

### **FAILURE 1: THE WILDFIRE LIABILITY RISK IS NOT "TEMPORARY"—IT'S STRUCTURAL AND UNQUANTIFIABLE**

The bull keeps saying: *"Utilities are too important to fail. Regulators will protect them."*

**This is wishful thinking. Here's why:**

#### **A. The Regulatory Precedent the Bull Cites Actually Proves the Bear Case**

The bull mentions PG&E's 2020 bankruptcy as evidence utilities survive. **Let me ask: Is that really the precedent we want?**

- **PG&E filed for bankruptcy in 2020** because of wildfire liability exposure
- **It emerged** in 2020, yes—but at what cost?
- **$13.5 billion in total liability claims** were filed; shareholders were diluted; bondholders took haircuts
- **PG&E shareholders lost 99% of their value** during the bankruptcy process

**The bull's argument is: "See? Utilities survive!" My argument is: "See? Shareholders get annihilated!"**

This is the critical point the bull glosses over. Yes, SCE and California's grid will survive. But **EIX shareholders will not.**

#### **B. The Failed Liability Bill Wasn't a "Negotiating Detail"—It Was a Fundamental Policy Rejection**

The bull is being naive about California legislative dynamics:

1. **Who killed the bill?** Consumer advocates, environmental groups, and progressive legislators rejected it. These constituencies have *political power* in California.

2. **Why did they kill it?** The bill would have capped utility liability and shifted wildfire costs onto *ratepayers*—effectively socializing risk while privatizing profit for utilities.

3. **What does this signal?** California's political culture has fundamentally shifted against utilities. After decades of watching wildfire victims go uncompensated while utilities collected dividends, the public is *done.*

4. **Will they "compromise"?** Maybe eventually, but the compromise will come *at the expense of utilities and shareholders*, not in their favor. Any revised bill will:
   - Impose *higher* liability thresholds on utilities
   - Require *larger* reserve contributions from utilities
   - Impose *stricter* operational and vegetation management standards with financial penalties

**The bull assumes a 50-50 negotiation. In reality, utilities are negotiating from weakness.**

#### **C. "Rate Base Recovery" Is Not Unlimited—And It Assumes Regulatory Goodwill**

The bull claims: *"Regulators will authorize rate increases to cover wildfire costs."*

**Let me expose this fantasy:**

1. **Regulators have political constraints.** California's regulatory bodies face public pressure. If EIX seeks massive rate hikes to recover wildfire liabilities, expect:
   - Public commission hearings where angry Californians testify about rising electricity costs
   - Political pressure from state legislators
   - Potential rate cap legislation
   - Competition from rooftop solar and battery storage providers offering alternatives

2. **The "reasonable cost" doctrine has limits.** If EIX faces $5B in wildfire claims (a conservative estimate), can it pass that *entirely* to ratepayers? In theory, maybe 70-80%. But the other 20-30%? **That comes out of shareholder equity.**

3. **Regulator cooperation is not guaranteed.** Look at the regulatory environment:
   - California Public Utilities Commission is increasingly hostile to utilities
   - Recent "decoupling" mechanisms have already limited utility upside
   - CPUC has shown willingness to reject utility spending proposals

4. **Precedent is mixed.** The bull cites Hurricane Sandy cost recovery. But:
   - Hurricane damage is *weather-related*, not *operational negligence*-related
   - Wildfire claims involve questions of utility responsibility, vegetation management, equipment maintenance
   - Courts and juries are far more sympathetic to natural disaster relief than to utilities' operational failures

**My point: The bull is assuming 100% regulatory pass-through. I'm pricing in 60-70%, which means EIX absorbs 30-40% of wildfire liability. At scale, that's catastrophic.**

#### **D. Insurance Is NOT Going to Solve This**

The bull says: *"AIG and Lloyd's of London will underwrite this risk."*

**This is backwards-looking analysis.** Here's the insurance market reality in 2026:

1. **Wildfire risk premiums have exploded.** Post-2020 PG&E crisis, insurers have dramatically raised rates for utility liability coverage. The cost of insuring against catastrophic wildfire is now *prohibitively expensive.*

2. **Capacity is limited.** Specialty carriers like AIG face their *own* exposure limits. Lloyd's of London syndicates are already pulling back from California utility liability. There isn't unlimited capacity to insure this risk—the market is rejecting it.

3. **Exclusions are expanding.** New insurance policies are explicitly excluding certain wildfire scenarios or imposing conditions (equipment upgrades, vegetation management) that are costly to implement.

4. **Cost economics break down.** If EIX needs $500M-$1B/year in wildfire liability insurance, that's 10-15% of annual earnings *gone* just to premiums. At that point, insurance isn't a hedge—it's a profit killer.

**The bull is assuming insurance markets will solve this. They won't. Instead, insurance will become a growing expense item that compresses margins.**
