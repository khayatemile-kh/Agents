## The Dividend Cut Isn't Inevitable—It's Possible But Not Certain

You point to the 1.0x interest coverage ratio and say it's "not a stable equilibrium." Correct. But you then leap to "dividend cuts are coming." That's a probability statement disguised as certainty, and the probabilities are actually more favorable than you're suggesting.

Here's the reality: EIX just declared preferred stock dividends on schedule. The board didn't waffle. They didn't defer. They declared. Now, you correctly note this is "legacy commitment" with lower flexibility than common dividends, and you're right about that distinction. But consider what a dividend cut would signal: it would be an admission by management that they can't service obligations through normal operations. That's a bankruptcy signal.

The cash flow picture, though strained, isn't there yet. Q2 2026 operating cash flow was $1.27 billion. If we annualize that conservatively at $5 billion—acknowledging Q2 might be seasonally weak—that still covers $5.4 billion in current debt obligations if you get no help from the balance sheet. It's tight. Dangerously tight. But it's not a guarantee of a cut. It's a pressure point.

What matters more is this: if EIX cuts its common dividend, the stock probably falls another 30-40% from here. Conservative analyst, you understand that cascade. But here's what you're underweighting: that same outcome means the yield rises to 8-9% on the resulting lower price. At that point, the company becomes a zombie value trap that eventually gets acquired or restructured. That's a known outcome path. We can price that in.

What we *can't* price in is whether it happens in the next 60 days or 18 months. And that timing distribution matters enormously for a "hold" decision.

## Regulatory Resolution Is Actually More Likely Than You're Framing

Conservative analyst, you're right that Governor Newsom's liability shield proposal failed. You're right that lawmakers rejected protective measures. But then you conclude that "the probability of favorable legislation in Q3-Q4 is 20-25%." I think that's actually too pessimistic, and here's why.

When major legislation fails, what typically happens? Nothing for two to four weeks, then reassessment. California's legislature doesn't just move on from utility liability frameworks—they're a recurring issue because the problem doesn't go away. Wildfires will continue. Utilities will continue to face liability exposure. The political mathematics might look impossible today, but they shift.

Here's what the news flow actually shows: the issue was live enough that Newsom *proposed* a solution. That means it's politically active. When something is politically active in California, it tends to come back. Not necessarily with the same terms, but in some form.

Now, you're right to point out that the next iteration could be *more* restrictive than Newsom's proposal. That's genuine tail risk. But let's quantify that properly. The base case scenarios are:

1. Legislation that's moderately protective to utilities (40% probability, 10-15% upside)
2. Legislation that's neutral-to-slightly-restrictive but provides clarity (35% probability, 0-5% upside)
3. No legislative resolution this year, continued uncertainty (15% probability, -20% downside)
4. Highly restrictive legislation that caps recovery (10% probability, -30% downside)

Under this distribution, the expected value of regulatory resolution is actually *slightly positive*, not negative. That changes the risk-reward materially.

## The Balance Sheet Deterioration Is Real, But It's Not Unique to EIX

Conservative analyst, you point to the doubling of working capital deficit as evidence of operational stress. You're right to flag it. But context matters. EIX is a utility. Utilities are capital-intensive businesses with seasonal working capital patterns. The jump from Q3 2025 to Q2 2026 could partly reflect the timing of equipment purchases, inventory buildup ahead of fire season, or litigation-related cash outlays that aren't recurring.

The current ratio of 0.66 looks alarming, but utilities historically operate with sub-1.0 current ratios because they have predictable cash flows and committed customer bases. This isn't a manufacturing company that might face demand destruction. It's a regulated monopoly. The current ratio deterioration is concerning because it's combined with *earnings* deterioration, not because it's inherently destabilizing on its own.

That said, your underlying point stands: the balance sheet is under pressure. The company is levering up into a downturn. That's real. But it's also recoverable if earnings stabilize in the next two quarters. And aggressive analyst, you're betting on that stabilization. Conservative analyst, you're betting it doesn't happen. I think the truth is murkier: earnings will probably stabilize somewhere between where they are now and where they were pre-crisis.

Aggressive analyst, you're claiming that when rates ease, utilities become "vacuum cleaners for capital." That's true in a normal interest rate environment. But conservative analyst is right that we're not in a normal environment. The Fed has signaled no rate cuts until December at earliest, and only a 10% probability of cuts even then.

That means the rate volatility risk is more symmetric than either of you is treating it. There's a floor under Treasury yields for this cycle. Cuts won't come until 2027 at the earliest. That's a headwind that's already in the price.
