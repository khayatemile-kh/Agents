That's not a temporary panic; that's evidence of a structural problem that won't be solved quickly.

The market tested the bottom, found no fundamental support, and retreated.

The aggressive analyst wants us to believe that 24.6 million shares trading hands represents forced liquidation by weak hands. But I'd argue it represents *discovery*—the market discovering that the forward guidance is deteriorating by 28%, that interest coverage is collapsing to 1.0x, and that a regulated utility suddenly faces unquantified tail risk. That's not panic selling. That's pricing adjustment.

Here's where the aggressive analyst's argument becomes dangerous. They say "utilities rarely cut dividends unless they're actually bankrupt." That's historically true, but it's also a statement made by investors who watched Enron, who watched Lehman Brothers, who watched countless utilities restructure their dividends when leverage ratios became unsustainable.

The interest coverage ratio at 1.0x is not a stable equilibrium. That means EIX is generating just enough EBIT to cover interest expense—with zero margin for error. Now add a 28% forward EPS decline. The board "just declared dividends," as the aggressive analyst notes. But let's look at what that actually means in context. The declaration was of preferred stock dividends—a legacy commitment that has lower flexibility than common dividends. And it came *despite* the stock having already collapsed 27%. That's not confidence; that's inertia. It's the board following its quarterly routine while the fundamental platform beneath the stock is cracking.

If EIX cuts its common dividend—and I'm increasingly confident they will need to—that triggers a cascade of selling from the income-focused investors who make up a substantial portion of utility shareholder bases. ## The "Regulated Utilities Always Survive" Narrative Is Incomplete

The aggressive analyst invokes the 2004 California electricity crisis as proof utilities get restructured but survive. They're right that the sector survived. But they're wrong about the implication. When utilities go through liability crises, *shareholders* don't always survive intact. Look at what happened to PG&E's equity holders after the 2017-2018 wildfire crisis evolved. The company survived as an entity. Shareholders got diluted into oblivion through restructuring, massive capital raises, and eventual bankruptcy protection.

The aggressive analyst also assumes that if California passes a liability framework, it will be favorable to utilities and allow "cost recovery through rate increases." That's assumption, not analysis. California's legislature just *rejected* Governor Newsom's protective legislation. Why? Because there's political will against utilities absorbing taxpayer protection. If California does pass a framework, it could easily *increase* utility responsibility while capping rate recovery. We don't know which direction the regulation goes. That's the precise definition of tail risk.

And here's the thing about regulated utilities that the aggressive analyst missed: they don't get repriced based on technical mean reversion. They get repriced based on regulatory decisions and credit metrics. If Edison's debt-to-equity ratio hits 240% and stays there while forward earnings decline, rating agencies *will* downgrade the debt. That increases borrowing costs, which compresses margins further—a death spiral for a levered utility.
