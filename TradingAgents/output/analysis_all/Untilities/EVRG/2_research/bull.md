## **THE CORE BULL THESIS**

### **1. REGULATED UTILITIES ARE NOT NORMAL BUSINESSES — And That's The Point**

Let me address the elephant in the room immediately: **Yes, EVRG has a 160% debt-to-equity ratio and negative free cash flow.** Any bear worth their salt will lead with this. But here's what they'll get wrong:

**In a regulated utility, debt isn't a risk factor—it's a *feature*, not a bug.**

Here's why:

- **Regulated Rate of Return:** EVRG operates under state regulatory frameworks in Kansas and Oklahoma that *guarantee* a regulated return on its asset base. This isn't competitive capitalism—it's a sanctioned monopoly with built-in profitability guardrails.
- **Pass-Through Economics:** When EVRG takes on debt to finance infrastructure (which is mandated by grid modernization and reliability standards), it can pass the **cost of that debt through to ratepayers** via rate recovery mechanisms. This is baked into the business model.
- **Debt Service as Revenue:** Unlike a commercial business where debt financing comes from earnings, EVRG's debt service is *pre-approved* by regulators and embedded in customer rates. The debt isn't a threat—it's monetized.

**The Comparison That Matters:**
Compare EVRG's leverage to that of a leveraged tech company or a cyclical industrial firm. In those cases, 160% D/E would be disastrous. For EVRG? It's literally how the utility business works. Duke Energy (DUK), Dominion Energy (D), and other peers operate with similarly high leverage precisely because regulators *expect* this capital structure.

The bear will point to **-$1.44 billion in free cash flow** as evidence EVRG is financially distressed. This is a fundamental category error.

**Here's the reality:**

Let's break down the math:

- **Operating Cash Flow (TTM):** ~$2.1-2.3 billion (strong, healthy)
- **Capital Expenditures:** ~$3.5-3.7 billion annually
- **Result:** Negative FCF of -$1.44 billion

**This isn't a crisis. This is the regulatory contract working as intended.**

The $3.5-3.7 billion in CapEx is explicitly mandated or incentivized by state regulators for:
- Grid hardening (resilience against extreme weather—the 22% Midwest grid emergency risk is *exactly* what drives these mandates)
- Renewable integration infrastructure (mandated by state policies)
- Smart grid modernization (aging infrastructure replacement)

Here's the critical point: **These CapEx investments flow into the company's regulated asset base (RAB).** Once capitalized and depreciated, they become basis for future rate increases. The negative FCF is actually *deferred revenue*—it's money EVRG will recover through regulated rates over 20-40 year asset lives.

**The Cash Funding Model:**
- Operating CF covers ~60-65% of CapEx
- Debt issuance covers ~30-35% of CapEx
- Dividend reductions or equity issuance covers remainder (if needed)

This is *normal and expected* for utilities in growth/modernization phases. It's not bankruptcy—it's infrastructure funding.

**Answer: Because regulators won't allow the dividend to be cut.**

Here's the regulation-backed logic:

1. **Investment-Grade Rating Pressure:** EVRG (and all utilities) must maintain investment-grade credit ratings to access capital markets at reasonable rates. S&P, Moody's, and Fitch have *explicit* expectations that regulated utilities maintain stable, predictable dividends.

2. **Regulatory Precedent:** State regulators (Kansas Corporation Commission, Oklahoma Corporation Commission) have **never** allowed a major incumbent utility to slash its dividend. Doing so signals financial distress and regulatory failure. Regulators actively work to *prevent* dividend cuts because they protect the utility's credit rating and cost of capital.

3. **Rate Recovery Mechanism:** EVRG's regulated rates are *explicitly set* to cover operating expenses, maintenance CapEx, debt service, *and* a reasonable return on equity that supports the dividend. As long as CapEx is regulatory-approved and rates are adjusted accordingly, the dividend is mathematically protected.

4. **Dividend Coverage:** Operating CF of $2.1B covers the annual dividend ($650-700M annually) **3x over**. The FCF goes negative only because of discretionary infrastructure investment, not because operating profits are insufficient.
