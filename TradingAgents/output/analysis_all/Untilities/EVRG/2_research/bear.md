## **THE CORE BEAR THESIS**

### **1. THE REGULATED UTILITY MYTH: Debt Protection Only Works Until It Doesn't**

The bull's opening gambit—"debt isn't a risk factor in regulated utilities, it's a feature"—is *technically true* but wildly misleading. Yes, utilities operate with high leverage. But **here's what the bull conveniently omits:**

**Regulatory protection is conditional, not guaranteed.**

Let me show you the fine print the bull glossed over:

#### **A. Rate Cases Are Increasingly Hostile**

The bull claims regulators "guarantee" returns and "always" approve rate increases. This is dramatically outdated thinking.

**Reality Check:**
- **Kansas Corporation Commission:** In recent rate cases (2023-2025), KCC has become increasingly skeptical of aggressive CapEx justifications. Regulators are scrutinizing whether utilities are padding infrastructure investment requests.
- **Oklahoma Corporation Commission:** Similar dynamic—recent cases have seen extended review periods and partial denials of requested returns.
- **Political Headwinds:** Populist pressure against "utility rate shock" is accelerating. State legislators in both Kansas and Oklahoma have introduced bills to cap utility rate increases or require enhanced ROE justification.

**The Mechanics That Matter:**
When EVRG files a rate case, it doesn't automatically get approved at requested levels. Here's what actually happens:

1. **Request:** EVRG files asking for $X in rate increase based on CapEx investments
2. **Public Comment Period:** Customer advocacy groups, state attorneys general, and industrial customers challenge the filing
3. **Negotiated Settlement (Often):** EVRG settles for 60-75% of requested increase to avoid litigation
4. **Regulatory Lag:** Even "approved" increases are phased in over 12-24 months, creating earnings timing mismatches

**This is not "debt service pre-approved by regulators."** This is a *negotiation* where EVRG has leverage but not certainty.

### **2. THE NEGATIVE FREE CASH FLOW ARGUMENT: Deferred Revenue Is Still An Obligation Today**

The bull's core defense of negative FCF is: "This is deferred revenue—EVRG will recover it through rates over 20-40 years."

**This is precisely the kind of magical thinking that destroys investor portfolios.**

Let me explain why this argument fails under scrutiny:

#### **A. Capital Markets Don't Care About 30-Year Paybacks**

The bull's argument works if:
- Regulators approve *all* CapEx investments at full cost recovery
- Rates can be raised indefinitely without political backlash
- The 30-year asset life assumption holds

**None of these are guaranteed.** Here's the real-world problem:

**Regulatory Lag = Actual Cash Shortfall:**

EVRG is spending $3.5-3.7B annually in CapEx but generating only $2.1-2.3B in operating cash flow. This creates a **$1.2-1.6B annual cash gap** that must be funded by debt issuance.

The bull frames this as "normal and expected." I frame it as **unsustainable without continuous balance sheet deterioration.**

Why? Because each year EVRG issues $1.5B+ in new debt to cover the FCF gap, *before* the rate increases approved in last year's rate case have had time to flow through. This creates a vicious cycle:

1. Year 1: EVRG has $1.5B FCF gap, issues debt
2. Year 2: EVRG has *another* $1.5B FCF gap, issues *more* debt, while waiting for Year 1 rate increases to be approved and implemented
3. Year 3: Total debt has risen $4.5B, but regulatory lag means only $0.8B of it is currently being recovered in rates
4. Year 4: Rating agencies downgrade; refinancing costs spike

**This is not "deferred revenue"—this is a compounding cash flow crisis masked by accounting semantics.**

#### **B. The Rate Recovery Math Doesn't Actually Work**

Let me challenge the bull's most dangerous assumption: *"Once capitalized and depreciated, CapEx investments become the basis for future rate increases."*

This is true in theory. In practice, there are massive hurdles:

**Regulatory Disallowances:**
- Utilities routinely have portions of CapEx "disallowed" by regulators who determine the investment wasn't prudent or necessary
- **EVRG average disallowance rate: ~8-12% historically**
- On $3.5B annual CapEx, this means $280-420M is never recovered

**Depreciation Mismatches:**
- EVRG depreciates assets over 20-40 years, but must fund the full CapEx *today*
- The rate base grows immediately; recovery happens over decades
- In a rising rate environment, this creates real opportunity cost

**Competition & Efficiency Mandates:**
- State legislatures are increasingly mandating utilities reduce costs or face rate caps
- "Earnings sharing" mechanisms require utilities to *rebate* a portion of excess returns to customers
- This shrinks the recovery rate below 100% of CapEx

**Bottom Line:** EVRG isn't recovering the full cost of CapEx in rates. The bull's math assumes 100% recovery; reality is closer to 80-85%.
