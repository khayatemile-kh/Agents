## **The Florida Regulatory Gamble Is NOT a Feature—It's a Genuine Tail Risk We're Mispricing**

The Aggressive Analyst frames the Florida rejection as procedural friction—"just an iteration before DUK files a compliant proposal." I fundamentally reject this characterization because it conflates *likelihood* with *certainty*, and it dismisses the specific language from the state regulator.

Let's examine what actually happened. Walt Trierweiler, Florida's Public Counsel, didn't say "revise and resubmit." He said DUK's proposal "doesn't attempt to comply with the most basic provisions" of SB 484. That's not a drafting error. That's regulatory critique suggesting fundamental misalignment between what DUK proposed and what Florida policy intends.

Here's what the Aggressive Analyst is glossing over: **SB 484 exists in Florida's political context.** Yes, Florida wants data centers—but Florida also has a history of protecting residential and small-business ratepayers from disproportionate cost-shifting. The law's "basic provisions" likely include protections ensuring that large-load customer tariffs don't become subsidy mechanisms disguised as efficiency gains.

DUK's initial proposal apparently failed this test. Does that mean the next proposal will pass? Not necessarily. It means the company is now operating in a narrower regulatory window where the margin for error is smaller. The regulatory dynamic has shifted from "uncertain but potentially favorable" to "we got our hand slapped, now we must navigate a tighter constraint."

**Here's the risk I'm identifying:** If the Q4 2026 Florida PSC ruling comes back unfavorable—or worse, if it comes back with conditions so restrictive that data-center capex monetization is materially curtailed—the entire "structural opportunity" thesis collapses. And what does that leave us holding? A utility trading at a 4-10% valuation discount relative to peers *for a reason*. That discount would likely persist, perhaps deepen, because the market would have validated the concern that DUK's regulatory relationships are materially more constrained than we assumed.

The Aggressive Analyst quotes a 5-8% upside against a 3-5% downside as a favorable asymmetry. But I'm looking at the data and asking: what if the downside is actually 8-12% if regulatory clarity reveals a fundamental impairment to capex monetization? The Q4 2026 catalyst isn't binary between "mildly good" and "mildly bad"—there's a tail risk where "quite bad" is absolutely possible given the regulatory language we've already seen.

Let me reframe the peer comparison. NextEra (NEE), Xcel (XEL), and PPL are trading at higher multiples not because the market is irrational—it's because those utilities have *already secured* large-load customer contracts or have executed capex with regulatory clarity. Their data-center revenue is *visible*. DUK's is contingent.

The valuation spread isn't an anomaly; it's a *maturity gap*. DUK is one regulatory filing behind its peers in the data-center monetization cycle. That's not a reason to assign it a peer-level multiple. That's a reason to assign it a *discount* until it catches up.

Now, here's the critical point the Aggressive Analyst skips over: **that discount may compress to peer levels *if and when* regulatory clarity arrives. But until it does, we're being asked to hold through an extended period of below-peer valuation on the assumption that resolution is imminent and favorable.** That's not prudent capital stewardship; that's hope masquerading as analysis.

And let's be honest about the dividend coverage claim. Yes, 3.4x OCF coverage sounds fortress-like. But let's look at what's actually happening with free cash flow. The TTM FCF is negative $4.48 billion. That means operationally, the company is burning cash relative to capex. The dividend is being sustained *not* by operational cash generation exceeding obligations, but by the company's access to capital markets to issue debt.

The Aggressive Analyst dismisses this as "how utilities finance"—and yes, that's technically true. But here's where the risk manifests: **if regulatory outcomes become unfavorable, that access to capital markets on favorable terms may evaporate.** Debt investors will demand higher yields if they perceive regulatory risk is rising. At that point, the company faces a choice: cut the dividend or raise even more debt at higher rates, further compressing returns.

We're sitting here at $120.39 assuming that capital markets access remains benign and regulatory outcomes are favorable. Neither assumption is certain.
