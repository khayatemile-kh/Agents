**Error #2: Weaponizing the Asset Impairment as Evidence of Mismanagement**

The aggressive case highlights the $894 million Q2 2026 asset impairment as a "smoking gun" proving capital allocation is broken. This is where I need to be blunt: that argument shows a dangerous lack of rigor.

Asset impairments in utilities are *procedurally driven*, not necessarily indicative of operational failure. When a regulated utility revises its estimate of useful lives, write-down assumptions, or asset valuations, it takes an impairment charge. This is accounting discipline, not distress. 

Moreover—and this matters enormously—*utilities recover asset impairments through regulatory rate-base adjustments*. When Dominion takes a write-down, it's not a permanent loss of shareholder value. It's a balance-sheet restatement that regulators subsequently factor into rate-recovery mechanisms. The aggressive analyst treats the impairment as a pure loss; regulators treat it as a timing adjustment.

Is there a lag between the charge and recovery? Yes. Does that create near-term earnings volatility? Absolutely. Does it signal fundamental business deterioration? No. It signals that management is being appropriately conservative in asset valuations, which is exactly what we should want from a utility that underpins critical infrastructure.

The Hennessy Fund didn't exclude D from its high-ROIC portfolio because of a one-time accounting charge. Institutional capital sees through this noise. So should we.
