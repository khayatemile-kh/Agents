## **1. THE FUNDAMENTAL CASE: QUALITY ASSETS AT ATTRACTIVE VALUATIONS**

### A. Fortress Balance Sheet Meets Quality Asset Base

Let me address the leverage argument directly—because this is where bears will attack first. Yes, D has $53.4B in net debt and a 1.6x debt-to-equity ratio. **But this is not a distress signal; it's the appropriate capital structure for a regulated utility.**

Here's why:

- **Utilities operate in a fundamentally different financial paradigm than industrial companies.** Dominion's debt is backed by **$81.7 billion in regulated, tangible assets** generating stable, long-term cash flows. This isn't speculative leverage; it's operational leverage on a regulated rate base.

- **The $22.3B in construction in progress** isn't a concern—it's the engine of future rate base growth. Each dollar invested in grid modernization, renewable infrastructure, and transmission upgrades flows directly into regulated rate base, which regulators allow to generate **9-11% returns on capital**. This is contractual, not competitive.

- **Debt capacity is being efficiently utilized.** With $2.1B in annual interest expense on $53.4B in debt (a ~4% effective rate), D is benefiting from the financial architecture that all regulated utilities employ. The real question: are the assets generating returns *above* the cost of debt? Yes—the utility's operational margin of 29.2% and EBITDA margin of 46% confirm operational strength.

**The Bears Will Say:** "But net debt is up $4.5B year-over-year!"

**My Response:** *This is exactly what we want to see.* D is investing heavily in grid modernization and renewable infrastructure. The question isn't whether debt is rising—it's whether those investments are generating regulatory returns. They are. The $81.7B in net PP&E (up from $78.3B a year ago) reflects this strategic deployment. In a rate-regulated environment, growth in rate base *drives* shareholder value, not erodes it.

## **2. THE NEXTÉRA MERGER: CATALYST FOR VALUE, NOT EXECUTION RISK**

Now, let me pivot to what bears *will* focus on: regulatory headwinds on the NextEra (NEE) merger. The headline narrative is: "Five New England states are pushing back; the deal is at risk."

**I say this narrative misunderstands the situation entirely.**

### A. Deal Fundamentals Remain Sound

- **NextEra is operationally strong:** 12.6% one-year stock performance, demonstrating excellent execution and financial capacity
- **No financing stress signals:** If the deal were in trouble, we'd see NextEra's cost of capital rising, management commentary shifting, or deal financing falling apart. None of this is happening.
- **D fundamentals are intact:** The Hennessy Fund letter validates that D remains attractive *even as a standalone entity*. This is crucial—it means the deal doesn't need to close for D shareholders to win.

### B. Regulatory Delays ≠ Deal Failure

The bears will point to state-level opposition and extended review periods as deal risk. But here's what they're missing:

1. **Extended timelines are normal in $66.8B utility mergers.** This isn't a surprise. The original timeline was late 2026/early 2027 anyway—extending to mid-2027 is not catastrophic.

2. **State-level opposition has political, not financial, teeth.** The Five New England states want extended comment periods and conditions. This typically means:
   - Community benefits agreements
   - Local hiring or workforce commitments
   - Grid reliability pledges
   - Potentially dividend/rate constraints
   - **But NOT deal failure.**

3. **NextEra has capacity to accept conditions.** If states impose operational requirements, NEE can absorb them. They're acquiring an $81.7B asset base with 29%+ operating margins—they have flexibility to negotiate.

### C. The Merger Upside Scenario

Here's what happens if the deal closes (which I estimate at 70-75% probability):

- **D shareholders receive $66.8B in deal consideration** (exact cash/stock split TBD, but the total value is contractually committed)
- **Implied deal price targets are embedded in that $66.8B valuation**—this sets a price ceiling and floor for D stock
- **Current D price of $66.38 is *already near or slightly above* certain deal consideration models**, meaning D shareholders are not paying a premium for deal upside; they're capturing it at a discount to intrinsic value

**Even if the deal doesn't close** (25-30% scenario), D remains an attractive standalone utility yielding 4%+ with regulated cash flows. So the risk/reward is asymmetric to the upside:
- Deal closes → D shareholders get $66.8B value (minimal additional upside but capital preservation)
- Deal fails → D trades as standalone utility at attractive 4%+ yield with dividend growth tailwind
