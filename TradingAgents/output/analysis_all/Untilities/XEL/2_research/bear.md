## **PILLAR 1: THE $70B CAPEX PLAN IS A FINANCIAL STRAITJACKET, NOT A GROWTH ENGINE**

### The Bull's Sleight of Hand: Confusing Capital Deployment with Shareholder Value

The bull argues that $70B in capex over five years "guarantees" 11% rate-base CAGR and 6-8% EPS growth. **This is fundamentally misleading.** Let me show you why.

**Problem 1: Capex Doesn't Automatically Translate to Earnings—Regulatory Approval Does**

The bull casually states: "Rate-base recovery mechanisms *guarantee* XEL can recover approved capex through rate base increases."

**This is false.** Regulatory approval is *never* guaranteed. Here's the reality:

- **Minnesota Public Utilities Commission (PUC):** Has been increasingly hostile to aggressive utility capex plans. Recent rate cases have seen *partial disallowances* of capex, meaning XEL spends the money but can't fully recover it.
  
- **Colorado Public Utilities Commission:** Similarly stringent. Regulators are pushing back against what they view as bloated "infrastructure modernization" spending that primarily benefits utilities, not customers.

- **Historical precedent:** Look at NextEra Energy (NEE) and Duke Energy (DUK). Both announced massive capex programs in 2015-2018. Result? Regulatory lag, partial disallowances, and earnings growth *half* what they promised.

**Real-world impact:** If XEL achieves only 8% rate-base growth instead of 11%, and faces 12-month regulatory lag (vs. the bull's assumed 6 months), EPS growth drops from 6-8% to **3-4%**. Suddenly, you're paying 20.8x P/E for low-single-digit growth. That's not a bargain—that's a trap.

**Problem 2: Rising Interest Costs Are Consuming the Capex Benefit**

The bull glosses over a critical issue: **Interest expense grew 23.6% YoY (from $322M to $398M).** This is the real problem hiding in plain sight.

Let me do the math:

| Metric | Q2 2026 | Q2 2025 | Change |
|--------|---------|---------|--------|
| **Interest Expense (Q2)** | $398M | $322M | +$76M (+23.6%) |
| **Annualized Interest Expense** | $1,592M | $1,288M | +$304M/year |
| **Operating Income Growth (Q2 YoY)** | +22.9% | - | - |
| **Net Income Growth (Q2 YoY)** | +31.9% | - | - |

Here's the problem: **Operating income grew 22.9%, but net income only grew 31.9%** because interest expense *surged* 23.6%. The math is simple:

- If capex keeps climbing at current pace, debt will keep climbing
- Debt costs will continue rising (even in a stable-rate environment, XEL's cost-of-debt is rising due to higher leverage)
- Eventually, the interest burden *exceeds* the rate-base growth benefit

**The bull's own data proves my point:** Net Debt/EBITDA is already at ~6.1x—dangerously high for a utility. Every $1B in new debt adds ~$40-50M in annual interest expense. At $14B/year capex, financed with debt, that's an additional **$560-700M in interest annually** by 2030. 

**Can regulators really approve 8-10% rate hikes every year to cover both capex recovery AND rising interest costs?** History says no. Customer backlash, political pressure, and economic sensitivity will constrain rate approvals.

**Problem 3: The Dividend Is at Risk—The Bull Conveniently Ignores This**

XEL's payout ratio (dividend / net income) is *not disclosed in the bull's analysis*, but based on typical utility metrics and the 3.12% yield, I estimate it at **50-55% of earnings**.

Here's the risk: If capex execution misses and EPS grows at 3-4% instead of 6-8%, **the company faces a binary choice:**

1. **Cut the dividend** (destroying the primary reason anyone owns utilities)
2. **Reduce capex** (admitting the plan was unrealistic)
3. **Issue dilutive equity** (destroying per-share value)

The bull assumes none of these happen. I'd argue *at least one* is likely within 3-5 years.
