## ON THE INTEREST EXPENSE PROBLEM: BOTH SIDES ARE PARTIALLY CORRECT

The Conservative Analyst correctly points out that net income grew 31.9% YoY while interest expense grew 23.6%. That's mathematically accurate and appears to support their thesis. But—and this is critical—the Conservative Analyst is cherry-picking a single quarter.

Let me zoom out. Here's what the fundamentals report actually shows us about the trajectory:

**Operating cash flow (the truest measure of what the company can actually distribute):**

- Q2 2026: $1,100M
- Q1 2026: $1,697M
- Q2 2025: $1,081M

Wait. Q2 operating cash flow declined $597M quarter-over-quarter. That's a 35% sequential decline. The Conservative Analyst used net income growth as their primary defense, but operating cash flow—which matters far more for a capital-intensive utility—is actually deteriorating sequentially.

Now, the Conservative Analyst would argue this is seasonality (summer months typically have lower electricity demand and therefore lower working capital generation). That's fair. But it creates a dangerous dynamic when combined with rising debt service.

Here's the real problem that *neither* analyst has adequately addressed: **XEL is in a phase where capex spending is front-loaded, but cash inflows from those investments are still lagging.** This creates a temporary but material liquidity squeeze that requires debt financing. The interest expense growth is real, it's accelerating, and it *will* compress margins during this capex ramp phase.

The question—the real question—is whether that margin compression is temporary and acceptable, or whether it signals a fundamental break in the earnings growth narrative. That's not something we can definitively answer from Q2 results alone. We need to see Q3 and Q4 to understand whether operating cash flow stabilizes or continues to deteriorate.

**The Aggressive Analyst sees this deteriorating trend and extrapolates it forward as proof of eventual guidance miss. That's reasonable caution but uncertain timing.**

**The Conservative Analyst sees one good quarter and dismisses the longer-term trend as noise. That's dismissive of real cash flow stress.**

**The neutral position is: This is a legitimate intermediate-term risk that might materialize into guidance pressure by late 2026 or early 2027, but it's not an immediate break.**

## ON VALUATION: THE 20.8X MULTIPLE IS DEFENSIBLE ONLY IF EXECUTION IS FLAWLESS

Here's where I want to push back on both of them, but especially the Conservative Analyst.

The Conservative Analyst keeps pointing to the Forward P/E of 16.62x as evidence that the market is already pricing in some regulatory haircut. But let's think about what that actually means. A forward P/E of 16.62x assumes the company will deliver on its 6-8% earnings growth guidance. If that guidance is revised downward—and the Aggressive Analyst has made a credible case that regulatory haircuts could force a revision—then the 16.62x multiple becomes meaningless.

Historical utility multiples in a 4.5-5% rate environment are indeed lower than the current 20.8x. The Aggressive Analyst is correct on that data point. But here's where the Conservative Analyst has a partial defense: utilities with visible, credible long-term growth can justify premium multiples to historical norms *if the execution risk is low*.

The real question is: Is execution risk low or high? And that's where both analysts are making unjustified certainty calls in opposite directions.

**The Aggressive Analyst says execution risk is HIGH because regulators will disallow capex. But they haven't quantified this risk—they just reference historical precedent without giving us a probability.**

**The Conservative Analyst says execution risk is LOW because the company just delivered strong Q2 results and regulators haven't rejected the plan. But one good quarter and regulatory silence isn't the same as approval certainty.**

Here's what I actually think is defensible from the data: **Regulatory execution risk is meaningfully elevated—let's call it 30-40% probability of material guidance revision—but not certain.** That means the 20.8x multiple is *defensible* at current rates if you believe in full execution, but it's also *vulnerable* to a downward multiple compression if guidance gets revised.

At $75.89 with that risk profile, I'd actually argue the stock is fairly valued with a downside skew—not "screaming sell" but not "hold for long-term growth" either.

## ON DEBT AND LEVERAGE: THIS IS WHERE THE AGGRESSIVE ANALYST MIGHT ACTUALLY BE ONTO SOMETHING REAL

Let me dig into the 6.1x Net Debt/EBITDA figure more carefully because I think both analysts have underestimated its significance in a rising rate environment.

The Conservative Analyst is correct that 6.1x is a *temporary* elevation created by front-loaded capex spending. That's true. But here's what they're glossing over: **the path back to 5.5x leverage requires sustained EBITDA growth that outpaces debt increases, which requires successful capex returns AND successful rate base recovery AND no additional refinancing costs.**

In other words, getting back to 5.5x leverage depends entirely on execution of the very capex plan that the Aggressive Analyst is questioning. If regulatory disallowances reduce expected returns on that capex, then EBITDA growth slows, and the company stays at 6.1x or even drifts higher.

Now, here's where the Aggressive Analyst gets it right: **at 6.1x leverage with rates expected to stay elevated, XEL is vulnerable.** Not imminently—the company has investment-grade ratings and can still access debt markets. But there's a plausible scenario where:

1. Q3-Q4 2026 results disappoint (operating cash flow doesn't stabilize)
2. Regulatory bodies in Minnesota or Colorado signal tougher scrutiny of capex recovery
3. Credit rating agencies downgrade XEL from A- to BBB+ (still investment grade but with higher borrowing costs)
4. Higher refinancing costs further pressure margins
5. 2027 earnings guidance gets revised downward
6. Multiple compresses from 20.8x to 17-18x
7. Stock goes from $75.89 to $60-65

Is that scenario certain? No. Is it materially possible? Absolutely. And at current levels, the market isn't pricing in much cushion for that downside scenario.

**The Conservative Analyst is right that this isn't imminent. The Aggressive Analyst is right that it's plausible enough to warrant defensive positioning.**
