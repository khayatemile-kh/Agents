## REBUTTAL 1: THE MACRO BACKDROP IS ACTUALLY HOSTILE TO PEG'S FINANCIAL POSITION

### The Bull Misframes Rate Stability—It's a *Negative* for Leveraged Utilities

The bull claims: "89% probability of no rate cuts means rate *stabilization*, which is what utilities need."

This is backwards. Let me explain why:

**For a leveraged utility like PEG, rate CUTS are the relief valve. Rate STABILITY at 5.5% is a BURDEN.**

Here's the mechanics:

- **PEG carries $24.68B in debt** (142% debt-to-equity)
- **Current Interest Expense** (implied from Q2 2026 financials): ~$1.1–$1.2B annually
- **Refinancing Risk**: As old debt matures, it's rolled at 5.5% rates. The company cannot escape its leverage via lower rates.

What PEG *needed* (and the bull secretly hoped for) was **Fed rate CUT signals** that would:
1. Lower refinancing costs on new debt issuance
2. Support utility valuations via multiple expansion (lower discount rates)
3. Ease the debt-service burden as a % of operating cash flow

Instead, PEG is trapped in a regime where:
- **Debt costs remain sticky at elevated levels** ($500M issuance in Q2 at stable-but-high spreads)
- **Utility valuations won't expand** (bull admits this) because the 5.5% risk-free rate anchors discount rates
- **Refinancing becomes a perpetual grind**, not a one-time relief

The bull says "no re-rating headwind"—true, but that's code for "no multiple expansion to drive capital gains." PEG's only return driver is earnings growth, which at 3.9% YoY is barely above inflation.

**The macro backdrop doesn't eliminate downside risk; it extends PEG into a grinding, low-return holding pattern.**

### Recession Risk at 8% Doesn't Protect Against Regulatory Risk

The bull argues: "Recession risk is low, so regulators will be sympathetic to rate increases."

Let me highlight what the bull ignored in the macro report: **even in a soft-landing scenario, regulatory approval timelines stretch 18–24 months.** PEG can't wait that long.

Here's the real risk:

- **NY and NJ regulators are increasingly hostile to utility capex spending** that doesn't demonstrably support decarbonization or grid modernization
- **Customer advocates argue against "excessive" rate bases** that over-reward utilities
- **Even with low recession risk, state budgets are under pressure**, making politicians reluctant to approve residential rate hikes ahead of elections
- **PEG's 142% debt-to-equity is already elevated**; regulators will scrutinize whether aggressive capex is prudent or empire-building

The soft landing doesn't guarantee regulatory approval. In fact, in a *healthy* economy, regulators often take a tougher stance: "Why do utilities need rate increases if the economy is strong?"

**The bull conflates macro stability with regulatory favorability. History suggests the opposite.**
