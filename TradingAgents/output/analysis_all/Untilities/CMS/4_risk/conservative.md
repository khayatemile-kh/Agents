## THE FUNDAMENTAL MISREADING: "HIGHER FOR LONGER" ISN'T THE STORY FOR UTILITIES

The Aggressive Analyst leads with a claim that's superficially compelling but fundamentally flawed: utilities suffer when the Fed holds rates steady at elevated levels because "terminal value compresses."

Here's what they're overlooking, and it's critical:

**Regulated utilities don't operate in a pure discounted cash flow world.** They operate under rate-setting formulas established by state regulatory commissions. When CMS serves customers in Michigan, the Michigan Public Service Commission doesn't re-appraise the utility's allowed return to equity based on the Fed funds rate. The Commission sets an *allowed return on equity* (typically 9-10% for utilities like CMS) that's designed to remain stable across rate cases, which occur every 2-3 years.

This is the entire *point* of utility regulation—to create cash flow predictability precisely *because* utilities are capital intensive. The Aggressive Analyst is treating CMS like an unregulated growth company facing compressed multiples in a high-rate environment. It's not. CMS's earnings power isn't compressed by the Fed holding rates at 5.25%. CMS's earnings power is determined by its regulated rate base and allowed return on equity.

So when the Aggressive Analyst says "terminal value of cash flows compresses dramatically," they're applying a DCF logic that simply doesn't apply to 70-80% of CMS's earnings base. The regulated side of the business is *insulated* from this dynamic.

Now, here's where it gets interesting: the Aggressive Analyst does correctly identify that $870 million in debt matures at higher rates. That's true. But let's look at their math critically.
