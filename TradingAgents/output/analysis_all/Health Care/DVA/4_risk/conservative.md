## **The "Oversold Setup" Argument Conflates Technical Rebound Potential with Fundamental Safety**

Yes, RSI hit 24.23 on August 21. Yes, that's technically oversold. The Aggressive Analyst presents this as evidence that the market "overcorrected" and that kidney disease demand didn't change between July 31 and August 5. Correct on both counts. But here's what they're glossing over: *oversold conditions do not guarantee reversal, and they certainly do not guarantee reversal to prior highs.*

RSI extremes are mean-reversion signals, not buy signals. They tell you that momentum has exhausted *in one direction*. That's valuable information for *tactical* trades—bounces of 3-8% from local lows are absolutely tradeable on oversold setups. But the Aggressive Analyst is using oversold RSI as justification for *accumulation* into a position, not for a tactical bounce play. That's a category error.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: DVA is oversold *for a reason*. Yes, the August 5-6 gap down was panic-driven. But the subsequent grind lower from $188.69 to $173.82 (August 5 through August 21) wasn't panic—that was *rational repricing* as the market processed what the negative equity situation actually means for DVA's financial flexibility and balance sheet risk in a stressed scenario.

The Aggressive Analyst says "Berkshire owns 27%, so there's a structural floor." I understand the intuition, but this is dangerous thinking. Berkshire's 27% holding is a *legacy position* from their 2011 merger with HealthCare Partners, not a recent value bet. Buffett has been explicitly critical of Berkshire's healthcare holdings in recent years, noting that the economics are less attractive than they appear. If DVA drops another 20-30% from here, Berkshire's incentive to defend the stock evaporates—they'll mark it down on their balance sheet and move capital elsewhere. Buffett doesn't throw good money after bad to catch falling knives. That's precisely the kind of emotional reasoning we should not emulate.
