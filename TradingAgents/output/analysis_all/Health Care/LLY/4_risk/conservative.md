## **THE "FDA WEAPON" NARRATIVE IS DANGEROUSLY INCOMPLETE**

The aggressive analyst keeps pointing to this new FDA "weapon" as proof that payer headwinds are being solved. Let me be very clear about what we actually know: the FDA gave LLY *something* that strengthens negotiating position with payers. But here's what we don't know, and what the aggressive analyst glosses over:

**First, we don't know what this tool actually is.** The source material says "The FDA Just Handed Eli Lilly Its Best Weapon Yet," but the specific tool isn't detailed. Is it a label expansion? A reimbursement guidance document? A new indication? Until we know what it is, treating it as a solved problem is premature.

**Second, and more critically, no FDA "weapon" solves the fundamental unit economics problem.** The news reports are unambiguous: employer coverage of GLP-1 drugs is collapsing heading into 2027. This isn't because of regulatory uncertainty. It's because the drugs cost $1,000+/month and employers are seeing unsustainable medical spend growth. An FDA guidance document doesn't change that math. A label expansion doesn't change that math. What changes it is either price reduction or sustained efficacy/safety data that justifies the cost—and we haven't seen either at scale yet.

**Third, the FDA action is reactive, not proactive.** If LLY needed an FDA "weapon" to defend payer coverage in the first place, that tells you payers are already pulling back *despite* positive clinical data. The Zepbound study came through positive—and coverage is *still* collapsing. That's not a story where the fundamental problem is being solved; it's a story where fundamentals are deteriorating while messaging improves. Those are different things.

The aggressive analyst asks: why would you not act on this if the FDA just acted in LLY's favor? My answer is simple: **because regulatory messaging doesn't override payer economics.** And the payer economics in the GLP-1 space are hostile right now.

## **THE MERIDA ACQUISITION IS A TELL, NOT A HEDGE**

Think about the timing and sequence here:
- GLP-1 obesity market is booming (2025-2026)
- LLY is cleaning up with Zepbound
- Clinical data is great
- Yet, LLY simultaneously spends $2.875B to buy a Phase I immunology platform

Why would you do that? If the obesity franchise was truly a multi-decade revenue engine generating high-margin cash flow, why spend nearly $3B on milestone-dependent, early-stage immunology bets? 

The answer is obvious when you look at what management is actually signaling with capital allocation: **they don't believe obesity is a durable, long-term franchise at current pricing and coverage levels.** They think it's going to commoditize or face structural reimbursement headwinds that erode margins. So they're hedging by moving into immunology where there's less price competition and more specialty-care positioning.

The aggressive analyst says "that's intelligent portfolio construction." I say **it's a yellow flag that management doesn't believe in the narrative they're selling to investors.**

Now, the aggressive analyst counters by saying the deal is "milestone-dependent, so realistic milestone probabilities are baked in." That's true. But here's the problem: **MER511 is Phase I.** Phase I drugs have roughly 90% failure rates before reaching market. The deal could end up being a $2.875B write-off if the compound fails. And even if it succeeds, we're talking about 2032-2035 commercialization timelines at best.

What does LLY do for cash flow and earnings in 2027-2031 if obesity coverage continues to collapse? They can't fund that gap with a Phase I drug. They're betting that payers will stabilize OR that obesity market remains large enough despite coverage erosion. Both of those bets are increasingly questionable.
