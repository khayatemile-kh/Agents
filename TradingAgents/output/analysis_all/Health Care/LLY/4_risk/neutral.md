Let me cut through both of these narratives directly, because I think they're both seductive in ways that obscure what the data is actually telling us.

The aggressive analyst wants us to treat a 9.4% pullback as a gift-wrapped opportunity to add aggressively into a stock that's already priced at 24.55x forward earnings on 58.5% expected growth. The conservative analyst wants us to treat management's $2.875B acquisition as a confession that the obesity franchise is doomed and that leverage is about to become a noose. ## **WHERE THE AGGRESSIVE CASE FALLS APART**

Yes, the golden cross is intact. Yes, the 200 SMA is 8.8% below current price. Yes, the RSI at 43 means we're not in capitulation territory. I'm not going to dispute any of that technical framework. But here's what the aggressive analyst is doing: they're treating technical structure as sufficient justification for aggressive capital deployment when fundamental uncertainty is actually *widening*, not narrowing.

Let me be specific. The aggressive analyst argues that we should treat the FDA "weapon" as evidence that payer headwinds are being resolved. But this is where I have to push back hard: **that's not what the data says.** The data says payer coverage is collapsing despite positive clinical data. The data says Novo Nordisk, which has oral Wegovy in market, is trading at a 58% discount to fair value because investors don't believe in the unit economics. The data says employers are systematically pulling back from GLP-1 coverage because $1,000+/month drugs don't pencil out when you're managing medical costs.

Now, the aggressive analyst counters: "An FDA weapon helps LLY navigate this better than competitors." Maybe. But "better than competitors" in a shrinking market isn't the same as "profitable growth." If the obesity GLP-1 market shrinks by 40% due to payer pullback, and LLY's share of that shrunken market is 55% instead of 50%, that's still a decline in absolute revenue. That's not a buying signal; that's management trying to defend a deteriorating position.

The aggressive analyst also points to the Merida deal as optionality. And sure, optionality has value. But here's where the aggressive case gets sloppy: they're treating a Phase I asset as though it's uncorrelated to obesity revenue uncertainty when it's actually *correlated with management acknowledging that very uncertainty*. If LLY's leadership believed obesity was a durable, high-margin cash engine for the next decade, why would they be spending $2.875B on milestone-dependent bets that don't generate meaningful revenue until 2032+?

The aggressive analyst's answer is: "That's intelligent portfolio diversification." My answer is: **that's management hedging their base case because they don't believe in it anymore.** Capital allocation tells you what management actually thinks, not what they tell analysts on earnings calls.

And let me address the valuation argument directly. A 24.55x forward P/E on 58.5% expected growth is not automatically cheap just because the PEG ratio is 1.12. That math only works if LLY actually delivers 58.5% growth. What happens if they deliver 35-40% growth instead because obesity coverage erodes faster than payers can be convinced otherwise? Suddenly that 24.55x P/E becomes 35-40x on a smaller earnings base. That's a 50-60% downside scenario, and it's not a tail case—it's increasingly looking like the base case.

The aggressive analyst is essentially asking us to bet that the positive catalysts (Zepbound study, FDA action, Merida deal) are *enough* to overcome structural payer resistance. I think that's optimistic.

## **BUT THE CONSERVATIVE CASE IS ALSO OVERSTATING THE RISK**

Now, to the conservative analyst: you're right that management is clearly concerned about obesity sustainability, right that leverage is rising, right that 58.5% EPS growth is a heroic assumption. But I think you're sliding into a narrative where every piece of evidence becomes proof that the disaster is imminent. That's not balanced either.

Here's what you're missing: **LLY is still generating $7.1B in net income in Q2 2026 on $23B in revenue.** That's a 33.5% net margin in a healthcare business. That's exceptional. Even if obesity revenue declines by 20-30% from peak, LLY still has enormous cash generation to service $48B in debt. That debt-to-equity ratio of 162% looks scary in isolation, but it's serviceable in a business that's throwing off $28B in annual EBITDA (annualizing Q2).

You're also treating the Merida deal as evidence of doom when it might just be evidence of opportunism. Yes, MER511 is Phase I. But the therapeutic area is immunology, where unit economics are fundamentally better than obesity. If MER511 fails, LLY hasn't destroyed shareholder value—they've just spent $2.875B and learned something. If it succeeds, they own a potentially massive asset at an early valuation. That's actually reasonable risk-taking from a capital allocation perspective, not panicked hedging.

And here's where I really diverge from your narrative: **you're assuming payer coverage collapses to near-zero.** The actual data doesn't support that. The data supports payer pullback, optimization, and likely some price pressure. But "optimization" doesn't mean "elimination." It means GLP-1 market remains very large, just smaller and lower-margin than investors currently expect. That's still a multi-billion-dollar business, and LLY's market share is strong.

So your downside scenario (obesity revenue craters, debt becomes problematic, stock declines to $900-1,000) is possible. But it's not the most probable outcome. The most probable outcome is somewhere between: obesity market stabilizes at 60-70% of current expectations, payers work out risk-sharing agreements, LLY's EPS growth comes in at 35-40% instead of 58%, and the stock re-rates to a lower multiple but doesn't crater.
