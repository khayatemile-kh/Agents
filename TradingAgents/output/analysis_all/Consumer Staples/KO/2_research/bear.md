## **1. PRICING POWER IS AN ILLUSION—THIS IS DEMAND DESTRUCTION**

My opponent has built the entire bull thesis on one assertion: "KO has pricing power." Let's examine this claim with brutal honesty.

### **The Actual Story Behind the "Pricing Power"**

Yes, KO raised prices. And yes, margins expanded. But here's what the bull conveniently glosses over:

**The fundamental reality:** When a company experiences simultaneous revenue growth *and* margin expansion on pricing alone, it signals **demand destruction at higher price points.** Here's the math:

- **Revenue growth:** 6.7% YoY (Q2 2026)
- **If pricing alone drove this:** Volume must be *flat or declining*
- **Why?** If volume remained robust + pricing increased = revenue growth would be in the double digits

The fact that revenue growth (6.7%) is *lower* than historical averages while margins expand tells me consumers are buying *less* at higher prices. This is textbook demand destruction.

### **The Data Point They'll Ignore:**

Look at the operating income growth (+8.0%) versus revenue growth (+6.7%). The spread is only 1.3 percentage points. In a healthy pricing power scenario with true volume resilience, operating income should grow *significantly* faster than revenue as leverage kicks in. Instead, we're seeing minimal leverage, which suggests **volume headwinds are eating into the pricing gains.**

### **Here's the Real Risk:**

What happens when consumers fully adapt to the new price points? When the low-hanging fruit of price increases is exhausted? KO will face a choice:
1. **Stop raising prices** → margins compress back to historical levels (destroying the bull narrative)
2. **Keep raising prices** → volumes accelerate downward, triggering a death spiral

This is not "proven competitive advantage"—this is a company *extracting value from its customer base before demand truly breaks.*

### **The Comparison to PepsiCo Exposes the Weakness:**

Bull analyst claims PEP is struggling while KO dominates. But look deeper: PEP is also raising prices in beverages. The difference? **PEP has snacking to offset beverage volume pressure; KO has nothing but beverages.** When consumers get priced out of beverages, where does KO's growth come from? Nowhere.

## **2. THE DIVIDEND YIELD TRAP: "2.41% is Fine" is Dangerous Thinking**

### **The Yield-on-Cost Fallacy:**

My colleague argues: *"Don't worry about the 2.41% yield; investors who bought 10 years ago have a 6-8% yield on cost!"*

**This logic is fundamentally broken.** Here's why:

**Today's investor doesn't get yield-on-cost. Today's investor gets 2.41%.** And more importantly, they're paying $88/share for the *privilege* of getting that 2.41% yield. That's a **poor risk-adjusted return** in an environment where:

- **10-year Treasury yields:** ~4.2% (risk-free!)
- **S&P 500 dividend yield:** ~1.5% with *growth potential*
- **High-quality corporate bonds:** ~5.5% with less volatility

KO's 2.41% yield **does not adequately compensate for equity risk**, especially when the company is leveraged at 115% debt-to-equity and rates are predicted to stay elevated.

### **The Dividend Growth Story Has Problems:**

Bull says: "5-7% dividend growth is historically consistent."

**But look at the constraints:**

| Factor | Problem |
|--------|---------|
| **Free Cash Flow** | $5.2B annually; dividend payout is ~$7-8B → The company is **paying more in dividends than it generates in FCF** |
| **Debt Reduction** | Company claims it's deleveraging, but with net debt of $30.6B and FCF of $5.2B, actual debt reduction is minimal (~$5B/year) while simultaneously funding dividends AND buybacks |
| **The Math Doesn't Work** | FCF ($5.2B) = barely covers capex ($2B) + base dividend ($7-8B). Where does the *growth* in dividends come from? **More debt or cutting buybacks.** Neither is sustainable. |

### **The Real Risk: Dividend Yield Trap Unwind**

When the market realizes that KO cannot sustain 5-7% dividend growth *while* deleveraging *while* maintaining current capex, here's what happens:

1. **Dividend growth expectations reset** → Forward earnings estimates fall
2. **Stock multiple compresses** (P/E falls from 26x to 20x) → Capital loss
3. **Yield rises** as stock falls → But it's a "value trap" unwind, not a buying opportunity

An investor buying at $88 for a 2.41% yield with declining growth expectations is positioned for a **death by a thousand cuts.** You're getting paid 2.41% to wait for disappointment.

**Wrong.** Here's the actual implication:
