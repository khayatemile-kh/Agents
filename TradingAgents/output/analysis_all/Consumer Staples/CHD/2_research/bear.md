- Reduce usage of discretionary personal care items

The bull claims this is "efficient leverage." Let's test that claim against reality:

**What happens if CHD's brands lose value?**

If the market reassesses the value of Arm & Hammer, OxiClean, and others due to competitive pressure, private-label competition, or volume declines, the $6.11B intangible asset base gets written down. When that happens:

1. **Goodwill impairments reduce equity directly**

3. **Debt-to-Equity ratios spike dangerously**
4. **Credit ratings get cut**
5. **Cost of capital increases**
6. **Dividend cuts become possible**

The bull's counterargument—that Net Debt/EBITDA of 1.26x is "manageable"—is true *only as long as EBITDA holds up.* But if volume declines accelerate and margin compression intensifies, EBITDA contracts. At that point, 1.26x becomes 1.5x, 1.75x, or higher very quickly.

**Moreover, there's a hidden risk the bull ignores: share dilution in the form of buybacks funded by debt.** The company has a $300M buyback authorization while carrying $2.21B in debt. This is financial engineering—using leverage to buy back shares and artificially inflate EPS. It's not a sign of confidence; it's a sign of desperation to keep EPS growing when organic growth is slowing.

The intangibles are not a moat—they're a *liability* in a downturn. They represent the premium the company paid for acquisitions. If those brands underperform, you don't get that premium back.
