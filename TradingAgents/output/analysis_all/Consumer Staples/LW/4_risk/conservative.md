## THE TECHNICAL CASE IS OVERSTATED AND IGNORES REVERSAL SIGNALS

The 10 EMA crossing above price at $53.91—okay, granted. But look at the broader structure: the 50-day SMA is still rising and sitting at $49.66, well below current price. The 200-day SMA is at $46.53. We have **three moving averages all pointing upward and in proper bullish sequence.** Price is above all three. That's not a breakdown; that's a pullback within an established uptrend.

Now, about that "critical floor" at $49.66. The Aggressive Analyst is suggesting a breakdown below this would invalidate the uptrend and trigger an 11% move down. But let's be honest about what that means: **price would need to fall 5.7% from current levels just to hit the 50-day SMA.** That's well within normal consolidation range for a stock that just rallied 24.5% over nine weeks. This isn't a setup for disaster; it's a setup for normal mean reversion within a bullish intermediate trend.

And here's the critical point that the Aggressive case completely sidesteps: **Bollinger Bands show price contracting into the lower band at $51.07.** When bands contract, they eventually expand. The question isn't whether price will drop to $49.66; it's what happens *after* it tests that level. Historically, when equities in established uptrends pull back to the 50-day moving average on band contraction, they don't break through—they find support and re-initiate the move higher.

The September 1st 5.2% reversal? That's being characterized as "exhaustion," but I read it differently. A single large reversal day after a move that stretched nearly 25% is actually a **healthy de-rating of overbought extremes.** It's not panic; it's profit-taking. And profit-taking off resistance highs is where institutions reload for the next leg up, not where they abandon positions.

The RSI divergence is real, I'll grant that. But RSI at 50.72 is neutral—it's not oversold. It's not signaling imminent collapse. It's saying momentum is cooling, which is exactly what you'd expect after a strong rally. **Momentum cooling is not the same as trend reversal.** This is a critical distinction the Aggressive framing collapses.

**Q3 2026 gross margin came in at 20.4%—the best in recent quarters.** This is not a company losing pricing power or hemorrhaging from input costs. This is a company that has successfully managed cost inflation and maintained pricing discipline. The TTM gross margin of 20.7% is stable and healthy. When a company is supposedly in structural decline, you typically see margin compression, not stability.

EBITDA margin in Q3 2026 was 15.5%, with TTM at 16.7%. Again, these are healthy numbers. The company is not seeing operational deterioration; it's seeing organic volume pressure that is being offset by efficiency and pricing actions.

Here's what I think the Aggressive case is missing: **organic revenue decline in a mature, defensive business often stabilizes before it reverses.** It doesn't just keep declining forever. And there are early signals this company might be approaching stabilization:

- **Inventory levels at $968.5 million are elevated, which could indicate either weak demand OR strong advance ordering ahead of anticipated price increases or demand recovery.** The Aggressive case assumes the former; I'm not convinced that's the right read.

- **The company has been systematically reducing net debt, with a 5.6% YoY reduction in the absolute debt balance.** That's meaningful for a leveraged company. This is not a firm in distress; this is a firm executing financial discipline.

- **Forward EPS is projected to grow 62% to $3.37 versus TTM of $2.07.** Now, the Aggressive case dismisses this as "market optimism," but let's be serious: if the company is in structural decline with no recovery catalysts, how does EPS grow 62% year-over-year? ## THE LEVERAGE CONCERN IS REAL, BUT NOT A NEAR-TERM CATALYST FOR EQUITY LOSSES

Okay, I need to address the elephant: the 221% debt-to-equity ratio and the 3.49x net debt-to-EBITDA.

The Aggressive Analyst is correct that these are elevated metrics. In isolation, they're concerning. But in context, they're not catastrophic, and they're certainly not a reason to take short risk at current price levels.

Here's why:

**First, LW operates in a defensive sector with predictable, recurring cash flows.** Lamb Weston sells frozen potatoes to quick-service restaurants, food processors, and retail chains. These are contracted relationships with sticky demand. During recessions, people still eat french fries. The cash flows from this business are not volatile; they're relatively stable.

**Second, the company is *actively deleveraging.*** Net debt fell 5.6% year-over-year while equity improved 2.8%. That's the right trajectory. At this pace, net debt-to-EBITDA could approach 3.0x within 18-24 months, which is a solidly investment-grade metric.

**Third, interest coverage at 3.68x is adequate.** It's not comfortable, but it's not stress-test territory either. And again, this metric should improve as EBITDA expands and debt continues to decline.

The Aggressive Analyst is suggesting that a 10% EBITDA decline would push leverage to stress levels. Fair point in theory. **But what would actually cause a 10% EBITDA decline in a frozen potato company?** A foodservice recession? We're pricing only 8% recession probability through year-end, and the company has already weathered multiple economic cycles. A demand shock? The business is too sticky for that.

Here's the real risk with the Aggressive recommendation: **we'd be shorting a company that is executing exactly what creditors want to see—debt reduction, stable margins, disciplined capital allocation—and we'd be betting against that execution.** Why would we do that?

The $68 million cash balance is low, I'll grant that. But it's not a crisis. The company has $5+ billion in inventory and receivables that convert quickly. Working capital is positive. And if there's ever a stress scenario, the company has access to the commercial paper market and asset-backed lending given its asset base.
