## WHERE BOTH OF YOU ARE MISSING THE REAL RISK

Now let me articulate what actually concerns me about both arguments, because you're both dancing around the core issue without directly addressing it.

**The real problem isn't the leverage or the restructuring. It's the cash flow volatility combined with cyclical sector exposure in a no-rate-cuts environment.**

Let me be specific. The macro research shows 89% probability of zero Fed rate cuts through 2026. That means we're in a sustained 5%+ rate environment indefinitely. For a company carrying 4.76x net debt-to-EBITDA, that environment is *structurally hostile.* Here's why:

When rates are high and stable (or rising), heavily leveraged companies face three sequential headwinds:

1. **Refinancing risk accelerates.** Amcor's debt structure requires periodic rollover. Every dollar that matures gets refinanced at current rates. If the company has $2-3B in maturities annually (typical for a $16.7B debt portfolio), that's meaningful refinancing risk. Higher rates eat into normalized interest expense and compress coverage ratios further.

2. **Consumer cyclicality bites harder.** Packaging demand is tied to consumer spending. With rates at 5%+ and the Fed holding, consumer balance sheets are getting stressed (high mortgage rates, elevated auto loans, credit card rates at historical highs). The base case of "steady demand" is only credible if consumers don't pull back. But the macro research itself notes "consumer cyclical headwinds risk" if "tight monetary policy could eventually pressure consumer demand." That's not alarmism; that's the consensus view.

3. **Normalized profitability is compressed.** Restructuring charges will eventually lap, yes. But when they do, Amcor won't be operating in a high-growth, low-rate environment—it'll be operating in a flat-growth, high-rate environment. The "margin expansion" the aggressive analyst is banking on (from PCR products gaining share) is modest and incremental, not transformative. A single Tiptree deal doesn't prove market inflection.

**Here's what I think both of you are missing:** The right framework isn't "should we be bullish or bearish?" It's "what's the realistic base case, and what conditions would make it break?"
