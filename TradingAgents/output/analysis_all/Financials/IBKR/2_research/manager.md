**Recommendation**: Overweight

**Rationale**: Both sides make reasonable points, but weighing them carefully the bull case rests on firmer, more verifiable ground while the bear case, though raising legitimate cyclicality concerns, overreaches in several places.

On profitability: the bull's numbers (22.5% operating income growth, 39.3% net income growth, 85.6% operating margin) are actual reported Q2 2026 figures, not projections. The bear's rebuttal that this is "peak volume" is a fair caution, but the bear's own "normalized" $800-900M net income estimate is itself a guess with no historical baseline provided—it's speculation used to counter hard data. Notably, the bear doesn't dispute the trend of sequential acceleration (Q1→Q2 +13.2%), just its durability.

On valuation: the bear's critique that 26.2% forward EPS growth implies more margin expansion than 10.4% revenue growth suggests is a genuinely sharp catch — it exposes a real tension in the bull's PEG math that wasn't reconciled. This is the bear's strongest point and should temper enthusiasm on the "cheap" framing. However, even the bear's own downside scenario still lands at 19-22x forward earnings on normalized volumes for a business generating 24% ROE, 89%+ gross margins, and essentially zero debt — that's not an unreasonable multiple for a quality compounder, it's just not "screaming cheap" either.

On macro: the bear's argument that higher rates suppress trading velocity by making cash/bonds more attractive is theoretically plausible but contradicted by the actual data presented — trading volumes and revenues are accelerating right now in this same higher-rate environment, and IBKR's exceptionally elevated margins are happening concurrently with the rate backdrop the bull describes. The bear's counter-argument is more theoretical than evidenced here.

On international expansion: the bear's caution about white-label revenue share (Korea) and execution risk (LatAm) is well-founded — the bull's "$5-10B TAM" figure is unsubstantiated speculation, and the bear is right to flag that historical white-label deals contributed <5% growth. This lowers confidence in that pillar as a near-term catalyst, though it doesn't negate the current strong core business.

Net take: the bear successfully punctures the more speculative bull claims (PEG "undervaluation," TAM expansion figures, unqualified rate tailwind), but does not overturn the core empirical facts — accelerating revenue, expanding margins, high ROE, pristine balance sheet, essentially zero debt, and real earnings growth happening today, not hypothetically. The bear's thesis is essentially "this could reverse when volumes normalize," which is a valid risk to monitor but is not evidence of current deterioration; it's a call for caution on sizing and multiple expansion, not a reason to exit a business executing this well. Given the demonstrated operating leverage and balance sheet strength versus a bear case built largely on hypothetical de-normalization and overstated multiple concerns, I lean bullish but tempered — this warrants a constructive add-to-position stance rather than aggressive full conviction Buy, given the legitimate cyclicality and margin-durability risks the bear raised.
