The bear's stablecoin argument is more damaging in principle (disintermediation risk to money-market complex, and banks controlling custody rather than needing BLK), but it's speculative on both sides — no hard evidence yet that stablecoins are cannibalizing BLK AUM today. The bear's direct-stock-pick critique (Firefly, power names) is a fair point about mark-to-market risk being smaller and less relevant than the diversified fee base, but it's a rounding error relative to BLK's ~$11-12 trillion AUM engine. Where the bear is weakest: they never seriously engage with the valuation argument (PEG <1, FCF yield ~8%) or the core AUM/fee flywheel that doesn't require any single new revenue stream to work. Where the bull is weakest: the exact dollar figures on stablecoin/AI-linked EPS accretion are speculative and probably overstated, and the technical/momentum debate favors caution near-term given the pullback from highs.

Net: the debate is not perfectly balanced. The bull's core valuation argument (reasonable forward multiple against strong consensus EPS growth, high FCF yield, and multiple optionality sources — AI-linked fund flows, private credit, real estate, and even nascent digital-asset infrastructure) is more evidence-based and doesn't hinge on any single narrative working out. The bear raises legitimate risk flags (capex deceleration, stablecoin disintermediation, mark-to-market risk on direct stakes) that argue against aggressive buying here, but doesn't present evidence that BLK's core moat (world's largest asset manager, Aladdin platform, scale advantages in ETFs/private markets) is actually eroding. This tilts toward a constructive-but-not-maximally-aggressive stance: Overweight rather than a full conviction Buy, acknowledging bear's near-term risk (technical pullback, speculative bolt-on bets, stablecoin regulatory uncertainty) while recognizing the valuation and core franchise strength support adding exposure gradually rather than aggressively chasing or trimming.
