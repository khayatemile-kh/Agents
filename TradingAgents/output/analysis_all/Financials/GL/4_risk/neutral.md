## **THE RATE CONVERSATION IS WHERE BOTH ANALYSTS MISS THE NUANCE**

The Aggressive analyst builds their entire bearish case on the premise that rates will fall, compressing NII and earnings. The Conservative analyst counters that 89% of the market expects no Fed cuts through year-end 2026, so GL has a 12-15 month runway.

Both are partly right, but both are oversimplifying how rates actually affect GL's earnings.

Let me be precise about what the macro data tells us:
- **89% probability of no Fed rate cuts through end-2026.** That's clear. Rates stay put for the next four months.
- **59% probability of a soft landing.** That's not the same as 89%. It's actually lower confidence.
- **Soft landing probability is declining** (-2.5 percentage points week-over-week).

The Aggressive analyst uses this declining soft-landing probability to argue for stagflation risk. The Conservative analyst dismisses it by saying soft landings are "good for insurers." Both are partly correct.

Here's the nuance: **a soft landing is good for GL's underwriting business** (low lapses, stable mortality, strong sales). But it may be *neutral to modestly negative* for GL's NII because soft landings eventually lead to rate cuts. The market's 59% soft-landing probability already reflects the expectation of eventual rate cuts in late 2026 or 2027. This isn't a surprise; it's priced in.

The real risk is if we get **stagflation** (the probability of which is rising, albeit from a low base). In stagflation, you get higher inflation with slowing growth. That's bad for both GL's NII (real returns compress) and GL's underwriting (lapses may rise as policyholders strain on cash). But that's only a ~35-40% probability scenario at best.

So the macro setup is actually **moderately favorable for GL**, not unfavorable. The Aggressive analyst is overweighting the tail risk of stagflation. The Conservative analyst is underweighting the certainty that rate cuts *will* eventually happen, compressing valuations.
