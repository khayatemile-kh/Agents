The market gave a housing collapse a near-zero probability in 2007. **Prediction markets are useful anchors, but they are not reality.**

More critically, even if rates stay elevated, that doesn't automatically mean M&A volumes remain robust. Corporate deal-making is driven by CEO confidence, access to capital, and strategic opportunity—not merely interest rate levels. We saw this clearly in 2023-2024: even as the Fed paused rate hikes, M&A activity remained depressed relative to historical norms because corporate executives were uncertain about growth prospects. ## The FCF Analysis Ignores the Structural Deterioration Embedded in the Numbers

**Margin Expansion:** The aggressive analyst says net income grew from $4.06B to $4.16B—a $100M increase, or 2.5%. That's *not* margin expansion; that's margin compression relative to revenue growth. **Working Capital Management:** The aggressive analyst credits working capital improvements for freeing up cash. Once you've done this optimization, you can't repeat it. If the $500M working capital benefit in 2025 was a one-time swing, then normalized operating cash flow reverts closer to the $4.30B run rate we saw in 2024.

This is precisely what the research team flagged: **2025 FCF of $5.0B contains one-time elements that won't persist.** The aggressive analyst dismisses this concern by pointing to CapEx stabilization at 1.1-1.3% of revenue. But that's a non-sequitur. Even if CapEx remains stable, if operating cash flow normalizes downward due to working capital reversals, FCF will normalize downward as well.

**Here's the critical math the aggressive analyst avoided:** If we assume:
- Organic revenue growth of 5-6% (conservative for mature brokers)
- Operating margins that stabilize rather than expand (more realistic than the aggressive case)
- Working capital improvements that partially reverse (one-time benefit not repeatable)

Then normalized FCF is closer to **$4.2-4.5B**, not $5.0B. And if we're in a recession scenario—which, while priced at 8%, is *not zero*—then FCF could compress to $3.8-4.0B as commissions and advisory fees decline.

The aggressive analyst is extrapolating a peak-cycle FCF performance and calling it "normalized." That's exactly the kind of reasoning that gets firms into trouble.
