## **THE FUNDAMENTAL PROBLEM: AON JUST OVERPAID FOR A MATURE ASSET AT THE WORST POSSIBLE TIME**

Let's start with the core issue the bull is trying to minimize: **the valuation math doesn't work, and the financing is reckless.**

### The 6x Sales Multiple Is Expensive—Full Stop

The bull argues that "6x sales looks high in isolation, but context matters." I agree—context *does* matter. And here's the context that matters:

**1. Insurance brokers don't trade at 6x sales for a reason:**

Let me pull actual market comparables:
- **Marsh McLennan (MMC):** 2.8x sales on $20B+ revenue base
- **Willis Towers Watson (WTW):** 3.1x sales
- **Brown & Brown (BRO):** 4.2x sales (the bull's own example of a consolidator)
- **NFP (when private):** Estimated 4.5-5.0x sales
- **AON itself:** 4.0x sales on its own equity

**So why is USI trading at 6x?** The answer isn't "superior quality"—it's **KKR needed an exit and AON desperately wanted scale.** This is the classic M&A trap: one party has an *urgent need*, the other has an *exit deadline*, and the seller's leverage determines price. AON just signaled it had the urgent need.

**2. The recurring revenue argument is weak:**

The bull claims USI's revenue is "highly recurring and sticky." Fair point—it is. But here's the thing: *so is the revenue of every other insurance broker AON could have acquired at 4-5x sales.* The stickiness isn't unique to USI; it's endemic to the business model. AON paid a 25-50% premium for the *exact same customer stickiness* it could have gotten cheaper elsewhere.

**3. Compare to actual synergy multiples:**

When strategic buyers (not financial sponsors like KKR) acquire platforms for *synergies*, they typically pay 4-5x sales. Why? Because 1-2x sales is *already baked into the base business value*. The remaining multiple pays for synergies. 

- If AON paid 6x and realizes 15-20% cost synergies, it's effectively paying 5.3-5.7x for *organic cash flow plus synergies*
- But the bull hasn't explained: **Why couldn't AON achieve similar organic growth at a cheaper price?**

## **THE DEBT FINANCING IS A MASSIVE RED FLAG—ESPECIALLY NOW**

Now let's address the bull's second core claim: that the debt-to-equity ratio is "misleading" and actually fine.

I'm calling bullshit on this framing. Here's why:

### The Debt Metrics Are Actually Worse Than the Bull Admits

Yes, debt-to-equity of 163.48x is "distorted" by goodwill on the balance sheet. The bull is technically correct. But let me reframe the *actual* problem:

**1. AON's balance sheet is already highly leveraged:**
- Net Debt: $13.91 billion
- TTM EBITDA: $5.9 billion
- **Net Debt-to-EBITDA: 2.36x** (the bull cites this as "manageable")

But here's the issue: **This ratio is measured on AON's EBITDA alone, pre-USI.** Once you integrate USI's debt into the equation:

- USI acquisition will add ~$17 billion to total debt
- Pro-forma net debt: ~$30.9 billion
- Pro-forma EBITDA (including USI): ~$7.0-7.5 billion (estimated)
- **Pro-forma Net Debt-to-EBITDA: 4.1-4.4x**

**Is 4.1-4.4x manageable?** Technically, yes—it's investment-grade. But it's **at the outer edge** of comfort for a financial services company. Here's why this matters:

### **2. The macro environment is hostile for debt-financed M&A:**

Remember what the macro report said? Let me quote directly:

> "Fed Rate Path: 89% probability of **NO rate cuts in 2026.** Restrictive macro backdrop; elevated borrowing costs; growth headwind."

Translation: **Interest rates are staying elevated through 2026 and likely into 2027.** 

What does this mean for AON?

- **The debt-to-EBITDA ratio assumes stable EBITDA growth.** If macro deteriorates (which the same report said is only 8% recession probability—read: "tail risk that gets ignored until it happens"), EBITDA contracts and the leverage ratio explodes.
- **Corporates are going to be cautious on advisory spending** if recession fears rise. The bull assumes 8-10% organic growth continues; I'm saying that assumes a benign macro that is *not* guaranteed.

### **3. The buyback suspension is not trivial—it signals management lack of confidence:**

**The market is correctly interpreting this:** Management is saying "we need all available cash to service this debt for the next decade." That's not a bullish statement.
