## **The Macro Environment Argument Conflates Two Separate Risks**

The aggressive analyst makes a clever but ultimately flawed argument about the macro backdrop. They say: "We get the rate-hike boost without the recession headwind." That's presented as asymmetric upside, but it's actually a false dichotomy that ignores real insurance sector dynamics.

Let's break this down:

**On the rate-hike benefit:** Yes, higher rates support net investment income. The company manages $56.51 billion in investments, so a rising rate environment theoretically helps reinvestment yields. But—and this is critical—that benefit is only realized if the portfolio is actively redploying capital into higher-yielding instruments. Much of a large insurance company's portfolio is locked into longer-duration assets and bonds already yielding lower rates. The portfolio doesn't instantly re-price upward when Fed policy shifts.

Moreover, rising rates have a documented negative impact on *equity valuations* within insurance company portfolios. Many of HIG's $29.52 billion in equity investments will see mark-to-market losses as discount rates rise. The net effect on total investment income is far less clean than the aggressive case suggests. You don't get "free" earnings accretion. You get a mix of benefits and headwinds.

**On the recession probability:** Now here's where I need to push back hardest. The aggressive analyst cites an 8% recession probability for 2026 as if that's comforting. But let me ask: what's the probability of a *meaningful economic slowdown* short of a full-blown recession? What's the probability of credit stress, even if GDP stays positive? What's the probability of elevated catastrophe losses in a climate-volatility environment?

The market is not priced for "no recession." The market is priced for "no recession *right now.*" That's vastly different. An insurance company writing policies on a 12-24 month basis is exposed to *realized* catastrophe experience and claims inflation over that horizon—not just the probability of recession.

And here's the data the aggressive case glosses over: the world affairs report flags elevated leverage at HIG (Debt-to-Equity of 22.33x, though that's typical for insurers). If we do get economic stress—even short of recession—that leverage becomes problematic. Rising unemployment → higher claims on disability/workers comp lines. Commercial stress → higher surety losses. That's not theoretical. That's the insurance sector playbook when economic headwinds emerge.

The 8% recession probability provides false comfort. It's not a guarantee against claims deterioration.
