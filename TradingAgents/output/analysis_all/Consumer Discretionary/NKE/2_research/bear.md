## **OPENING STATEMENT: Nike's "Recovery" Story Is Built on Sand**

I appreciate the bull's optimism, but let me be direct: **we're looking at a company in structural decline being packaged as a "temporary headwind" story**. Yes, Q4 margins look impressive at 49.2%. Yes, the balance sheet is strong. But here's what the bull is conveniently glossing over: **strong balance sheets don't fix broken growth engines, and one quarter of margin recovery doesn't erase a deteriorating business trajectory**.

The stock isn't down 10.5% because the market is irrational. It's down because institutional investors—including Dick's Sporting Goods, Nike's own wholesale partners, and retail at large—are voting with their feet. That's a signal we ignore at our peril.

Let me systematically dismantle the bull case and show you why Nike is a value trap, not a value opportunity.

## **SECTION 1: THE "EARNINGS RECOVERY" IS A MIRAGE**

### **A. One Good Quarter After Freefall Doesn't Constitute a Turnaround**

The bull is hanging everything on Q4 2026's margin recovery. Let's look at the actual context:

| Quarter | Revenue | YoY Revenue Change | Gross Margin | Operating Income |
|---------|---------|-------------------|--------------|------------------|
| **Q4 2026 (May 31)** | $10.97B | **UNKNOWN** | 49.2% | $1.31B |
| **Q3 2026 (Feb 28)** | $11.28B | **UNKNOWN** | 40.1% | $553M |
| **Q2 2026 (Nov 30)** | $12.43B | **UNKNOWN** | 40.6% | $1.01B |
| **Q1 2026 (Aug 31)** | $11.72B | **UNKNOWN** | 42.2% | $927M |

**Here's the critical problem:** The bull is celebrating 49.2% gross margin as "pricing power intact," but **this margin expansion is almost certainly driven by aggressive inventory clearance and favorable product mix—not by healthy underlying demand**.

Here's why:
1. **Margins expand when you clear junk inventory at premium markups temporarily** — but this isn't sustainable. Once the excess inventory is gone, you revert to normal margin profile (40-43%).
2. **The revenue trend tells the real story**: Q4 revenue of $10.97B is down from Q2's $12.43B. That's a **$1.46B or 11.7% sequential decline**. 
3. **The bull conveniently omits the revenue trajectory**: If Nike were healthy, we'd see stable or growing revenue with *healthy* margins. Instead, we see **shrinking revenue with temporarily inflated margins due to inventory clearance**. That's the definition of milking a situation, not a turnaround.

**The Bull's Counter Argument Will Be:** "Q4 margins prove pricing power and operational excellence; the revenue will recover once inventory is cleared."

**My Rebuttal:** Show me the evidence in the data. Where are the order trends from wholesale partners? Where's the guidance that suggests revenue recovery? The bull is extrapolating hope, not evidence. And the data from wholesale partners like Dick's Sporting Goods—which just cut guidance by 31%—suggests the opposite: **consumer demand for apparel and footwear is soft, and Nike isn't immune**.

### **B. The Q3 Collapse Reveals Structural Weakness**

Let me dig deeper into Q3 2026 (ended February 28, 2026):

- **Revenue:** $11.28B (down 9.2% sequentially from Q2's $12.43B)
- **Gross Margin:** 40.1% (a 65 bps collapse from Q2's 40.6%)
- **Operating Income:** $553M (down 45% from Q2's $1.01B)

The bull waves this off as a "rough quarter" that was immediately corrected. But **a 9.2% revenue drop isn't rough—it's a warning sign of demand destruction**.

Here's what this tells us:
1. **Demand fell off a cliff in Q3.** Nike didn't just have inventory to clear; Nike had demand that evaporated. That's different from "excess stock we can work through."
2. **The margin compression (40.1% vs. 40.6% prior quarter) suggests the inventory clearance required heavy discounting.** If Nike had simply mixed better or operated more efficiently, margins would have *expanded*, not contracted.
3. **Management's inability to forecast demand accurately** is itself a red flag. If Nike's planning teams couldn't see Q3 demand softness coming, what confidence do we have in their ability to execute a turnaround?

The bull argument—"companies that can adjust quickly are exactly the ones worth owning"—is backwards. Companies that *need* to adjust quickly are companies that *failed to see problems coming*. That's a sign of poor operational discipline and forecasting capability, not excellence.

But notice the trend: The Q3 trough suggests if we normalize for the demand cycle, a run-rate closer to **$2.8-3.0B net income** is more realistic, not the $3.11B the bull cites. And that's assuming Q4's recovery is durable—which is a big assumption if wholesale demand remains soft.

**The real forward picture:** Net income likely to be $2.8-3.0B annually, not $3.11B. That means the forward P/E of 16.69x is actually closer to **18-19x on normalized earnings**, not the discount the bull claims.

## **SECTION 2: THE BALANCE SHEET STRENGTH IS IRRELEVANT TO THE CORE PROBLEM**

### **A. A Strong Balance Sheet Can't Fix Broken Growth**

The bull makes much of Nike's fortress balance sheet:
- $7.56B cash
- 0.74x D/E ratio
- $12.06B working capital
- 1.96x current ratio

I agree: **this is a strong balance sheet**. But here's the fundamental problem: **balance sheet strength is only valuable if it's deployed to solve business problems. Nike's fortress balance sheet is being used to prop up dividends and buybacks—not to fix the actual issues.**

Look at the strategic choices Nike is making:
1. **Maintaining a 4.3% dividend yield** — This is $3B+ annually going to shareholders while core business faces headwinds.
2. **Executing share buybacks** — More capital being deployed to boost EPS rather than invest in competitive positioning.
3. **Hiring a new CCO (Jane Ewing)** — A tactical org restructuring, not a fundamental strategic pivot.

**Where is the balance sheet strength being deployed to compete in China?** Where is the aggressive R&D investment to counter Li-Ning and ANTA? Where's the capital investment in DTC capabilities to offset wholesale channel weakness?

**The answer: Nowhere.** Instead, Nike is using its balance sheet to *manage decline gracefully* — paying dividends, buying back shares, making incremental tactical moves. That's what mature, no-growth companies do. That's not what you do when you're executing a turnaround.
