## 1. THE "CASH MACHINE" NARRATIVE IGNORES DETERIORATING UNIT ECONOMICS

### The Fatal Flaw in the Bull's Logic

The bull frames MCD as "a real estate company that happens to license a restaurant brand," arguing that franchisees bear traffic risk while MCD collects rents regardless.

**This is theoretically elegant and operationally false.**

Here's why:

**MCD's Revenue Model Breakdown:**
- **Company-operated restaurant sales:** ~$7.1B (TTM)
- **Franchisee royalties (% of sales):** Typically 5-6% of franchisee sales
- **Rent and property income:** Derived from franchisee revenues

**The Critical Math:**
When franchisee sales decline—which is *exactly what's happening right now*—MCD's royalty income *automatically* declines. It doesn't matter that MCD "owns the real estate." If a franchisee's sales drop 3-5%, MCD's percentage royalty drops by the *same percentage*.

**Real Impact (Q2 2026 Signals):**

From the sentiment report: *"Restaurant traffic likely to stay stable in second half **after depressed quarterly trends**"* (UBS)

Translation: **Q2 traffic was DOWN. H2 is expected to be FLAT, not recovering.**

A franchisee paying 5.5% royalty on $100k in daily sales is paying $5,500/day. If that franchisee's sales drop to $95k (a 5% decline that we're seeing in real time), royalty revenue drops to $5,225/day—a $275 daily hit per location.

**Multiply that across 30,000+ franchised locations globally, and the headwind is massive.**

The bull says: *"Franchisees bear the operational risk."* 

**Technically true. Economically irrelevant.** Because MCD's profitability is mathematically tied to franchisee sales volumes through royalty structures.

### Where's the Evidence of Declining Franchisee Performance?

The company-operated restaurant sales in Q2 2026 were $7.1B. Let me compare that to historical trends:
- This is *lower* than prior-year comparables (implied by "depressed quarterly trends")
- If company-operated stores are under pressure, the 30,000+ franchised stores are experiencing *worse* pressure (they're typically in less premium locations)

**The bull has no answer for why royalty income would remain stable when transaction volumes are visibly declining.**

**So 21x P/E isn't a "discount"—it's the market correctly re-rating MCD to a lower-growth profile.**

## 3. "TRAFFIC PRESSURE IS CYCLICAL" – THE BULL'S MOST DANGEROUS ASSUMPTION

### What the Data Actually Says vs. What the Bull Wants to Believe

The bull's entire thesis rests on this assumption:

*"When we see 'depressed traffic' in a non-recessionary, no-rate-cut environment, what we're observing is consumer **caution**, not consumer **collapse**."*

**Let me expose the three critical errors in this logic:**

#### **Error #1: The Market Has Already Factored In "No Recession"**

The bull cites:
- 8% recession probability
- 0% Fed rate cut probability
- "Tight labor market"

**But here's what the bull ignores:** The fact that we're *not* in a recession and rates aren't cutting *despite* consumer weakness is *itself* a negative signal for MCD.

Why? Because:
- If consumers are cautious and depressed *without a macro excuse*, that caution is more structural
- If the Fed isn't cutting because inflation is sticky, that means **discretionary spending will remain under pressure**
- A "tight labor market" means **wages aren't rising fast enough to offset inflation on essentials** (rent, food, utilities), leaving less for restaurant spending

The bull is citing macro stability as proof of recovery. **Actually, macro stability with declining traffic suggests the decline isn't macro-driven—it's *competitive* and *structural*.**

#### **Error #2: Q2 Depressed Traffic Should Have Been Worse if Macro Was Truly Supportive**

Let me flip the logic:
- Labor market is tight (per bull)
- Recession risk is only 8%
- Yet traffic is *depressed in Q2* and expected to only *stabilize* in H2

**If macro is so supportive, why is traffic DOWN in the *current* quarter?**

The answer: **Consumers are choosing NOT to spend at MCD**, despite having the ability to do so.

This is a *choice*-driven decline, not a *capacity*-driven one. And choice-driven declines are *stickier* and *more competitive in nature* than macro-driven ones.
