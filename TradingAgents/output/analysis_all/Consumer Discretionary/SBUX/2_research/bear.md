## **OPENING STATEMENT: The Illusion of Recovery**

But when you strip away one-time gains, account for mounting structural headwinds, and consider the macroeconomic environment we're entering, SBUX at $106 is expensive—not just in absolute terms, but relative to the risks ahead.

Let me systematically dismantle the bull thesis, point by point.

## **SECTION 1: THE NEGATIVE EQUITY CATASTROPHE—NOT AN "ACCOUNTING ARTIFACT"**

My colleague's defense of SBUX's **-$7.67 billion negative equity** is creative but fundamentally misleading. Let me explain why.

### **The "Accounting Artifact" Argument Doesn't Survive Scrutiny**

**The bull's claim:** "Negative equity is just ASC 842 operating leases being capitalized. Look at net debt-to-EBITDA instead—it's 1.74x, which is healthy."

**My response:** This is misdirection. Here's why:

**1. The Real Problem: Structural Capital Destruction**

Let's be clear about what negative equity actually means:
- **Total liabilities ($36B) exceed total assets ($28.3B) by $7.67 billion**
- This isn't just an accounting restatement—this reflects that the company has *destroyed shareholder capital* over time
- The equity deficit has *worsened* from Q2 2025 to Q2 2026 (equity more negative), not improved

From the data:
- **Q2 2026 Equity:** -$7.67 billion
- **Q2 2025 Equity:** We can infer this was less negative given the debt reduction

**The trajectory is the problem.** A company aggressively paying down debt while equity gets *more negative* is signaling that it's *consuming cash to reduce liabilities*, not building value.

**2. ASC 842 Is a Red Herring**

Yes, lease obligations are now capitalized. But here's the trap in the bull's logic:

- **Pre-ASC 842, SBUX's reported equity might have been ~$1-2 billion positive**
- **Post-ASC 842, it's -$7.67 billion**
- The difference is the $9.16 billion in capital lease obligations

**But here's what the bull conveniently ignores:** Those $9.16 billion in leases are *real obligations*. They're not an illusion. Whether they appear on the balance sheet or in footnotes, SBUX is legally bound to pay those lease obligations for decades. The accounting change didn't make them disappear—it made them visible.

**The real implication?** SBUX's true leverage picture is *far worse* than it appears. The company is essentially insolvent before considering operating performance.

**3. The McDonald's Comparison Is Misleading**

The bull argues: "Look at MCD—net debt-to-EBITDA is 3.5x vs SBUX's 1.74x, yet MCD has positive equity."

**Why this comparison fails:**

- **McDonald's owns significant real estate**, which appears as an *asset* on the balance sheet, offsetting debt
- **Starbucks leases its stores**, so lease obligations appear as *liabilities* with no corresponding asset
- McDonald's higher leverage ratio (3.5x) reflects a different business model (owned real estate = higher absolute debt), not weaker financial position
- **When you adjust for the business model difference, McDonald's is actually less leveraged than SBUX**

The key metric should be **operating cash flow to total obligations** (debt + lease obligations). Let me be direct: I suspect SBUX's ratio is weaker than MCD's once you normalize for structure.

**4. The Capital Returns Problem: Cannibalizing Equity to Fund Dividends**

Here's the most damning point:

SBUX is maintaining:
- **Dividend yield of 2.34%** on a $121.66B market cap = roughly **$2.8B annual payout**
- **Share buybacks** (not specified in data but historically significant)

Meanwhile, **stockholders' equity is negative and worsening**.

This means: **The company is borrowing money to pay shareholders.** It's not generating sufficient *retained earnings* to fund these returns. Instead, it's taking on debt and burning through equity.

Compare this to a healthy company:
- Healthy companies: *Retained earnings build equity over time*
- SBUX: *Retained earnings are negative ($-8.54B), so dividends consume future cash*

**For a consumer cyclical stock entering a period of potential consumer weakness (8% recession probability, no Fed cuts through 2026), this is reckless capital allocation.**

## **SECTION 2: THE Q2 "EARNINGS EXPLOSION" IS A MIRAGE**

Now let's dissect the earnings story, which is the centerpiece of the bull case.

### **The Math Doesn't Work Once You Adjust for One-Time Gains**

The bull highlights:
- **Net Income:** $1.05B vs $558M prior year = **+88%**
- **EPS:** $0.91 vs $0.48 = **+89%**

**But here's what gets glossed over:** The Q2 2026 results included a **$536M gain on sale of business units**.

Let's do the math:
- **Reported Net Income:** $1.05B
- **Less: Gain on Sale:** -$536M
- **Adjusted Net Income:** ~$514M
- **Adjusted YoY Growth:** ~-8% (essentially flat to slightly down)

**The headline +88% growth? It's entirely driven by a one-time transaction.**

The bull acknowledges this ("future quarters won't have this gain") but then hand-waves it away by saying "the forward guidance reflects ongoing operational profitability."

**Here's my problem with that:** Forward earnings of $3.16 likely *don't* incorporate another $536M gain. So we're being asked to believe that Q3-Q4 2026 will deliver massive operating profit increases to justify forward EPS of $3.16.

**But where's the evidence?** 

- **Q1 2026 EPS:** $0.45 (normalized, excluding gains)
- **Q2 2026 EPS:** $0.91 (but ~$0.47 of that is the gain)
- **Normalized Q2 EPS:** ~$0.44

**So on a normalized basis, we're flat to down YoY.** That's not a turnaround. That's stagnation with a one-time gift wrapping.

### **Revenue Is Declining—A Major Red Flag**

The bull argues: "Revenue declined only -1.5% YoY, but profitability exploded. This shows pricing power and mix optimization."

**Let me reframe this:** When a company's top line is contracting while it's aggressively cutting costs, it's usually not a sign of strength—it's a sign of *contraction*.

- **Q2 2026 Revenue:** $9.32B
- **Q2 2025 Revenue:** $9.46B
- **Decline:** -1.5%

And from the TTM data:
- **TTM Revenue:** $38.34B
- **Most recent quarter:** Slight sequential decline Q1 to Q2 ($9.53B → $9.32B)

**The pattern is concerning:** Revenue is rolling over. The company is maintaining profitability *through cost-cutting*, not through growth. In a consumer cyclical business, revenue decline + cost-cutting is a yellow flag for emerging demand weakness.
