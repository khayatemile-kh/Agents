Once the refunds are exhausted, TSCO goes back to fighting for shelf space against Amazon and Walmart with no competitive advantage. That's not bullish—that's a temporary pain killer masking a broken leg.

**Argument 2: "Consumer spending is weak, but Petsense is resilient pet spending."**

This is the classic mistake of trying to hide sector weakness by pointing to one subsegment. Yes, pet spending is more defensive than farm equipment or seasonal merchandise. But here's the thing: **Petsense still has to compete in an e-commerce world where Amazon has a 41% share of online pet supplies.** An exclusive Instacart partnership is nice, but it doesn't change the fact that Petsense is a rounding error within TSCO's total revenue base. We're talking about same-day delivery from 130 stores—that's table scraps compared to TSCO's 2,000+ store footprint and multi-billion-dollar inventory. And here's the kicker: this deal was announced and priced in *before* August 12 when TSCO hit $36.18. The rally peaked right after the catalyst dropped. That's textbook exhaustion.

**Argument 3: "The stock is down 42.5% YTD, so it's oversold and due for a reversal."**

Wrong. A stock that's down 42.5% is down because the business is deteriorating, not because it was temporarily mispriced. And the deterioration is ongoing. Let me show you: Q2 2026 net income of $360.7M is 16.1% lower than Q2 2025's $430M. Gross margins are under pressure. EBITDA margins fell from 15.77% in Q2 2025 to 13.15% in Q2 2026. These aren't cyclical dips—these are structural shifts. A 42.5% decline isn't an overreaction; it's the market *correctly* repricing a company that's losing profitability in a consumer discretionary environment where consumers are stretched thin. The recent 14% bounce is hope, not healing.
