## The Core Bear Thesis: Three Structural Problems

Let me start here because this is the foundational flaw in the bull argument.

**One 76-unit turbine order does not a business transformation make.**

Here's what the bull glosses over: That single Dynamis order, while strategically significant as a *proof of concept*, represents **approximately $76M in top-line revenue**. Against BKR's $27.73B TTM revenue, that's **0.27%** of annual sales.

For context:

- The Dynamis order would represent roughly **4.2 days of normal quarterly revenue**
- Even with optimistic 20% gross margin, it adds maybe $15M in gross profit

**So when the bull says "This signals BKR capturing a piece of the data center boom," what they actually mean is: "One customer ordered turbines, and we're extrapolating that into a major business line."**

That's speculation dressed up as strategy.

**BKR's turbine business is:**

### **2. THE BALANCE SHEET ISN'T FORTRESS-LIKE—IT'S FRAGILE ENGINEERING**

Now let me address the bull's "clever balance sheet" argument because this deserves real scrutiny.

**Yes, BKR has $15.73B in cash. Let's talk about how they got it.**

The bull frames the $9.89B debt issuance in Q1 2026 as "proactive balance sheet engineering to lock in favorable rates." 

**Here's what actually happened:**

| Metric | Q4 2025 | Q2 2026 | Change |
|--------|---------|---------|--------|
| **Cash** | $3.72B | $15.73B | +$12.01B |
| **Total Debt** | $6.09B | $16.25B | +$10.16B |
| **Debt-to-Equity** | 0.32 | 0.81 | +153% |
| **Net Debt** | $2.37B | $0.53B | -$1.84B |

Let me translate this: **BKR borrowed $10B to sit on an extra $12B in cash.** The company didn't borrow money because it needed to fund operations or strategic investments. It borrowed money to have cash on hand.

**Why should that concern us?**

1. **Interest Rate Risk**: BKR just issued $9.89B in debt into a **higher-rate environment** (89% market probability that the Fed holds rates through 2026). They're locking in borrowing costs at elevated levels. When the inevitable rate cuts finally come in 2027, they'll be sitting on expensive debt while competitors refinance at lower rates.

2. **The Cash Burn Question**: The bull says "with $4.4B in annual FCF and $15.7B in cash, BKR can easily delever." 
   
   **But where is this cash deployment happening?** If BKR is generating $4.4B in FCF and has no major capex needs, but is instead *accumulating* cash, that signals one of two things:
   - **Management has no confidence in deploying capital effectively** (red flag)
   - **They're building a war chest for a major acquisition or shareholder distribution they haven't announced** (speculative, unpriced risk)

3. **The Debt Maturity Schedule**: BKR now has $15.48B in long-term debt. The company needs to manage refinancing risk. If oil prices collapse, or if energy capex spending dries up, BKR's FCF generation could plummet—and suddenly that $9.89B debt burden becomes very real. The company borrowed aggressively *just as* energy sector fundamentals are flattening (rig count unchanged).

**Here's the real concern: This looks like borrowing into weakness, not strength.**

If management was truly confident in the outlook, why lock in expensive debt today instead of maintaining optionality? The answer is: they're either hedging for a downturn they see coming, or they're managing markets. Neither is bullish.
