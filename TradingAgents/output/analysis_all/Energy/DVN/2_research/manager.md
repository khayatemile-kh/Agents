**Recommendation**: Underweight

**Rationale**: The debate boils down to a valuation and balance-sheet risk assessment, and I find the bear's rebuttals more rigorous and better grounded in the actual numbers presented.

On valuation: the bull's 9.04x forward PE 'discount' argument doesn't hold up once you accept that energy earnings are commodity-cyclical and inherently deserve a lower multiple than the broad market or even staples/tech comps - that's an apples-to-oranges comparison the bull leans on too heavily. More damning, the bull's own scenario table is a double-edged sword: it shows the current $49 price already implies WTI sustaining at $85-87/bbl. Since WTI has averaged $65-75/bbl over the last decade and the bull's own admission is that today's $85+ price is substantially a geopolitical risk premium (16% invasion probability), the downside case if that premium unwinds is real and, using the bull's own math, points to ~$32-38 fair value at $70-75/bbl oil. The bear's added point about multiple compression in downturns (historical energy PEs compressing to 7-8x in stress periods) compounds this risk further - the bull is implicitly betting on both sustained elevated oil prices AND stable multiples, which is a low-probability double condition.

On the balance sheet: the bull calls 1.16x net debt/EBITDA "fortress" but the bear correctly flags a mechanical flaw - the EBITDA denominator is TTM and includes three quarters of pre-acquisition (smaller) EBITDA against post-acquisition debt in the numerator, meaning the truly annualized post-deal leverage is likely closer to 1.4-1.6x, a much less comfortable level. The working capital collapse - current ratio falling from 1.01x to 0.72x, working capital swinging from +$28M to -$1.99B in a single quarter - is a legitimate red flag that the bull hand-waves as "integration noise." A sub-1.0x current ratio combined with $1.5B of near-term debt maturities is a real liquidity concern that deserves to be priced into risk, even if outright default is unlikely given operating cash flow. The bull didn't adequately rebut this mechanical leverage-timing issue or the magnitude of the working capital deterioration.

That said, I don't think this is a full-blown Sell. The company does generate substantial free cash flow (even if the bull's $6.9B FCF figure is likely optimistic given real capex needs for a doubled asset base), the dividend appears currently coverable, and oil prices, even if they moderate, are not being priced by the bear to actually collapse to crisis levels - the bear's $35-38 target is only 25-30% downside, not a thesis of imminent failure. The debate is genuinely tilted bearish but not to a degree that screams full liquidation - there's optionality if oil stays firm and integration synergies materialize. Given the risk asymmetry (leverage looks worse than headline suggests, working capital deteriorated sharply, and the bull's own valuation math implies substantial downside if the geopolitical oil premium fades), I'm siding with the bear's case as the stronger, better-evidenced argument, but calibrating to Underweight rather than outright Sell because the FCF generation and potential de-leveraging path over time provide a partial offset and this is a name where trimming rather than fully exiting is the more prudent response to the identified risks.
