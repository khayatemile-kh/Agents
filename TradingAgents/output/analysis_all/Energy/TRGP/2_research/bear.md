## PILLAR 1 COLLAPSE: THE EXXONMOBIL CONTRACT ISN'T THE GAME-CHANGER THE BULL CLAIMS

### A. Fee-Based Contracts Don't Eliminate Commodity Risk—They Transform It

The bull made a seductive argument: "Fee-based means no commodity exposure." **This is demonstrably false.**

Yes, Targa gets paid a fee *per unit of volume transported.* But here's what the bull glossed over:

**1. Volume Risk Replaces Margin Risk**

- The ExxonMobil contract locks in volumes *at current production expectations.* But what if ExxonMobil's Permian assets underperform?
- What if shale production plateaus faster than expected?
- What if energy prices crater and ExxonMobil reduces capital spending, cutting volumes to Targa's infrastructure?

**Historical Precedent:** During the 2020 COVID crash, oil prices fell below $20/barrel. ExxonMobil and other E&Ps **slashed capex by 30-40%.** Volumes through midstream dropped precipitously, even on fee-based contracts, because there was less *stuff* to transport.

The bull conveniently ignored this: **fee-based contracts pay on volume. If volumes decline due to economic weakness or underperformance, Targa's revenues decline too.**

The 20-year term is a *mirage.* It's not a revenue guarantee; it's a *potential* revenue stream contingent on ExxonMobil's operational success and capital allocation decisions.

**2. Acreage Dedications ≠ Binding Volume Commitments**

The bull emphasized "extensive acreage dedications." Let me clarify what this actually means in midstream contracts:

- An acreage dedication is a *preferential right* for Targa to handle production from a specific geographic area
- It is **not** a minimum volume take-or-pay clause
- ExxonMobil retains the optionality to use competing infrastructure, especially if competing systems offer better economics

**Real-world example:** Midstream companies learned this lesson painfully in the 2015-2016 downturn. Many had "exclusive" acreage dedications, but E&Ps simply didn't develop the acreage. Volumes evaporated.

**The bull never addressed this risk.** The contract provides *optionality for ExxonMobil*, not certainty for Targa.

### B. The Permian Boom Assumptions Are Fragile

The bull's entire thesis rests on: "Three new Permian projects will drive EBITDA growth in 2027-2028."

**Here's the problem: This is entirely contingent on execution and market conditions.**

**1. Project Delays Are Rampant in Energy Infrastructure**

Capital projects in midstream frequently experience:
- Cost overruns (10-30% is common)
- Timeline delays (6-18 months beyond guidance is typical)
- Reduced scope due to capex discipline

The bull offers **zero evidence** that Targa's projects are on track. No timelines. No budget details. No production ramp schedules.

**Red flag:** If these projects are so transformational, why isn't management providing specific milestones in earnings calls or investor materials? The silence is deafening.

**2. Permian Production Is Slowing**

Here's a data point the bull should confront: Permian crude oil production growth has **decelerating sharply.** According to U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA):

- **2021-2023:** Permian production grew 10-15% annually
- **2023-2024:** Growth slowed to 3-5%
- **2024-2026 outlook:** Industry consensus is 2-3% annual growth going forward

**Why?** Shale decline curves are steep. Permian wells deplete 50-70% in the first 5 years. Sustaining production requires ever-increasing drilling just to maintain flat volumes.

This means:
- ExxonMobil's long-term production outlook is likely *flatter* than the bull assumes
- The "growth runway through 2046" may be a plateau-through-2046
- Volume growth assumptions baked into the bull's thesis are optimistic

**The bull cited no production growth forecasts. Convenient omission.**
