## WHAT THE AGGRESSIVE ANALYST GETS RIGHT (AND WRONG)

A 7.95% decline with a death cross in early August, sustained negative MACD momentum, and weakening moving average positioning are legitimate warning signals. Let me be specific. The aggressive analyst argues that "RSI can stay in the 30-50 range for months during prolonged downtrends" and therefore the bounce from 33.76 to 40.09 in a single day is a "trap." But here's where they're misreading their own data: **a 6.33-point jump in RSI in one session is not characteristic of a prolonged downtrend. That's capitulation exhaustion followed by covering.** Sustained downtrends show gradual, grinding RSI deterioration—not violent reversals. The aggressive analyst is essentially claiming that technical capitulation signals are bearish. That's backward.

On the valuation argument, the aggressive analyst makes a reasonable point about P/B compression being possible, but then they leap to a conclusion: "At 1.5x book value, the stock is worth $55." Okay—but on what timeline? Over what macro scenario? If rates stay elevated for two years and occupancy declines 200 basis points, sure, maybe. The aggressive analyst is presenting a bear scenario as inevitable, when it's actually *one plausible outcome among several*.

If they held the dividend through a *credit crisis with widespread tenant bankruptcies*, they can handle a 50-basis-point occupancy decline in a no-recession environment. The historical precedent matters precisely *because* it's from a worse macro environment.

## WHAT THE CONSERVATIVE ANALYST GETS RIGHT (AND WRONG)

The positive divergence forming (RSI weaker relative to price decline) is a legitimate technical observation. These are all valid points that the aggressive analyst hand-waves away as "traps."

But the conservative analyst also has a critical blind spot: **they're essentially arguing that the trader should hold and wait for stabilization signals to fully confirm.** That's reasonable if you're managing risk within a position, but it's *not* an argument that the current sell decision is wrong. The conservative analyst keeps saying "we're not reversing yet, but stabilization is forming"—which is true—but that's an argument for *trimming* or *rebalancing*, not for holding the full position and hoping.

More problematically, the conservative analyst is downplaying the leverage concern in a way that feels like rationalization. Yes, REITs operate at higher leverage than manufacturers. Yes, 5.5-5.9x debt-to-EBITDA is "within normal REIT ranges." But here's what they're glossing over: **normal REIT ranges were established in a declining interest rate environment (2010-2022).** We're now in a regime where rates are staying elevated longer than consensus expected. That changes the calculus.

The conservative analyst also sidesteps a core problem: the current valuation at 3.20x price-to-book is genuinely elevated. In a rising cap rate environment, that multiple *should* contract. The question isn't whether it contracts—it will. The question is *how much, how fast, and from what price*. The conservative analyst seems to assume we've already priced in most of the pain (hence the 7.95% decline), but that's not necessarily true. The market could easily re-rate down another 10-15% if:

1. Q3 earnings show occupancy pressure
2. Refinancing costs are disclosed at higher levels than expected
3. The Fed signals no cuts through mid-2027 (raising the odds above 10%)

These aren't extreme scenarios. They're plausible.
