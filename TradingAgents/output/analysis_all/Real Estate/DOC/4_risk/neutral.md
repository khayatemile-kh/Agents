But **you haven't proven the payout ratio is actually unsustainable.** Healthcare REITs often operate at payout ratios in the 70-90% range for FFO, which is normal and sustainable. You're claiming >200%, which would be a red flag, but without actual FFO disclosure, that's an assumption, not a fact.

The financial report shows Q2 revenue at $771.6M, up 2.5% sequentially from Q1's $753.0M. **If revenue is growing but EBITDA is declining, that suggests either:**

1. Operating expenses are rising faster than revenue (margin compression)
2. One-time charges or non-recurring items are embedded in Q2 results

You haven't differentiated between these possibilities. Margin compression would be concerning. One-time charges would not. Your binary interpretation—"Q2 EBITDA fell, therefore operations are deteriorating"—conflates correlation with causation.

You're calculating leverage ratios using potentially inflated near-term denominator volatility, which overstates the risk.

You've claimed the 8% recession probability and 89% no-rate-cut probability are tailwinds for healthcare REITs. Let me push back.

Healthcare REITs, while defensive, still face risks in a recession: occupancy might increase but *pricing power* declines, operators take margin hits, and if the recession is severe enough to impact Medicare/Medicaid reimbursement rates, that cascades directly to DOC's tenant profitability and thus rent growth.

The 89% no-cut probability similarly cuts both ways. It means markets aren't bracing for rate hikes, which is good for refinancing risk. But it also means *no cuts* to cushion valuation multiples. If we're stuck at 4.5% funding costs and DOC's FFO yields only 4.8% (at $1.00 FFO and $20.82 price), you've got minimal spread. That's not a tailwind; that's a compressed risk-reward trapped between rates and earnings growth.
