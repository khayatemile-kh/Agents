## The "Tariff Narrative is Unproven" Argument Actually Proves Too Much

The Aggressive Analyst says the market "rejected" FSLR's tariff beneficiary thesis when SolarEdge rallied 8% while FSLR "drifted down" on the inverter curb news. This is presented as evidence that FSLR can't pass tariff costs to customers. But this argument commits a category error that concerns me deeply.

SolarEdge and First Solar operate in *completely different market segments*. SolarEdge makes residential inverters and power electronics. FSLR makes utility-scale modules. The inverter curbs directly benefit SolarEdge because they protect its core domestic inverter market from foreign competition. That's a *direct hit* to their competitive advantage. Of course SolarEdge rallied.

But here's what the Aggressive Analyst glosses over: **FSLR also benefits from those same inverter curbs, just indirectly**. When foreign inverters become more expensive, the entire installed cost of residential systems rises. That creates *system-level inflation* that trickles down to module pricing power across the board. The fact that SolarEdge rallied *more* doesn't mean FSLR rallied *incorrectly*. It means the market was calibrating relative valuations.

But more importantly, let's examine the actual evidence about tariff pass-through in solar. The research report notes that FSLR manufactures domestically while competitors rely on imports. That's not theoretical—it's structural. When you impose a $0.38/watt tariff on imported modules, you've just created a permanent cost floor for the entire industry. FSLR doesn't have to "pass" that cost to customers; the tariff *does it for them*. Competing imported modules now cost more. FSLR's modules look competitive not because they cut prices, but because competitors can no longer undercut them.

The Aggressive Analyst suggests utility developers with locked-in PPAs can't pass tariff costs. But that's backwards. Those PPAs lock in *module prices at current costs*. When import tariffs raise competing module costs, FSLR has *more* negotiating power on the next tranche of PPAs. And the industry data shows utility-scale developers constantly refresh procurement. They're not locked into decade-old supplier relationships.

So when the Aggressive Analyst says "we don't know how tariff pass-through will play out," what they're really saying is: *we're waiting for perfect certainty before acting*. But perfect certainty never arrives in markets. We're supposed to assess *probabilities*, not wait for proof. And the probability that domestic manufacturing is advantaged by anti-import tariffs is not speculative—it's basic economics.

The Canadian Solar comparison is a red herring, by the way. CSIQ is a vertically integrated manufacturer facing margin compression across the board, largely because it's *dependent* on Chinese supply chains. FSLR has a different business model, different supply chains, and different cost positioning. The fact that CSIQ is struggling tells us nothing reliable about FSLR's tariff positioning.
