## WHERE THE AGGRESSIVE ANALYST WINS—AND WHERE THEY OVERREACH

The aggressive analyst is absolutely right that BMY's technical setup is genuinely constructive. The moving average alignment, the RSI at 64.28, the MACD beginning to rebuild—these are all legitimate bullish indicators. I'm not going to pretend they aren't. And yes, a 9.87x forward P/E for a company in a strong operational position is objectively cheap relative to historical pharma valuations.

But here's where the aggressive analyst makes a critical error: they're treating valuation and technicals as if they exist in a vacuum, independent of balance sheet reality. They keep circling back to the EBITDA coverage argument—"with $18.93 billion in EBITDA, even at $1.5-2 billion interest expense, that's fortress-level coverage." Okay, I hear that. But they're fundamentally misunderstanding what the market is actually pricing in.

The aggressive analyst says the 9.87x forward multiple is a "mispricing" and that the discount to healthcare sector averages is an "opportunity." Let me reframe that: **the market is not mispricing BMY; the market is correctly pricing a company with unresolved leverage questions into a discount that reflects execution risk, not just operational risk.**

Here's the thing the aggressive analyst glosses over: they claim interest expense is $1.5-2 billion. But the conservative analyst actually did the math here more carefully. If BMY's debt is $65-75 billion (which aligns with a post-Celgene acquisition balance sheet), and debt service costs are 4-5% in the current environment, we're looking at $2.6-3.75 billion in interest expense. That's not a small delta from the aggressive analyst's assumption. That's a material difference that changes the solvency calculus.

So here's where I push back on the aggressive analyst: **you're right that the technical setup is strong, but you're using that technical strength to justify ignoring leverage risk. And that's backwards.** Strong technicals on a highly leveraged company don't reduce leverage risk; they just mean the equity holders are more optimistic about near-term price appreciation. That's not the same as saying the balance sheet is safe.

The aggressive analyst also makes an odd argument about the 44.5% forward EPS growth. They say the "burden of proof" is on skeptics to explain why EBITDA-to-EPS math doesn't work. But that's not how financial analysis works. When management projects forward earnings that are materially inconsistent with the company's current net income, the burden of proof is on management to explain the source of that growth. The aggressive analyst can't just hand-wave past that by saying "the company is generating $8.11 billion in FCF, so the math must work." FCF and net income are different things—one is an accounting construct, the other is a cash reality. The aggressive analyst conflates them.

And in pharma, a 10% decline in EBITDA typically takes years to manifest, not quarters. It's not like a cyclical industrial company where demand can crater overnight. Pharmaceutical revenue is relatively stable because the patient base is relatively stable.

More importantly, the conservative analyst is being inconsistent about what constitutes prudent capital allocation. They say the hold is "prudent" and that we should wait for "management clarification" on debt and growth. But management is not going to voluntarily clarify a problematic balance sheet situation by waving a red flag. If they're going to address leverage, they'll do it through actions—debt reduction, asset sales, slower acquisition pace—not through press releases. So the conservative analyst is essentially saying "wait for evidence of balance sheet repair that may never come in explicit form."

That's not prudent; that's paralysis disguised as prudence.
