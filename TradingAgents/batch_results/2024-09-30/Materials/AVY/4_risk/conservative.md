- Revenue is $9.25 billion

- ROE is 31.16%

**What we don't know, but absolutely need to know:**
- Total debt outstanding (the 158.65x ratio tells us debt is extremely high, but doesn't give us the absolute figure)

- Debt maturity profile (how much is due in the next 3 years?)

- Covenant structures that might trigger forced deleveraging

The Aggressive Analyst casually asserts "2-3x debt service coverage ratios" and calls this "investment-grade territory." But 2-3x coverage is the *bare minimum* acceptable for investment-grade debt—it's not comfortable, it's survivable. And this calculation assumes:

1. **Free cash flow remains stable at $1.0 billion.** But AVY is a consumer cyclical company. In a recession or extended slowdown, FCF contracts. If consumer spending weakens and packaging volumes decline, that $1.0 billion could drop to $700 million, $600 million, or worse. That's when 2x coverage becomes 1.3x coverage—dangerously tight.

2. **Interest rates stay where they are.** The Aggressive Analyst points to the 68% probability that rates hold through October 2026. That's only 12 months away. What about the bonds maturing in 2027, 2028, 2029? If AVY needs to refinance and rates have spiked due to broader economic stress, debt service costs explode. And we're operating in an environment where "higher for longer" is the consensus—meaning rates aren't going down anytime soon. Refinancing risk isn't some phantom concern; it's a structural feature of a leveraged company in a high-rate environment.

3. **The balance sheet doesn't deteriorate.** But that's not guaranteed. If a recession hits and AVY needs to raise capital to maintain operations or pay down debt, they'd be forced to raise it in a distressed market. The 1.13 current ratio they cite as "adequate" becomes a liability when liquidity needs spike.

Here's the crucial point the Aggressive Analyst won't articulate: **The 31.16% ROE they're celebrating is partly a function of the aggressive leverage.** You generate higher returns on equity when you use a lot of debt. But that's not genius; it's accounting math. It means if anything goes wrong operationally, the leverage amplifies downside just as much as it amplifies upside. When the market corrects, those estimates get slashed.
