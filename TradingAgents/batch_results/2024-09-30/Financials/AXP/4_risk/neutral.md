## **Aggressive Analyst: Your NII Stabilization Thesis Has a Timing Problem**

I appreciate the reframing of the 148% interest expense growth as "lagging," but you're conflating two different things: whether NIM will stabilize, and whether it stabilizes at high enough levels to support 22% forward EPS growth. Those are not the same claim.

You're right that deposit repricing is in a more mature phase than it was in 2023. But here's where your logic breaks down: you're betting that the repricing is "largely complete" based on the fact that the Fed has paused hikes. That's not how deposit competition works. Deposit repricing lags rate hikes on the way up, but it *accelerates* on the way down—or in a plateau. When rates are "higher-for-longer," depositors don't stop shopping around. They actually intensify their search because they know rates aren't going higher, so they've got to lock in what they can get.

The data backs this. Interest expense grew 148% year-over-year. You need to show me quarter-over-quarter deceleration in the rate of interest expense growth to convince me that the pressure has peaked. I don't see that in the available data. In fact, what I see is TTM data through roughly Q2/Q3 2024 still showing elevated deposit costs running at nearly 2.5x the growth rate of interest income. That's not stabilization. That's ongoing compression of the spread.

Here's my real concern: You're using a reasonable macro backdrop (92% probability no recession, no aggressive rate cuts) as proof that everything's fine. But macro probability distributions don't drive earnings revisions. Actual quarterly results do. And if Q3 or Q4 earnings show that NII growth continued to decelerate quarter-over-quarter, the consensus 22% forward EPS growth estimate gets cut. Then the valuation story changes completely.

So yes, I agree NIM won't collapse. But I'm not confident it stays wide enough to support current consensus growth rates. That's not bearish; that's realistic.

## **Conservative Analyst: You're Treating Leading Indicators Like They're Already Baked Into Results**

Now to you: You're flagging rising delinquencies, record balances, and student loan payment resumption as evidence that credit stress is materializing *now*. But you're conflating the *appearance* of stress with the *materialization* of stress. There's a meaningful difference, and it matters for portfolio timing.

Yes, delinquencies are rising. But from what level? The fundamental report doesn't give me current delinquency rate trends, only the statement that they're rising. If delinquencies are rising from 0.8% to 1.0%, that's a 25 basis point move and materially different from rising from 1.5% to 2.5%. One is early stress; one is real deterioration.

Similarly, you're right that student loan resumption is a headwind to discretionary spending. But AXP's exposure to private label student lending is not their core business. Their core business is affluent consumer credit cards and business cards. Those customers have lower student loan exposure and higher capacity to absorb payment resumption without material default stress.

Your real point—that buffers compress fast in credit cycles—is completely valid. But here's where I push back: You're using that as an argument for *caution*, when the macro data suggests we're not entering a credit cycle yet. Unemployment is 3.8-3.9% (depending on the latest read). Credit cycle deterioration typically accelerates when unemployment starts *rising*, not when it's stable at historically low levels. We're not seeing labor market stress yet.

So yes, credit quality *can* deteriorate fast. But the evidence we have suggests deterioration is early-stage, not accelerating. That means there's still time for the market to re-rate the stock based on actual earnings results rather than fear of what *might* happen.
