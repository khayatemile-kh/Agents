## THE "REGULATORY TAILWIND" FANTASY: WHERE THE ARGUMENT BREAKS DOWN

The aggressive analyst argues that high rates *justify* rate case approvals because "commissioners understand utilities need rate increases to service debt at higher cost of capital." This is where the analysis tips into wishful thinking.

Let me present the actual regulatory and political reality:

**First, rate case approvals are not automatic—they are *contested*.** The absence of news on Entergy's rate cases (as noted in the macro report) doesn't mean approval is assured. It often means the cases are in procedural phases where headlines haven't yet surfaced. Rate case denial or approval delay is *catastrophic* for a company in Entergy's position because it directly impacts cash flow.

**Second, the 48% Central US power grid emergency probability that the aggressive analyst frames as an "opportunity" is actually a *risk.*** Here's why: when grid stress spikes, regulators face political pressure to hold rates down to protect consumers, not approve increases. They face a political choice: either approve capex and let rates rise (unpopular), or constrain approvals to keep rates stable (politically safe). In utility regulation, the consumer advocate almost always has a seat at the table, and consumer advocates don't argue *for* rate increases during grid emergencies—they argue for rate *constraint* while demanding reliability be maintained with existing rate bases.

**Third, "politically untenable" is not a legal guarantee.** The aggressive analyst argues it would be "politically untenable" to deny rate cases for an essential utility. But utilities face exactly this scenario regularly. Look at the historical record: Pennsylvania, Michigan, and other states have delayed or constrained utility rate approvals despite genuine operational needs. Why? Because politicians survive on constraining costs, not approving them. The narrative that grid emergency + high rates = automatic rate approval is backwards. Grid emergencies often trigger *political constraints* on rate approvals because the public sees rising rates and rising grid instability at the same time.

The forward EPS guidance of $5.10 and the 30% growth assumption *depend entirely on those rate case approvals materializing at assumed levels.* If rate case approvals are delayed, denied, or scaled back—a material probability—then that growth doesn't happen. The valuation then resets, and you're holding a leveraged utility at 27.7x trailing PE with declining growth prospects. That's a value trap.
