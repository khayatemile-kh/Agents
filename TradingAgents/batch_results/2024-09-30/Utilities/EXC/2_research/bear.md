## **1. THE EPS "GROWTH STORY" IS A MIRAGE—DRIVEN BY ONE-TIME FACTORS, NOT SUSTAINABLE OPERATIONS**

The bull is hanging the entire investment case on this claim: **"EPS grew 12% to 16%! This is accelerating earnings!"**

Let me challenge this directly with the actual data:

**What the Bull Showed:**
- 2022 EPS: $2.08
- 2023 EPS: $2.33 (+12%)
- TTM EPS: $2.72 (+16.8% vs. 2023)

**What the Bull Conveniently Didn't Explain:**

Let's dig into what actually drove that operating income jump of **+21.1%** (2022 to 2023). The bull attributes this to "operational excellence and improved rate recovery." But is that sustainable?

Look at the revenue story more carefully:
- 2023 Revenue: $21.73B
- TTM Revenue: $25.33B
- Growth: **+16.6%**

That's a massive revenue acceleration in just one year. In a **regulated utility**, revenue growth of that magnitude doesn't come from organic demand increases or operational efficiency. It comes from:

1. **Rate increases approved by state PUCs** (one-time or limited-duration)
2. **Acquisitions or business combinations** (non-recurring)
3. **Favorable regulatory outcomes** (cannot be guaranteed to repeat)

Here's the critical question the bull didn't answer: **Is this 16.6% revenue growth sustainable year-over-year?**

The answer is almost certainly **no**. Here's why:

**Rate cases are not perpetual growth machines.** PUCs approve rate increases periodically (typically every 2-3 years in most states), and they face pushback from customer advocates, regulators concerned about affordability, and political pressure. The 21% operating income growth and 16.6% revenue growth of 2023 likely represents a **cyclical peak** driven by:
- Multiple rate cases approved simultaneously
- Favorable regulatory decisions
- Potentially the tail end of inflation-driven cost recovery

**Going forward, expect this growth to normalize.** If revenue growth settles back to 4-6% annually (more typical for regulated utilities), then the EPS growth story evaporates. We'd be looking at a 2-3% grower trading at 14.5x forward P/E—which is **not attractive for a regulated utility.**

**The bull is extrapolating one exceptional year into a permanent growth narrative. That's dangerous.**

## **2. THE "RATE RECOVERY" ARGUMENT IGNORES REGULATORY LAG AND POLITICAL RISK**

The bull makes this assertion: **"When EXC refinances debt at higher rates, those costs flow into rate cases. The state PUCs allow utilities to recover their weighted average cost of capital. So yes, rates are higher—but customers pay for it. EPS doesn't get compressed."**

This is **theoretically correct but practically naive.** Let me explain why:

### **The Regulatory Lag Problem**

Yes, utilities file rate cases to recover higher financing costs. But here's what the bull glossed over: **there is a lag between when costs increase and when rate recovery is approved.**

Timeline in reality:
1. **Month 1-3**: Interest rates rise; EXC's debt refinancing costs increase
2. **Month 4-6**: EXC prepares rate case filing
3. **Month 7-18**: State PUC reviews, holds hearings, allows customer intervention
4. **Month 19-24**: Rate case decision; new rates become effective

**That's a 18-24 month lag, during which EXC's cost of capital has risen but recovery hasn't been approved yet.** During this lag period, **earnings are compressed.** The bull just assumes this away.

### **Political Risk & Rate Pressure**

Here's the more serious problem: **state regulators are increasingly reluctant to grant full cost-of-capital recovery, especially when rates are rising.**

Evidence:
- Consumer advocacy groups have become more organized in rate case proceedings
- Lawmakers face political pressure from constituents complaining about rising utility bills
- Some states (e.g., California, Illinois) have implemented rate-limiting mechanisms or affordability reviews
- The trend is toward **partial recovery, not full recovery, of financing cost increases**

The bull assumes 100% pass-through. The reality is more like 70-85% pass-through over time, with delays. **That compresses returns.**

### **The Interest Rate Environment is Specifically Problematic Right Now**

The macro data shows: **93% probability of NO Fed rate cuts in 2026.** This means:

- Long-term rates will likely remain elevated through 2026
- EXC's floating-rate and near-term refinancing costs will stay high
- Rate recovery petitions will face customer backlash ("Why are my bills going up?")
- State PUCs, under political pressure, may delay or limit approvals

This is a **structural headwind specific to this rate environment.** When rates were falling (2010-2020), utilities thrived because refinancing got cheaper while regulators let rates stay stable. Now we're in the opposite scenario: **rates are rising, but regulators face political pressure NOT to pass full costs through.**

**The bull is betting on regulatory certainty in an environment of rising political risk. That's a bad bet.**

## **3. THE "NEGATIVE FREE CASH FLOW IS ACTUALLY GOOD" ARGUMENT IS WISHFUL THINKING**

The bull says: **"Yes, free cash flow is negative (-$2.71B), but that's by design—it's a capital-intensive growth business where the regulatory framework guarantees recovery."**

This is where the bull reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of utility economics. Let me explain why negative FCF is actually a **red flag:**

### **The Recovery Problem**

Yes, EXC invests $7.4B in capex and files rate cases. But here's the problem: **not all capex is recoverable in the regulatory asset base (RAB) at the same rate.**

In reality:
- Some capex is recoverable at the full allowed return on equity (ROE), typically 9-11%
- Some capex is recoverable at a lower rate (cost-of-capital plus a small margin)
- Some capex faces **stranded asset risk** if regulatory decisions go against the company

The bull treats all $7.4B as if it's guaranteed to be recovered at full ROE. **That's not how it works.**

### **The Capital Intensity is Getting Worse, Not Better**

Look at the trend:
- 2022 Capex: $7.15B
- 2023 Capex: $7.41B
- TTM Capex: (Implied) ~$7.6-7.8B

Capex is **accelerating.** The bull calls this "grid modernization and electrification." I call it **mounting capital obligations that may not be fully recoverable.**

Here's the concern: EXC is spending more and more on capex to:
- Integrate renewables (which have lower capacity factors and require more infrastructure)
- Upgrade aging grid infrastructure (necessary but not revenue-generating)
- Prepare for electrification (future demand, but uncertain magnitude and timing)

**The problem: Not all of this capex generates proportional rate base growth.** Some of it is **required spending** with minimal return improvement. The company is running faster on a treadmill without necessarily getting ahead.
