### **FAILURE 2: The "Regulatory Offset" Is Mythical—Cost Recovery Is Already Breaking Down**

Now let me directly challenge the bull's core regulatory assumption—the claim that "higher rates support higher regulated returns on equity" and that state regulators will automatically adjust ROE upward to offset rising financing costs.

**This assumption is outdated and increasingly false.** Here's why:

**First, the political reality:** State regulators—particularly those in progressive jurisdictions where DUK operates (North Carolina, South Carolina, Florida)—face intense political pressure to *limit* rate increases. Climate activists, consumer advocates, and elected officials are increasingly vocal about "protecting" ratepayers from rising electric bills. The regulatory model is under stress.

**Second, the deteriorating track record:** Utilities across the country are reporting that rate case victories are becoming *narrower*, not broader. Regulators are:
- Approving smaller ROE increases than utilities request
- Imposing longer amortization periods for infrastructure costs
- Requiring higher equity ratios (lowering leverage ratios), which reduces returns to shareholders
- Increasing scrutiny of CapEx spending, particularly on fossil fuel retirement

**This is the opposite of the bull's narrative.** The regulatory framework is tightening, not loosening.

**Third, the timing mismatch:** Even if a rate case is won, there's a substantial lag between when costs are incurred and when rate recovery is approved. In a rapidly rising rate environment, DUK incurs high refinancing costs *today*, but doesn't get rate recovery until *next year*. In the interim, margins compress and debt-to-equity ratios deteriorate.

**The bull claims: "Utilities that reported earnings through 2024 have already begun getting ROE increases in rate cases."** Let me ask for specifics: Which utilities? By how much? And are these increases enough to offset the 23.6% jump in interest expense DUK saw in 2023 alone?

I'm skeptical. What I *do* see is utilities deploying stock offerings and asset sales to reduce leverage, which is the *opposite* of what we'd expect if regulatory cost recovery were reliable.

**Here's the critical admission:** The bull's entire case depends on regulators being *willing and able* to pass through rising financing costs to ratepayers. But the political economy is shifting. In a world where EVs are proliferating, home solar is cheaper than ever, and grid reliability is under scrutiny, regulators face pressure to *moderate* rate increases, not approve them wholesale.

**DUK is betting on regulatory assumptions that are increasingly fragile.**
