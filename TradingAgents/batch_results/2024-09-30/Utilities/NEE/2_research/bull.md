Now, here's where the bear will jump: "NEE has negative $17.77B free cash flow!" Let me decode what that actually means.

### **Understanding Utility Capital Structure**

Negative FCF in utilities is **not a red flag—it's a feature of how the sector works.** Here's the critical nuance:

- **NEE is in a heavy capex cycle.** The company is investing in:
  - Renewable energy capacity (NEER expansion)

- **The negative FCF reflects *growth investment*, not financial distress.** Compare:

## 5. THE LEVERAGE IS MANAGEABLE—AND HERE'S WHY

A 162x debt-to-equity ratio *sounds* catastrophic to equities investors. It's not. Let me explain why utilities operate this way:

### **Utilities Are Not Typical Companies**

- **Utilities operate on a cost-of-service + return model.** FPL doesn't try to maximize ROE and minimize debt like a tech company. It operates to serve customers while earning a regulatory ROE (typically 9-10%). That ROE is *guaranteed by regulation*, independent of leverage.

- **High leverage is *rational* in utilities** because debt is cheaper than equity (debt is tax-deductible, equity is not). So utilities *intentionally* lever up to equity to minimize their cost of capital.

- **Refinancing risk is minimal.** NEE's debt is investment-grade, rated by Moody's and S&P. It refinances routinely. And with the Fed cutting rates, refinancing becomes *easier and cheaper*, not harder.

- **Compare to the debt absolute numbers:** $28.7B in revenue supports $14.59B in EBITDA. Debt service (interest + principal) is easily covered. The leverage ratio, while high in relative terms, is appropriate for a $175B market cap utility with stable, predictable cash flows.

**Bottom line:** The 162x ratio is a structural feature of the utility business model, not a sign of financial distress.
