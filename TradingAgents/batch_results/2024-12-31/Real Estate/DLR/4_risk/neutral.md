## FIRST, THE AGGRESSIVE ANALYST'S CRITICAL ERROR: MISAPPLYING OPERATING COMPANY VALUATION FRAMEWORKS TO A REIT

I need to be direct: the Aggressive Analyst has built their entire case on a foundational mistake that undermines nearly every valuation conclusion they've drawn. They compare DLR's 92x trailing P/E to Amazon at 50x and Apple at 30x as if this proves fantasy-level overvaluation. This is analytically sloppy, and frankly, it's the kind of error that gets traders blown up.

Here's why this matters: REITs are legally required to distribute 90% of taxable income to shareholders. This doesn't mean they're earning less—it means the *reported earnings* are artificially depressed by massive non-cash charges like depreciation and amortization. When the Aggressive Analyst looks at DLR's net income of $602 million and calculates a P/E of 92x, they're working with a denominator that's already been hammered by accounting rules that don't apply to operating companies.

The Conservative Analyst correctly identified this, but didn't go far enough in quantifying the distortion. Let me do the math: DLR's gross profit in 2024 was $3.0 billion on $5.6 billion in revenue. That's a 53.6% gross margin. Operating income was $757 million with a 13.6% operating margin. These are solid numbers for a capital-intensive business. But when you add back depreciation and amortization of $1.8 billion—non-cash charges—the real economic earnings become substantially larger than the reported net income of $602 million.

So here's the honest assessment: comparing DLR's trailing P/E to tech companies is intellectually dishonest. The relevant REIT metrics are price-to-FFO (funds from operations) and dividend yield relative to peers. We don't have FFO in the data provided, but we *do* know that DLR's 2.59% dividend yield is low for a REIT, which suggests the market is indeed pricing in growth expectations. That's different from "fantasy valuation"—it's a choice to hold a lower-yielding REIT because you believe in the growth story.

But here's where I push back on the Conservative Analyst: acknowledging that the P/E comparison is flawed doesn't mean the valuation is cheap. It means we need to evaluate it on the correct framework. A 2.59% yield for a REIT in a higher-rate environment *is* on the lower end. Historically, data center REITs have traded with yields in the 3-4% range. If we normalize DLR to a 3.5% yield using current cash flows, the implied valuation is approximately 15-20% lower than current levels. That's material, but it's not the 30-40% crash the Aggressive Analyst is implying.

The strong cash position ($3.9B, up 138% YoY) and declining net debt ($12.8B from $15.8B) support this reading.

Here's my neutral assessment: **both scenarios are plausible, and the data doesn't definitively rule out either one.** The key differentiator is Q4 earnings guidance. If management provides forward guidance showing EBITDA re-acceleration in 2025, Scenario Two becomes more likely. If they maintain cautious guidance, Scenario One gains weight.

But here's what separates a neutral analyst from an extreme one: I don't make a binary bet on either scenario before the information arrives. The Aggressive Analyst is essentially betting that Scenario One is true *right now* and recommending action based on that assumption. The Conservative Analyst is implicitly betting on Scenario Two without acknowledging the genuine risk if it's wrong.

A balanced approach says: **wait for the earnings data to clarify which scenario is playing out, then act decisively.** That's not "fence-sitting"—it's risk management with discipline.
