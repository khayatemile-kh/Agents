## The Covenant Risk Is Worse Than the Aggressive Analyst Admits

The aggressive analyst mentions the 7.57x Debt/EBITDA ratio and then pivots to dismiss it because CoStar has $4.68B in cash. But this dismissal reveals a critical misunderstanding of how covenant violations actually work.

### Here's What Typically Happens

Most institutional debt covenants have two triggers:

1. **Leverage ratio threshold** (Debt/EBITDA <5.0x or <6.0x)
2. **Interest coverage ratio** (EBITDA/Interest Expense >2.5x or >3.0x)

CoStar's Debt/EBITDA at 7.57x is already *above* most standard covenant thresholds. This means:

**Option A:** The debt already includes covenant waivers or modified terms that allow higher leverage (suggesting the lenders were already concerned about distress)

**Option B:** The covenant calculations use adjusted EBITDA (add-backs for one-time items), meaning the reported 7.57x might actually be lower on an "adjusted" basis

**Option C:** CoStar is in material breach and hasn't disclosed it yet (which would be a major problem)

The cash reserves don't matter here because covenants are measured on *flow metrics* (EBITDA), not stock metrics (cash on hand). A company with $10B in cash can still breach debt covenants if EBITDA falls below thresholds.

And here's where it gets serious: **if EBITDA continues to deteriorate in Q4 2024 or Q1 2025, covenant breach becomes a near-certainty.** At that point, lenders have the right to accelerate the debt, impose restrictions on capital allocation, or force asset sales. That's not a 15% downside—that's a **binary event** that destroys equity value.
