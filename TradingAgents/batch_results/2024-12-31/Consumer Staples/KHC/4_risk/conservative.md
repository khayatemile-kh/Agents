## YES, THE IMPAIRMENTS ARE REAL—BUT THEY'RE ALSO PARTIALLY RESOLVED

The aggressive analyst correctly identifies that three years of cumulative $5.2 billion in impairment charges is a serious red flag. I agree entirely. But here's what they're *not* saying: **impairment charges, by definition, are backward-looking admissions of past overpayment. They are not predictive of future deterioration.**

Think about what an impairment actually means. Management paid $X for an asset, believed it was worth $X, but now admits it's worth $Y (where Y < X). The write-down is painful, but it serves a critical function: **it resets the asset base to a realistic value.** 

This is crucial. Once you've taken the hit, you're not taking it again on the same asset. The $3.67 billion impairment in 2024 doesn't mean the brands are worth $3.67 billion less *next year*—it means management finally admitted what they should have admitted years ago.

Here's the uncomfortable part for the aggressive case: **if KHC has now fully impaired the brands they acquired at peak valuation, the *downside risk has been partially exhausted*.** The next chapter isn't "more impairments"—it's either "stabilization at lower brand values" or "operational improvement within the already-written-down asset base."

The aggressive analyst is extrapolating a *pattern* into the future. But patterns break when the underlying cause is resolved. Once you've admitted the mistake, you can't make the same mistake twice on the same asset.

**Gross profit actually *increased* despite revenue decline.** This is not the signature of demand destruction—this is the signature of **successful pricing and cost management offsetting volume pressure.**

The aggressive analyst conflates "not growing" with "dying." These are not the same thing. A mature packaged food company that maintains profitability while managing through a consumer shift toward healthier alternatives is not the same as a company in secular collapse.
