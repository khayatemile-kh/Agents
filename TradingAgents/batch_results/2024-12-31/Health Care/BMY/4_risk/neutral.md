The conservative analyst says some of it is Celgene integration related and should reverse.

I'm going to challenge both of you here, because you're both missing the real story embedded in those numbers.

First, yes, Celgene integration is a real factor. Celgene has lower gross margins than Bristol-Myers historically—Celgene's gross margin sits around 65 to 70 percent, while Bristol-Myers historical business was in the high 70s. So when you acquire Celgene in 2019 and consolidate the financial statements, margin compression is automatic and expected. That's not a sign of trouble; it's a sign of acquisition math.

But here's the thing: we're now *five years* into that integration, not one year. If margins were going to stabilize post-integration, they should have stabilized by now. The fact that we're seeing *continued* compression in 2024—from 74 percent in 2023 to 71 percent in 2024—suggests something beyond simple integration is at play.

What could that be? Aggressive analyst says biosimilar pressure and pricing erosion. That's plausible. But I need to push back on the magnitude. Biosimilar pressure is real, but it typically erodes margins by 200 to 300 basis points over a 3 to 5 year period, not 690 basis points in two years. The numbers don't quite add up.

Here's what I actually think is happening: there's a mix effect at play that neither of you is adequately accounting for. When you have a large legacy business like Eliquis or Opdivo facing patent cliffs and biosimilar competition, and those drugs were historically high-margin products, you lose that margin contribution. The revenue you replace them with—if you replace them at all—might be lower margin oncology combinations or newer therapies with different margin profiles. That's not a sign that the entire business model is broken. It's a sign that the *portfolio composition* is changing.

So here's my position: the margin compression is partially structural, partially mix-related, and partially integration-related. I'd estimate maybe 200 to 250 basis points of the compression is *truly* concerning. The rest is explainable and potentially reversible if the pipeline performs or if they successfully divest lower-margin assets. That's not the "evidence of broken economics" the aggressive analyst claims, but it's also not the "temporary integration headwind" the conservative analyst minimizes it as.
