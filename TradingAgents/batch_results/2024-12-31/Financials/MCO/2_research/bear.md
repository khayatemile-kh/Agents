
Bear Analyst: # **THE BEAR CASE FOR MCO: Why This Rally Is Built on Fragile Foundations**

---

## **Opening Statement**

Look, I appreciate the bull's comprehensive case. The fundamentals *do* look solid on the surface. But here's what concerns me: **We're buying a stock at a 29.6x P/E multiple that's priced for perfection—and perfection is precisely what MCO may not be able to deliver in 2025.** 

The bull is extrapolating one exceptional year of growth and margin expansion into an indefinite future, overlooking cyclicality, valuation risk, and the fragile macroeconomic assumptions underlying MCO's current momentum. Let me walk through why I think the bears have a compelling case here.

---

## **1. THE EARNINGS ACCELERATION IS A CYCLICAL ANOMALY, NOT A STRUCTURAL SHIFT**

The bull celebrates 28.1% net income growth and 20% revenue growth as evidence of a "pricing-power machine." I see it differently: **this is the peak of a credit cycle, and the bull is extrapolating backward from one exceptional year.**

Here's the critical context the bull glosses over:

**2024 was an exceptional year for credit activity because:**
- Corporate refinancing demand surged as companies locked in rates before further uncertainty
- M&A activity rebounded post-2023 malaise, driving rating-fee revenue
- Capital markets were buoyant, increasing debt issuance activity
- Credit volatility expanded, increasing demand for analytics

But let's look at the **growth trajectory more carefully:**

| Year | Revenue | YoY Growth | Net Income | YoY Growth |
|------|---------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| 2022 | $5,468M | — | $1,374M | — |
| 2023 | $5,916M | **+8.2%** | $1,608M | **+17.0%** |
| 2024 | $7,088M | **+19.8%** | $2,059M | **+28.1%** |

The bull wants me to believe this 20%+ growth rate is sustainable. **But notice what happened:** 2023 was only 8.2% growth—that's a *slowdown* from historical mid-teens norms. Then 2024 spiked to nearly 20% on the back of pent-up refinancing and a one-time surge in capital markets activity.

**This doesn't look like structural acceleration. It looks like normalization followed by cyclical bounce.**

When I model 2025 conservatively assuming:
- Refinancing demand normalizes (fewer rate-lock urgencies)
- M&A activity stabilizes at moderate levels
- Capital markets remain active but not frothy
- Macro uncertainty *actually materializes* into economic slowdown

...I get something closer to **10-12% revenue growth**, not 20%. And if margins compress even modestly from the 49.5% operating margin peak (which typically happens as growth decelerates), earnings growth could fall to **single digits to mid-teens**.

The bull's forward EPS estimate of $18.93 assumes roughly 29% earnings growth. **That's baking in 2024's cyclical peak as permanent.** That's dangerous.

## **2. THE VALUATION IS NOT "REASONABLE"—IT'S STRETCHED RELATIVE TO NORMALIZED EARNINGS**

The bull argues that 24.6x forward P/E is "defensible" for a business with 20% growth and 35% FCF margins. Let me challenge that directly.

**First, let's stress-test the forward estimates:**

If MCO earnings growth moderates to 10-12% (which is reasonable as 2024's cyclical tailwinds fade), the forward P/E isn't 24.6x—it's higher, because earnings won't hit the bull's optimistic $18.93 target. 

Let's say normalized forward EPS is closer to $17.50 (15% growth to mid-cycle), not $18.93:
- **Implied P/E: 26.7x**

That's for a company with:
- Cyclical revenue tied to capital markets and credit issuance
- No pricing power in a slowdown (customers can stretch ratings analysis internally or reduce subscription services)
- Regulatory pressure (post-2008, rating agencies face constant scrutiny about conflict of interest)

**For comparison:**
- S&P 500 trades at ~19.5x forward P/E
- Microsoft (genuinely structural 20%+ growth, AI tailwinds) trades at ~28-30x
- Berkshire Hathaway (predictable earnings, fortress balance sheet) trades at ~18-20x

MCO at 26-27x for *cyclical* 10-12% growth is **not a bargain.** It's priced for sustained 20%+ growth that I don't believe is achievable.

**The bull says "MCO has traded at 30-32x historically."** That's not a defense—that's evidence that MCO has been *expensive* historically, and investors who bought at 30x P/E in the past have faced multiple compression. We should learn from that, not repeat it.

## **3. THE "COUNTERCYCLICAL" NARRATIVE IS BACKWARDS**

The bull argues that trade war uncertainty will drive demand for Moody's ratings and analytics. **I think this is precisely backward, and here's why:**

**During corporate credit stress, what actually happens:**

1. **Capital markets *contract*, not expand.** When uncertainty rises and credit spreads widen, companies *reduce* debt issuance. They hunker down. M&A goes quiet. IPO windows close.

2. **Rating-fee revenue declines**, because fewer ratings are issued. Yes, existing ratings get reviewed, but one-time fees for new ratings (a major revenue driver) dry up.

3. **Investors do want analytics during stress**, so *some* demand increases. But this is typically offset by the loss of transaction-based revenue.

The bull cites the 2024 acceleration as proof: "The 2024 revenue acceleration (19.8% growth, up from 8.2% in 2023) wasn't random. It reflects real demand for credit intelligence."

**Correct—but 2024 was a capital markets *recovery*, not a stress scenario.** Refinancing exploded. M&A returned. Credit was cheap. Companies were *issuing* debt, not avoiding it.

Now the bull says: "Heading into 2025 with *increased* trade uncertainty, that demand should remain elevated or even accelerate."

**That's exactly backward.** If trade war actually materializes and corporations face real credit stress, the capital markets won't *accelerate*—they'll freeze. We saw this in 2020 (COVID), 2018-2019 (trade war fears), and 2008. Rating agencies' revenues get hammered.

The bull's scenario only works if uncertainty creates *perceived* risk without actual economic damage. But that's not what trade war looks like. It looks like slower growth, higher defaults, and frozen capital markets.

**Total debt: $7.746 billion**  
**EBITDA: $3.469 billion**  
**Gross Debt-to-EBITDA: 2.23x**

MCO is pursuing aggressive capital allocation (buybacks of $1.38B while carrying $7.7B in debt) during what appears to be the *peak* of a credit cycle.
