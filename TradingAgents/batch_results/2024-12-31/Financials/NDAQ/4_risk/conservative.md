## THE "INTEGRATION STORY" IS ACTUALLY A RED FLAG, NOT A BULL SIGNAL

Let me frame this another way: if NDAQ had maintained its 2022 share count (508.4 million shares) with 2024's net income of $1.117 billion, diluted EPS would be $2.20, not $1.93. That's a 12.3% higher earnings-per-share outcome with the exact same operational performance. The company has engaged in M&A that has shrunk per-share value by over 12% despite revenue acceleration.

Now, the aggressive analyst says this is "completely normal" and "bullish if management is executing disciplined M&A." But here's the uncomfortable truth: **we cannot yet validate whether this M&A was disciplined or not.** The integration costs are still flowing through the income statement. We don't know if the acquired businesses will generate the return-on-invested-capital (ROIC) that management promised. We're betting on a favorable outcome that hasn't materialized yet.

This is exactly the kind of assumption that creates portfolio blow-ups. We're assuming management knows what it's doing. But what if those acquired businesses underperform? ## THE VALUATION ARGUMENT IS CIRCULAR AND IGNORES FORWARD RISKS

The aggressive analyst points to a forward P/E of 20.3x and calls it a "bargain." Let me examine that claim more carefully, because it's foundational to their entire thesis.

NDAQ is trading at 20.3x forward earnings of $4.68 per share. The analyst claims this is cheap relative to 22% revenue growth and 33.2% EBITDA margins. But here's the critical question: **where is that forward EPS of $4.68 coming from, and what assumptions underpin it?**

If we trace backward from the TTM EPS of $3.43 and assume 5-7% annual growth as the aggressive analyst optimistically suggests, we get to roughly $3.60-$3.67 for 2025 EPS. That's dramatically lower than the $4.68 forward estimate. The $4.68 number implies either a return to historical EPS levels (which would require share count to stabilize and margin expansion to occur simultaneously) or a material acceleration in earnings growth from current levels.

Here's the problem: **we don't have visibility into either of those outcomes.** We're extrapolating from a period of heavy acquisition activity and integration costs, assuming both will normalize favorably. That's not valuation analysis—that's faith-based investing.

Compare this to the PEG ratio of 1.54, which the aggressive analyst cites as proof of undervaluation. A PEG above 1.0 actually suggests the stock is trading at a premium to its growth rate, not a discount. The analyst is using this metric backward. A stock with a PEG of 1.54 means investors are paying $1.54 for every $1.00 of annual growth—exactly the opposite of a bargain.

More critically, that PEG calculation assumes forward growth rates will materialize and that those growth rates are sustainable. Given the margin compression, the EPS decline, and the uncertainty around acquisition integration, we should be applying a higher discount rate to future earnings, not a lower one. The PEG should be trending toward 1.8-2.0 for a stock with this much execution risk, not 1.54.

The analyst is promising us margin expansion in 2026, but the 2024 data suggests the margin trajectory is moving in the wrong direction.
