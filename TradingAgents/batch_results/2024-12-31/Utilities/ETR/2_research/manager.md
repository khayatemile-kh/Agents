**Recommendation**: Underweight

**Rationale**: Both sides agree on the facts, they just disagree on interpretation, and when I weigh the concrete numbers against the narrative, the bear case is more grounded in what's actually happening on the ground right now versus what the bull is hoping happens in the future.

The bull thesis rests on three things: (1) AI/data-center demand tailwinds that are speculative and probabilistic (a 45% chance of grid emergencies isn't a certainty, it's a coin-flip risk scenario being spun as a positive catalyst), (2) the argument that EBITDA/operating income growth (+2-3%) matters more than the 55% net income collapse, and (3) a valuation reset story built entirely on forward EPS estimates ($5.10, +30%) that haven't been earned yet.

The bear case, in contrast, is built on cash flow and balance sheet facts that are already in the reported numbers, not projections: FCF has been negative and worsening for three straight years (-$230M → -$340M → -$610M), the dividend ($1.71B) is not covered by operating cash flow after capex, and the shortfall is being plugged with debt issuance (+$2.67B in 2024 alone, debt up 10.1% YoY). That's a real structural funding gap, not an accounting quirk. Debt-to-EBITDA (5.74x) and interest coverage (4.4x) are the weakest in the utility peer set the bear cited (DUK, SO, AEP, NEE all show better coverage and lower leverage), which undercuts the bull's "this is just how utilities work" defense — peers are managing similar capex cycles with materially healthier balance sheets.

The bull's rebuttal that higher interest expense is "temporary" and will reverse with Fed cuts doesn't hold up to the bear's math: only the ~$2.67B of new 2024 debt is exposed to near-term rate resets, the rest is long-dated and locked in, so realistic savings are more like $25-35M, not enough to offset a $610M+ FCF hole or a debt stock still growing. Meanwhile net income fell 55% in the same year opex/EBITDA metrics improved modestly — that gap is being driven by real, cash-effective interest costs, not just non-cash items.

I'm also skeptical of leaning on forward P/E (21.27x) and forward EPS ($5.10) as the valuation anchor when trailing numbers show deteriorating profitability and the company is depending on continued heavy capex (which the bear correctly notes is rising faster than operating cash flow) to hit those targets. The data-center demand thesis is real directionally for the sector, but it's a multi-year regulatory and buildout story, not something that fixes a negative-FCF, rising-leverage balance sheet in the next 12 months.

Net: the bear's argument that this is a company funding its dividend and growth capex through increasing debt issuance, with the weakest coverage ratios in its peer group and a P/E that isn't supported by current cash generation, is the stronger, better-evidenced case for now. It's not a slam-dunk sell — the underlying regulated-utility model and demand tailwinds are legitimate assets — but the near-term financial deterioration is real and unresolved, which argues for reducing exposure rather than holding or adding.
