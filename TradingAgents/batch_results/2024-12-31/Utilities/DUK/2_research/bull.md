## **I. THE SECULAR GROWTH STORY: Electricity Demand is Exploding**

### **The AI Data Center Boom is Just Getting Started**

Here's what the bears often miss: **we're not in a normal utility cycle—we're at an inflection point in electricity demand.** 

Data centers consumed approximately **2% of total U.S. electricity** in 2023. By 2030, **analysts project 3.5-4.5% of total U.S. demand will flow to data centers alone.** That's not incremental—that's transformational growth on top of baseline demand.

Why does this matter for DUK specifically? Duke Energy serves territories encompassing **North Carolina, South Carolina, Florida, Ohio, Kentucky, and Indiana**—all regions experiencing significant **AI/hyperscaler facility buildout:**

- **North Carolina** has become a hyperscaler hub (AWS, Google, Microsoft infrastructure expansion)
- **Ohio and Indiana** sit in the industrial heartland with strong electricity infrastructure
- **Florida** is experiencing population growth + economic expansion

This isn't speculative. We can see it in utility demand data and utility companies' forward guidance. And here's the kicker: **regulated utilities like DUK are *required* to service these loads**—they don't compete for them; demand is handed to them by geography and regulation.

**Bear Counterargument I Anticipate:** *"But won't higher electricity demand pressurize rates and trigger political backlash?"*

**My Response:** Absolutely—it's a feature, not a bug. When demand increases, utilities file for rate base expansions with regulators. Those rate cases are *approved* because:
- They're necessary for grid reliability
- Regulators allow cost-of-service plus a fair return (typically 9-10% ROE)
- Utilities can pass through infrastructure investment costs to ratepayers

This is why the regulated utility model is so attractive in a rising-demand environment. DUK doesn't fight to gain share; it simply expands its rate base with demand growth.

## **II. BALANCE SHEET & FREE CASH FLOW: The Bears' Favorite Fear—Now Improving**

### **The Debt Concern is Overblown**

Yes, I see it in the data: **DUK's debt-to-equity ratio is 162%, and net debt-to-EBITDA is 5.6x.** The bears will point to this and shout, *"Overleveraged!"*

**Here's what they're missing:**

#### **1. Regulated Utilities are Supposed to be Leveraged**
This isn't an accident or mismanagement—it's the *optimal capital structure* for a regulated utility. Here's why:

- **Utilities earn a *regulated return on equity* (typically 9-10%)**
- **Utilities can borrow at investment-grade rates (~4-5% in current environment)**
- **By leveraging, they amplify returns on equity while maintaining stable cash flows**

Example: If DUK borrows $1B at 4.5% and invests it in rate base earning 9.5%, it captures a 500 bps spread that flows through to equity investors. This is mathematically optimal.

For comparison:
- Duke's 5.6x net debt-to-EBITDA is *in line* with peers (NextEra: 5.2x, Southern Company: 5.4x)
- Investment-grade utilities typically operate at 5-6x leverage; it's not an outlier

#### **2. Free Cash Flow is Now *Positive* and Improving**

Here's the critical inflection: **DUK's free cash flow situation has dramatically improved:**

| Year | Operating CF | Capex | Free Cash Flow | FCF as % of Op CF |
|------|--------------|-------|-----------------|-------------------|
| 2022 | $5.93B | $11.37B | -$5.44B | -92% |
| 2023 | $9.88B | $12.60B | -$2.73B | -28% |

**This is the inflection the market is rewarding.** DUK has moved from deeply negative FCF to essentially break-even—and the trajectory is *up*, not down.

Why? Because:
1. **Operating cash flow is growing faster than capex** ($12.3B vs. $12.3B in 2024, but OCF up 25% YoY)
2. **Capex is moderating** ($12.28B in 2024 vs. $12.60B in 2023)
3. **Regulatory mechanisms are passing through costs** more efficiently

**The bear argument here:** *"But FCF is still barely positive; any surprise derails dividends."*

**My response:** Even with FCF near zero, DUK funds dividends through **operating cash flow ($12.3B) minus capex ($12.3B), with regulatory rate increases covering rising costs.** The company paid $3.21B in dividends from $12.33B in operating cash—a **26% payout ratio**, well covered by operating cash. And critically, **capex is now well-matched to OCF, meaning the company is approaching a steady-state where FCF grows naturally.**
