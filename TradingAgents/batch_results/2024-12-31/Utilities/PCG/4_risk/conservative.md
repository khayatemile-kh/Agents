## The Operating Leverage Story Masks a Deeper Problem

Yes, operating income grew 66.6% on flat revenue. The Aggressive Analyst presents this as evidence of disciplined execution. I see it differently: it's a one-year comparison against an artificially depressed baseline. Let me explain why this matters.

When a company emerges from bankruptcy—and PCG did in 2020—the immediate post-emergence years often show dramatic operating improvements as the company clears legacy restructuring costs, improves asset utilization, and normalizes its expense base. That's exactly what we're seeing here. The question isn't whether the company improved operationally in 2024. The question is whether that 66% operating income growth is *sustainable* or *mean-reverting*.

Here's the critical data point they glossed over: revenue was essentially flat year-over-year at $24.42B vs $24.43B. Not growing. Flat. For a utility that supposedly has tailwinds from California's green energy transition, grid modernization, and infrastructure investment, that's deeply concerning. If the company had genuine long-term growth drivers—as the bullish thesis suggests—we should see revenue expansion, not stagnation.

The operating leverage story only works if revenue starts growing again. But there's no evidence in the data that's happening. What we're actually witnessing is cost-cutting and operating expense management running its course. When you've squeezed out 17.3% in operating expense reductions, you're approaching the limit of what operational efficiency can deliver. The next phase of growth *has* to come from revenue expansion, not expense reduction.

And therein lies the risk: if revenue remains flat in 2025, operating income growth will decelerate sharply. The market will reprice once it realizes the efficiency gains are temporary, not structural. The Aggressive Analyst is buying into a narrative that assumes cost improvements continue indefinitely. They don't.

## The Equity Rebuilding Story Is Actually a Red Flag Dressed as a Tailwind

Let me address the 20.5% equity growth and the 44.3% retained earnings improvement directly, because this is where I think they're genuinely misreading the balance sheet dynamics.

Yes, equity grew and retained earnings improved. But notice what *actually* happened: the company moved from a -$7.54B retained earnings deficit to a -$2.97B deficit. That's still *negative*. The company is still destroying shareholder capital in a cumulative sense. We're watching the destruction rate slow, not the destruction stop.

More importantly, look at what drove the equity growth: it wasn't retained earnings improvement alone. Stockholders' equity grew 20.5%, but common stock equity grew only 14.1%. The delta tells a story. The company has been issuing equity to shore up the balance sheet. That's dilution. And in a regulated utility context, equity issuance means the company is seeking to maintain its capital structure ratios to satisfy regulatory requirements and credit ratings—not because the business is generating sufficient capital on its own.

This is a critical distinction the bullish case misses. A truly strong company rebuilds equity through retained earnings. A company under financial stress rebuilds equity through dilutive equity issuance. PCG is doing both, which suggests the balance sheet recovery is less organic than advertised.

And here's the real problem: if the company needs to continue issuing equity to maintain regulatory capital structure requirements, that's an ongoing *headwind* to per-share metrics. Every dollar of equity raised through secondary issuance dilutes existing shareholders, even if it technically strengthens the consolidated balance sheet.

## The 5.89x Net Debt-to-EBITDA Ratio Is Not a "Near-Term" Concern—It's a Structural One

The Aggressive Analyst suggests that Net Debt/EBITDA at 5.89x will "improve every quarter" as EBITDA grows and debt shrinks. They're assuming forward momentum continues. I'm looking at the historical data, and I see something different.

Let's examine the debt trajectory in the raw numbers: Total debt rose from $57.73B in 2023 to $58.34B in 2024. That's an increase, not a decrease. Long-term debt grew from $50.98B to $53.57B. The only reason they can point to current debt falling 31.5% is because of a temporary working capital improvement and near-term refinancing management. That's not deleveraging—that's timing.

In reality, PCG is *adding* debt in absolute terms while trying to grow EBITDA to improve the leverage ratio. But here's where the circular reasoning breaks down: EBITDA growth of 31% in 2024 came largely from operational efficiency and cost cuts, not from revenue expansion. If revenue growth doesn't materialize—and we've just established it's flat year-over-year—that EBITDA growth rate will decelerate sharply in 2025 and beyond.

When that happens, the company faces a choice: either accept rising leverage ratios, or cut capex and operational investments to maintain deleveraging targets. In a regulated utility, cutting capex means underfunding grid modernization and wildfire resilience infrastructure—which invites regulatory and political pressure, higher insurance costs, and reputational damage.

The 5.89x leverage ratio is not a temporary condition on its way to improvement. It's a structural constraint that will pressurize earnings for years. And the market knows this—which is why the stock trades at only 8.23x forward earnings despite the operational improvements. The market is discounting the leverage risk appropriately.

## The "Rising Rates Support Higher Regulatory ROE" Argument Misses the Refinancing Reality

The Aggressive Analyst argues that rising interest rates will push California regulators to increase the allowed ROE, which supports earnings. Technically, this is true. But they've missed the timing and magnitude of the refinancing headwind.

Yes, future allowed ROE assumptions may rise. But PCG's current debt burden was issued at lower rates over the past several years. As that debt matures and needs refinancing in a 4-5% interest rate environment, the company's actual cost of capital *immediately* rises, while the regulatory adjustment to allowed ROE lags by 1-2 years in the typical CPUC rate case cycle.

This creates a timing mismatch where near-term profitability gets pressured before the regulatory benefit arrives. And given PCG's elevated leverage, this pressure matters. If interest expense rises materially in 2025 while operating income plateaus and EBITDA growth decelerates, net income could actually decline year-over-year despite the regulatory ROE adjustment that comes later.

The market is currently trading PCG at a multiple that assumes a period of earnings pressure during refinancing. When that pressure manifests in Q1 or Q2 2025 earnings reports, the stock is likely to reprice downward—exactly the opposite of the reversal the bullish thesis expects.
