## PART 3: THE CAPITAL STRUCTURE ISN'T A DEATH TRAP—IT'S NORMAL FOR UTILITIES

### $29B in Debt Sounds Scary Until You Understand the Business Model

Let's address the elephant in the room: **AES has $29.02 billion in total debt**, which creates a debt-to-equity ratio of 256.9%. The bear will wave this around like a red flag.

**But here's what they're ignoring:**

1. **Utilities are supposed to be leveraged.** They have:
   - Regulated rate bases that guarantee returns
   - Contracted cash flows (PPAs—Power Purchase Agreements)
   - Stable, predictable revenues
   - Long asset lives supporting long-term debt issuance

2. **The debt-to-equity metric improved 77% in one year** (from 1,079% in 2023 to 257% in 2024) because equity surged from $2.49B to $3.64B on the back of earnings recovery. **The company is deleveraging through retained earnings.**

3. **Debt-to-EBITDA is the right metric for utilities:**
   - 2024 Total Debt: $29.02B
   - 2024 Normalized EBITDA: $3.61B
   - **Debt-to-EBITDA: 8.04x**

Now, 8.0x sounds elevated—and I'll grant the bear that point. But for a diversified utility with global assets, regulated revenues, and contracted PPAs, this is **within the normal range** (typically 6-9x for the sector).

**More importantly:** As EBITDA grows and debt is deleveraged through retained earnings (as it is now), this ratio will compress.

### The Bear Will Argue: "Rising rates mean refinancing costs explode"

**My response:** 
- AES's **weighted average cost of debt** remains manageable due to its strong credit profile
- The company has **well-laddered maturities**—it doesn't refinance everything at once

- If rates eventually decline (a high-probability scenario), AES becomes a clear beneficiary

The fear of refinancing is real—but it's already **priced into the 5.55x P/E multiple.** The stock isn't cheap *despite* the debt; it's cheap *partly because of* debt-related fears that are overblown.
