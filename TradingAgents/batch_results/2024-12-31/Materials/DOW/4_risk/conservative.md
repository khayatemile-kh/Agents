## THE AGGRESSIVE CASE CONFUSES TECHNICAL SIGNALS WITH FUNDAMENTAL RUNWAY

Let me walk through this carefully because this is where the distinction between trader conviction and fiduciary responsibility matters.

**DOW's negative FCF of -$151 million in 2024 occurred on the back of capital expenditures that jumped 23.7% to $3.07 billion.** Now, the aggressive analyst frames this as reckless—"a company betting on recovery it can't afford." But let me offer a different interpretation: **this is capex discipline in a down cycle.** 

Think about this: if DOW management believed the cycle had permanently turned, they would slash capex to preserve cash. Instead, they're maintaining investment despite the downturn. That signals either (a) management confidence in recovery, or (b) strategic capex commitments locked in by prior contracts. Either way, it's not the behavior of a company in "slow-motion liquidity squeeze."

Here's the concrete reality: **Operating cash flow of $2.91 billion still covered the $3.07 billion capex budget by 94.8%.** The negative FCF came from capex *slightly* exceeding OCF, not from operating cash flow collapsing into zero. That's materially different from a company hemorrhaging cash.

The aggressive analyst wants to frame this as proof DOW is "funding dividends by balance sheet depletion." Let me check that math: dividends and buybacks totaled $2.46 billion. Operating cash flow was $2.91 billion. **OCF alone covered shareholder distributions at 118% of payout.** The capex overage created the negative FCF—not the dividend.

So here's my question: **is it really distress if a company is using OCF to fund both capex and dividends, with capex temporarily exceeding cash generation due to strategic investments?** Or is that actually a normal capital cycle that the aggressive analyst has reframed as catastrophe?
