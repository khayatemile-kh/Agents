## The Capex Debate: Both Are Right, But Drawing Wrong Conclusions

Let me start here because this is foundational to the whole thesis.

The aggressive analyst says capex compression from 12.2% to 8.9% is "starvation." The conservative analyst says $505M in absolute capex is "healthy maturation." Here's the problem with both framings: they're treating this as binary when it's actually a timing question.

**What the data actually tells us:** Yes, Equifax is spending $505M on capex, which is substantial. But the aggressive analyst has a legitimate point that the *percentage of revenue* compression is material—a 330 basis point swing. That's not noise. It's not normal maturation either; it's a deliberate choice by management to prioritize FCF over growth investment.

Here's where I diverge from the aggressive case: the assumption that this *must* normalize back to 12.2% by Q1 earnings is speculative. It could normalize to 10-11%. It could stay at 9-10%. Or—and this is the critical possibility both analysts glossed over—management could have genuinely optimized the capex profile through cloud migration, outsourced infrastructure, and vendor consolidation. For a data business moving to cloud-based architecture, you might *structurally* need less capex going forward than you did three years ago.

But here's where I diverge from the conservative case: blindly assuming capex will stay at 8.9% going forward is equally risky. If EFX's AI infrastructure buildout requires an acceleration to 11-12% capex in 2025 (which is plausible if the company is trying to compete with newer, AI-native competitors), then FCF guidance could crater exactly as the aggressive analyst predicts. That's not hysteria; that's a legitimate risk that the conservative analyst is underweighting.

**The neutral takeaway:** The capex risk is real but *not* guaranteed. The prudent approach is to trim exposure *modestly* (not aggressively) into any near-term strength, while remaining positioned to re-establish if capex guidance comes in benign. This isn't hiding; it's hedging uncertainty.

## The Leverage Story: The Conservative Analyst Gets the Math Right, But Misses the Tail Risk

Okay, here's where the conservative analyst makes a strong mechanical argument that I actually agree with more than I do with the aggressive case—but for different reasons than the conservative analyst thinks.

**The mechanical case is solid:** $687.7M due within 12 months against $1.32B in operating cash flow is absolutely manageable. The company doesn't need capital markets. The current portion of debt is *lower* than 2023. Net debt is declining. The math doesn't suggest imminent refinancing distress.

I agree with all of that. But the conservative analyst then commits a logical error: because the math works in a benign scenario, they imply the leverage risk is "priced in" and "already reflected." This is where they're wrong.

Here's the actual risk that the aggressive analyst correctly identified: leverage risk isn't measured by ability to service debt in benign scenarios. It's measured by what happens when *unexpected* events occur. And EFX is levered enough that unexpected events—whether from earnings misses, credit spread shocks, or broader macro deterioration—would have outsized negative impact compared to lower-levered peers like EXPN.

The aggressive analyst's point about spreads widening 50-75 bps isn't "speculative." It's pointing to a real tail risk. If HY spreads widen from current levels (~330 bps or so) to 400+ bps—which happened repeatedly in 2018, 2020, and 2022—EFX's refinancing costs do materially increase. For a $687.7M maturity, that could easily add $15-20M in annual interest expense.

**But here's the critical nuance the aggressive analyst missed:** the question isn't whether spreads *could* widen. They could. The question is whether *now* is the right time to de-risk that possibility, or whether the compensation for the risk is already adequate.

Let me address this directly: EFX trades at forward P/E of 16.9x while lower-levered EXPN trades at similar multiples. Is that inadequate compensation for leverage? Actually, maybe not. Here's why: if markets were truly complacent about leverage risk in a 8% recession probability environment, we'd expect EFX to trade at a *tighter* multiple to EXPN, not a similar multiple. The fact that EFX is trading at near-parity multiples despite higher leverage suggests the market is already discounting some risk premium.

**The neutral takeaway:** Leverage is a real concern, but it's not as hidden or underpriced as the aggressive analyst claims. The appropriate response is *controlled* de-risking through selective trimming, not panic selling. The conservative analyst is correct that the company can service debt, but they're underweighting the tail risk of spread widening. A balanced approach would trim 10-15% of position into near-term strength—not 25% or the entirety.

## The Technical Setup: A False Choice Between "Breakdown" and "Noise"

Now let me address where I think both analysts are making a mistake on technicals, because this is where market timing bias is most dangerous.

The aggressive analyst sees price below three moving averages, MACD death cross, and RSI at 42.58 and concludes "structural breakdown." The conservative analyst looks at the same signals and says "normal consolidation, not capitulation."

**Here's the issue with both framings:** they're trying to extract too much signal from what is genuinely ambiguous technical data. Let me be specific.

A stock trading below its 50 and 200 SMA is bearish *in the context of what preceded it*. In this case, EFX rallied from $240 in October to $269 in December—a 12% run. Then it pulled back 6.8%. That's a *healthy correction after an intermediate rally*, not a breakdown. The MACD death cross that the aggressive analyst emphasizes? Look at the magnitude: MACD at -1.997 vs. signal at -1.326. Those are *small negative numbers*. This isn't a violent MACD divergence; this is a modest inflection that could reverse in a single strong week of trading.

**But—and this is the critical "but"—the conservative analyst's argument that "MACD has crossed over before without triggering 15% declines" is both true and irrelevant.** Of course it has. That doesn't tell us anything about *this specific setup*. Technical patterns work probabilistically, not deterministically. MACD death cross + price below moving averages + RSI neutral (not oversold) *does* tilt odds slightly toward downside, even if it doesn't guarantee it.

So here's my honest technical assessment: the setup is *slightly* bearish but not screaming "sell." It's more like "yellow light," not "red light." A 3-5% move either direction from here wouldn't violate any technical principle. Price could bounce to $260 and confirm a reversal, or break down to $245 and trigger stops. The technical setup itself doesn't resolve which way that goes.

**The neutral takeaway:** Don't over-index on technical signals as a primary driver of position sizing. Use technicals to *manage entry and exit points* on positions justified by fundamentals, not as primary directional forecasting tools. This means: if you're going to trim exposure, use the technicals to *time* the trim into any near-term bounce (which is what the research plan proposes), not to justify the magnitude of the trim.
