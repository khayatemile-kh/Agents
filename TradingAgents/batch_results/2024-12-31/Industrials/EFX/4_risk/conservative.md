## First: The "Capex Starvation" Argument Falls Apart Under Scrutiny

Here's what the data actually shows: Equifax generated $813M in free cash flow in 2024, up 57.6% year-over-year. Yes, capex as a percentage of revenue declined. But let me give you the fuller picture that matters for risk assessment:

**Capital expenditure in absolute terms was $505M in 2024.** That's not some skeletal investment level. For context, the company is investing over $500M annually in infrastructure, technology, and capabilities. This is the business spending on data centers, cybersecurity infrastructure, cloud migration, and yes—AI-related technology. These are not trivial investments, and frankly, companies that have optimized their capex efficiency aren't "starving" themselves; they're maturing.

The aggressive analyst is assuming capex must return to 12.2% of revenue or the market will punish the company. But why? Where's the evidence that 8.9% is insufficient? Equifax is generating consistent revenue growth (7.9% in 2024), EBITDA margins are stable at 30.3%, and the company is successfully managing its cost structure. If capex guidance comes in at 9-10% of revenue—not a return to 12.2%—that's not a "guidance crater." That's a modest normalization of already-healthy efficiency.

More importantly: **the aggressive analyst is betting on a binary catalyst—earnings disappointment on capex guidance—that may not materialize as dramatically as suggested.** Management has had time to communicate capex expectations. If they were going to shock the market with normalized capex, they would have telegraphed it in advance guidance or conference call commentary. The fact that the market hasn't already priced in capex normalization concerns suggests either (a) management has already communicated expectations, or (b) the concern isn't as acute as the aggressive case assumes.

This is speculative timing on a catalyst that may be priced in already or may not materialize at all.

Because in a tightening credit environment, demand for credit risk analytics and fraud detection services typically *increases.* Equifax doesn't get hit the same way a leveraged retail company or real estate speculator gets hit.

The fact that spreads aren't currently reflecting elevated tail risk suggests either (a) the market is accurately pricing low recession probability, or (b) the market will gradually reprice over time, giving us ample warning before any sharp spread blowout. This isn't a hidden tail risk; it's already somewhat reflected in pricing.
