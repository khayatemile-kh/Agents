## THE FUNDAMENTAL DISCONNECT: PAYING BUBBLE PRICES FOR CYCLICAL EARNINGS

### **1. The P/E Valuation Trap**

The bull opens with a confident argument: "28x P/E is *warranted* for a 54% operating margin and 31% ROE."

**I need to push back hard here.** This logic conflates *current profitability* with *sustainable, forward earnings power*—and that's where the argument breaks down.

**Here's the critical issue:** Blackstone's exceptional 54% operating margin and 31% ROE are *not* structural constants. They're cyclical outputs of a business model highly dependent on three things:

1. **Elevated carried interest and performance fees** (which spike in up markets and collapse in down markets)
2. **Realized gains on securities** (which are lumpy and tied to exit activity)
3. **Management fees on growing AUM** (which depend on continued capital inflows)

Look at the Q3 2025 earnings data more carefully:

- **Q3 Revenue: $2,686M**
- **Q2 Revenue: $3,161M**
- **QoQ Decline: -15%**

This is not a rounding error. **Revenue collapsed 15% quarter-over-quarter.** And the bull wants us to justify a 28x multiple on *today's* earnings as if this run rate is sustainable? That's dangerous.

Let me dig deeper into that $2.69B Q3 revenue:
- **Gains on sales of securities: $1.259B** (Q3) vs. $1.063B (Q2)
- This represents **47% of total quarterly revenue.**

**Translation:** Nearly half of BX's quarterly revenue in Q3 came from realizing gains on securities—essentially disposing of positions at favorable prices. This is *not* recurring management fee income. When portfolio companies are being harvested and exited, you're running down dry powder, not deploying it.

### **The Earnings Quality Problem**

The bull cites net income of $3.52B on a $149.9B market cap, implying a 28x multiple. But here's what we need to understand:

**Q3 2025 earnings were significantly inflated by non-recurring gains.** Strip out the $1.259B in securities gains (which are inherently lumpy and timing-dependent), and you're left with:

- Revenue ex-gains: ~$1,427M
- After operating expenses (~$1,239M), you're down to **~$188M** in core operating income
- That translates to a **~$0.24 EPS run rate** (quarterly), or roughly **$0.96 annualized**—not the $3.20 implied by the $3.52B net income

**This would put the actual forward P/E closer to 171x on *core* earnings, not 28x.**

The bull is using a multiple that includes a one-time bounce in securities realizations. That's not a fair valuation comparison to the S&P 500 or investment banks trading at 12-14x on recurring operating earnings.

## THE MACRO CASE IS ACTUALLY BEARISH, NOT BULLISH

### **1. "Fed on Hold Through 2026" = Higher Rates = Compressed Deal Returns**

The bull frames this as bullish: "Higher yields support credit strategies, and compressed PE valuations mean better entry multiples."

**This is a dangerous half-truth that ignores the full cost-of-capital picture.**

Yes, lower *entry* multiples are attractive in isolation. But here's what the bull is downplaying:

**The actual problem for Blackstone's returns:**

- **LBO financing costs have exploded.** With 10Y yields at sticky elevated levels (~4.2-4.5%), the cost of financing leveraged acquisitions has increased dramatically. Typical LBO leverage is 60-65% debt. If debt costs rise from 5% to 7-8%, that's a 200-300bps increase in annual financing expense *per portfolio company*.
- **This directly crimps IRRs.** A typical private equity deal model assumes 3-5 year hold periods with 20-25% IRR targets. When financing costs spike, those returns compress sharply—often into the mid-teens.
- **Blackstone's dry powder ($300B+) faces a **compressed opportunity set**. Yes, entry multiples are lower (8-10x EBITDA vs. 11-12x), but financing is so expensive that the risk-adjusted return profile is actually *worse* than when valuations were higher but financing was cheaper.

**Concrete example:** In 2021, a $1B acquisition at 10x EBITDA with 3% debt costs generated very different returns than a $1B acquisition at 8x EBITDA with 7% debt costs. The entry multiple is better, but the total return may actually be worse.

This is why **private equity activity has already slowed** in late 2024-2025. Deal volume is down, and dry powder is accumulating—not because deals are attractive, but because they're *not*.

The bull cites prediction markets pricing only 8% recession risk through end-2026 and frames this as a tailwind.

**I see this as a red flag—a sign of dangerous complacency.**

- **In 2019**, recession risk was ~10-15% for 2020. Then COVID hit.
- **Prediction markets are notoriously bad at anticipating tail risks.** They're efficient at pricing *known* information, but terrible at pricing unknown unknowns.
