When you have to "normalize" away a $5.7 billion write-down just to make quarterly results look acceptable, that's not a sign of health—that's a sign that headline results are so ugly that management feels compelled to provide alternative metrics. The Aggressive Analyst then goes further and claims the $3.4 billion receivables jump is just "seasonal timing" that will reverse. But they're asking us to accept this on faith, not on evidence. The data shows receivables jumped $3.4 billion in Q3 alone. That's a material working capital deterioration happening *right now*, regardless of whether it eventually reverses. On technicals, the Aggressive Analyst makes a seductive argument: price above moving averages, RSI normalized to 60, consolidation pattern before breakout. This is textbook bullish framing, and I'll grant them this—it's not obviously wrong. But they're conveniently minimizing the MACD signal, which is objectively in bearish crossover territory. That's not a minor detail. MACD crossovers are one of the most reliable momentum indicators, and when MACD turns negative while price is still elevated, that's not confirmation of continued strength—it's a warning that momentum is rolling over. The Aggressive Analyst's response is essentially "MACD declining is fine because price is still above moving averages." But that's precisely how most pullbacks *begin*. The two-month 20.6% rally has already happened. The question isn't whether it was real; it obviously was. The question is what happens next, and momentum indicators suggest the next phase involves consolidation *downward*, not upward.

The reality is more ambiguous. Yes, receivables jumped $3.4 billion, and yes, that suppresses reported FCF. It proves that historical acquisition valuations were too optimistic. It doesn't prove the current business is broken—it proves past expectations were wrong. Those are different things. A company can have overpaid for an acquisition in 2017-2018 and still operate a profitable insurance division today.
