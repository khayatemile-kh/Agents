## The Operating Margin Argument Doesn't Hold Up

The aggressive thesis cites the 18.67% operating margin as "exceptional for a utility" and suggests it's not priced into the 5.54x PE multiple. Let me explain why this is a sophisticated misdirection.

First, operating margin ≠ net margin. The 18.67% operating margin tells you the company is good at generating EBIT from revenues. But that's *before* interest expense, which for a company with $30.85 billion in debt is absolutely *crushing* the bottom line. 

The debt servicing burden is colossal. With $30.85B in total debt and assuming an average blended borrowing cost of even 4.5% (which is conservative given current rate environment), AES is paying roughly $1.4B *annually* in interest alone. That's interest expense that converts that 18.67% operating margin into a much thinner net margin.

And here's what really matters: that 5.54x PE ratio *already reflects* the operating margin. The market hasn't "forgotten" about 18.67% operating margins—it's priced them in and then *discounted the valuation further* because it recognizes that high operating margins are being consumed by financial leverage costs. The low PE isn't a sign that the market is underpricing operations; it's a sign that the market is *accurately pricing* the financial engineering and debt burden.

If you want to know what the market really thinks about AES's operational quality, look at the EV/EBITDA multiple. The company's EBITDA is $3.643B (2024 normalized). With a market cap of $10.56B and net debt of $29.091B, the enterprise value is $39.651B. That gives an EV/EBITDA of 10.89x. 

For context, regulated utilities typically trade at 12-14x EV/EBITDA. AES is trading at *below* that range, which tells you the market doesn't believe the EBITDA is as stable or assured as a pure-play regulated utility. And why would it? Because AES has $2.614B in *negative working capital* and a 0.746 current ratio—meaning the company's operational efficiency is built on a foundation of liquidity stress.
