#### **1. The Working Capital "Financing" Is a Ticking Time Bomb**

The bull presents negative working capital as a *feature* of a recurring revenue model:

> *"A $3.47B swing in working capital? That's $3.47B of free working capital financing—IBM's customers are essentially funding operations."*

**This is incorrect and shows a fundamental misunderstanding of deteriorating liquidity.**

Let's examine the actual numbers:

| Item | Q4 2024 | Q2 2025 | Change | Interpretation |
|------|---------|---------|--------|-----------------|
| **Current Assets** | $34.48B | $34.25B | ↓ -$230M | Stagnant |

| **Working Capital** | +$1.34B | -$3.47B | ↓ -$4.81B | **Massive Deterioration in 6 Months** |

**The Real Story:**

IBM's current ratio dropped from 1.04 to **0.91 in just six months**—this means current liabilities now exceed current assets. This is **not** a sign of an efficient subscription business model. This is a sign of **operational stress**.

When a company's current ratio falls below 1.0:
- It's dependent on continuous cash inflow to meet short-term obligations
- A disruption in cash flow creates immediate liquidity pressure
- Banks and credit rating agencies get nervous
- The company loses negotiating flexibility with suppliers and lenders

**Adobe, Salesforce, and ServiceNow (the bull's comparisons) all maintain current ratios above 1.0** because their subscription models *generate* working capital benefit without sacrificing liquidity. IBM's model is *consuming* liquidity while simultaneously building negative working capital—that's the opposite situation.
