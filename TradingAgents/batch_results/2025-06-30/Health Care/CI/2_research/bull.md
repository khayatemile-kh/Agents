## 3. BALANCE SHEET STRENGTH: STOP MISINTERPRETING THE INSURANCE MODEL

Now, here's where bears often stumble. They look at CI's balance sheet and say: *"Look at the debt! Look at the negative working capital! This is a financial house of cards!"*

**Wrong.** They're misunderstanding how insurance companies work.

**The Insurance Business Model is Structurally Unique:**

- **Negative Working Capital is a FEATURE, not a BUG:** Insurance companies collect premiums *upfront* and pay claims *over time*. This inverted cash cycle is the source of competitive advantage. Accounts payable of $45.41 billion sitting on the balance sheet means Cigna is holding customer and provider money—effectively **getting paid to hold liabilities.** This is called the "float," and Warren Buffett built Berkshire Hathaway on this principle.

- **Current Ratio of 0.846 is NORMAL for insurers:** The market is confusing manufacturing companies (which need 1.5x+ current ratios) with insurance companies (which have current ratios of 0.7-0.9x by design). Cigna's cash reserves of $4.33 billion + receivables of $31.15 billion = $35.48 billion in immediate liquidity against current liabilities of $56.78 billion. In the insurance world, this is healthy. Claims are paid gradually; money doesn't flee overnight.

- **Debt-to-Equity of 74.29x reflects insurance leverage, not financial distress:** This ratio is misleading when applied to insurers. Insurance companies naturally operate with high leverage because they deploy policyholder reserves into investments. The relevant metric is *debt-to-capital* and *interest coverage*—not equity leverage. Cigna's interest expense is $337 million on EBIT of $2.36 billion quarterly, yielding an **interest coverage ratio of 7.0x.** That's fortress-like. The company is not at financial risk.
