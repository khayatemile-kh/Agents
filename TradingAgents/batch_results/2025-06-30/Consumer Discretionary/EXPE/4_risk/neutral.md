## WHERE THE AGGRESSIVE CASE OVERREACHES: CONFLATING CYCLICAL DECELERATION WITH IMMINENT COLLAPSE

The aggressive analyst makes a compelling technical case, but then makes a critical leap that I need to directly challenge. You're taking three valid observations—MACD negative crossover, EPS growth deceleration, and macro headwinds from elevated rates—and interpreting them as a **unified signal for immediate downside.** But they're not the same thing, and treating them as a synchronized breakdown is where the risk emerges.

Let me isolate each:

**On the MACD collapse:** You're right that MACD turned negative on June 13 and is now at 0.57 versus signal 0.70. That's real. But here's where the conservative analyst is also right to push back—the volume picture matters enormously for interpretation. You cannot have a structural breakdown signal with volume *declining* from May panic levels. When MACD rolls over but volume stays subdued and the 50 SMA holds support, that's consolidation, not capitulation.

But—and this is the critical nuance—**consolidation doesn't guarantee an upside break.** It just means the market hasn't decided yet. In that context, a 20-30% position trim into a bounce toward $170-$172 is prudent risk management *not because the stock is about to crash, but because you're reducing exposure before the consolidation resolves.*

The aggressive case treats the consolidation as if it's already resolved downward. It hasn't. Price could consolidate here for weeks and then break above 174.92. That's not a reason to hold and accumulate, but it's also not a reason to dump the position immediately.

**On EPS deceleration:** You're absolutely right that growth is slowing from 68.6% to 10-11%. That's a real deceleration. But the conservative analyst nails the critical reframe here: **this is a return to normalized earnings from pandemic depressed levels, not a cliff.** 

Here's what you're both missing: The market doesn't reprice stocks based on absolute growth rates; it reprices based on *changes in expectations.* If Wall Street consensus expects EXPE to grow 10-11% in 2025 and 2026, and the company delivers exactly that, there's no re-rating. The repricing happens if *guidance changes*—either upward or downward.

The question isn't whether 10% growth is attractive in absolute terms. It's whether management will guide to something different than what's currently priced in. You haven't shown that they will. In fact, the latest guidance and analyst consensus probably *already incorporate* this 10-11% deceleration. So the trade isn't "sell because growth is slowing;" the trade is "sell if you believe management will guide *below* current consensus."

That's a different argument entirely, and you haven't made it.

**On macro headwinds:** This is where I agree with you most, but I also see why the conservative analyst isn't panicking. Yes, 93% probability of zero Fed rate cuts in 2026 is a headwind. Yes, travel is cyclical and sensitive to discretionary income. But here's the massive caveat: **We're currently in 2025 Q2, not 2026.** 

The question isn't whether rates will be elevated in 2026. They will be. The question is whether that's already priced into EXPE's current valuation. At 18.7x P/E with a 6.9% free cash flow yield, the market is assuming *compressed* growth and elevated rates. A *recession* in 2026 would be new information. Elevated rates staying elevated is already baked in.

## THE BALANCE SHEET ARGUMENT NEITHER OF YOU FULLY ADDRESSES

Let me pivot to something both of you touched on but didn't fully integrate: the capital allocation decision to deploy 79% of FCF to buybacks.

The aggressive analyst treats this as a yellow flag—management signaling they have limited reinvestment opportunities. The conservative analyst treats it as a sign of confidence. I think you're both partially right and both missing something.

Here's what the data actually shows: EXPE generated $2.329B in free cash flow in 2024 and deployed $1.839B to buybacks. That's 79% of FCF going to shareholders. Over the same period, they spent only $756M on capex—just 2.8% of revenue.

Now, ask yourself: **Why is a travel services platform spending so little on capex while simultaneously buying back shares aggressively?**

The answer should be: Either (a) the business is so mature and asset-light that minimal capex is genuinely required, or (b) management doesn't see attractive reinvestment opportunities and is returning cash because organic growth is constrained.

Both could be true simultaneously. OTAs *are* asset-light businesses. But the combination of asset-light ops *and* aggressive buybacks *and* slowing growth *and* macro headwinds suggests something specific: **the business is generating cash flows that exceed growth opportunities, so management is distributing excess cash.**

That's not necessarily bearish—it could indicate a stable, mature cash generator. But it's also not a sign of momentum or accelerating growth. It's a sign of an equilibrium business that's optimizing for cash return rather than growth investment.

In that context, the trader's 20-30% position trim makes sense. You're not betting against the company; you're recognizing that the growth is slowing, the macro is headwind-y, and the current technical setup doesn't offer conviction for holding the full position.
