## THE CASH FLOW ARGUMENT IS OVERSOLD

Yes, FDS generates $228.6 million in quarterly free cash flow with a 39.1% margin. Impressive on the surface. But let me ask the fundamental question the Aggressive Analyst conveniently avoided: **Where is this cash actually going?**

The data shows FDS paid down $542.5 million in debt last quarter while simultaneously issuing $498.4 million in new debt. That's not deleveraging with conviction—that's **debt refinancing**. They're replacing maturing obligations with fresh capital, likely at higher rates given where we are in the interest rate cycle as of June 2025. The net debt position remains stubbornly elevated at $1.07 billion.

Moreover, they returned $120.2 million to shareholders while increasing cash reserves by only $79.8 million. That's a concerning dynamic: the company is distributing capital *faster* than it's accumulating it beyond the threshold needed for operations. If there's any disruption to that free cash flow—and we'll get to why disruption is a real risk—this capital structure becomes problematic quickly.

The real issue: **that $228.6M in quarterly FCF assumes business continuity at current levels**. What happens when client spend contracts? What happens when there's a financial services slowdown? We have zero data on FDS's client concentration, contract duration, or renewal rates from the available sources. That's a massive blind spot.

One disruption—a client attrition event, a revenue slowdown, a credit event in their industry—and you're forced to draw on that cash buffer rapidly.

That's option-like risk on the downside.

Debt-to-EBITDA at 1.75x is serviceable under normal conditions. But look at what happens if EBITDA contracts even 15% due to a slowdown in financial services activity: suddenly you're at 2.1x. Add in higher refinancing rates, and the math becomes uncomfortable quickly.
