## THE "ACCOUNTING PHENOMENON" NARRATIVE IS PRECISELY THE PROBLEM

The aggressive analyst is correct that FIS generated $372 million in free cash flow despite a $470 million GAAP loss. Let me be precise about what that tells us: **it tells us the company has massive non-cash charges that management is deploying to suppress reported earnings.**

But here's where their logic breaks down: they're treating depreciation and amortization as some kind of accounting artifact that doesn't matter. That's dangerously incomplete. Those $480 million in D&A charges exist because FIS carries $21.388 billion in goodwill and intangible assets on its balance sheet. That's not a quaint feature of their acquisition strategy—**that's a fundamental structural problem.**

When a company has 64% of its asset base tied up in goodwill and intangibles, it means 64% of its value is not backed by tangible, liquidatable assets. It's backed by the assumption that future cash flows will justify those acquisition prices. And here's the tension the aggressive analyst glosses over: **if those future cash flows disappoint—if just one major client defects, if financial services processing volumes decline, if competition intensifies—FIS has to take an impairment charge.**

An impairment charge would wipe equity value overnight. We've seen this movie before with other large tech services acquirers. The cheerleaders pointed to cash flow, ignored the intangibles, and then watched 30-40% drawdowns when reality caught up. The aggressive analyst is asking us to have faith that the next impairment charge won't happen. I'm not willing to bet the firm's capital on that faith.

Moreover—and this is critical—**the Q2 2025 loss of $470 million represents an *acceleration* of deterioration, not a one-time event.** The aggressive analyst frames this as "temporarily hammered," but where's the evidence of recovery? Looking at the fundamentals, I don't see a business about to snap back to historical profitability. I see a business that's hitting earnings headwinds, and management is masking the severity through aggressive refinancing and debt issuance.

1. **The market sees deteriorating earnings quality** — that Q2 loss isn't priced in isolation. It's a signal that the trajectory is wrong.

2. **The market is concerned about leverage and refinancing risk** — FIS is highly levered, and refinancing is possible now but not guaranteed forever. If credit conditions tighten, this becomes a serious problem.

The aggressive analyst would have us believe that the market is simply *wrong*—that it's misprice FIS and we should fade that pricing. The low P/E is not an opportunity—**it's the market's way of saying "we're not confident in these earnings."**

## THE LEVERAGE STORY IS WORSE THAN THEY ADMIT

Now let's talk about the balance sheet in a way the aggressive analyst won't. They say the 3.5x net debt-to-EBITDA is "investment-grade" and therefore acceptable. But that's misleading on multiple dimensions.

First, **3.5x at the high end of the range is exactly where problems start for financial services companies.** The reason investment-grade companies operate at 2-4x is because 3.5x is the point where refinancing risk becomes material, where credit spreads widen, and where covenant violations become a real possibility in any downturn. FIS isn't at a comfortable 2.5x—they're at 3.5x, which means they have almost zero margin for error.

Second, and more importantly, **that 3.5x ratio assumes current EBITDA levels remain constant.** But if financial services demand weakens—if that "overheating" or "soft landing" scenario doesn't materialize, or if FIS loses market share—EBITDA could contract by 15-20% quite quickly. In that scenario, the net debt-to-EBITDA ratio spikes to 4.2-4.5x, and suddenly we're looking at a covenant violation or a forced refinancing at significantly higher rates.

Third, the aggressive analyst points to active debt refinancing as a sign of competence. I read it differently: **it's a sign of necessity.** When a company is refinancing $12 billion annually while carrying a negative retained earnings position and generating Q2 losses, they're not refinancing out of choice—they're refinancing because they have to. And every refinancing cycle is an opportunity for credit markets to reprice them higher.

The current ratio of 0.535 is also brushed aside as "normal for cash-generative businesses." That's technically true, but it's true because these businesses have high working capital turnover. FIS's working capital is *negative* at -$3.078 billion. That means they're not just operating lean—they're operating in a position where any disruption to cash collection could create a liquidity crisis. That's not comfortable, and it's not something we should accept in a scaled position.
