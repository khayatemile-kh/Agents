**Rationale**: Both sides agree on the core facts: DOW is in a deep cyclical trough, TTM EPS is negative (-$1.83), operating cash flow was -$470M in Q2 even after adding back $591M of restructuring charges, leverage is elevated (Debt/Equity 111.91%, Net Debt/EBITDA ~3.7x) versus peers like LyondellBasell (68.4%/2.1x) and Eastman (71.3%/2.4x), and the technical picture is weak (down 10.3% in two months, below both 50- and 200-day MAs, elevated volume on down days). The bull's case rests almost entirely on a forward-looking narrative: input costs will moderate, the cycle will inflect in Q4 2025/Q1 2026, and EPS will 'normalize' to $3.50-$4.00 by re-anchoring on 2021-2022 peak-cycle earnings. The bear correctly points out this is a heroic assumption - consensus FY2026 EPS is only $1.80, meaning the bull's 'normalized' valuation requires a leap 2+ years beyond even the closest available forward estimate, with no concrete catalyst identified for why margins recover on that timeline rather than staying compressed given persistent global overcapacity.

The bear's rebuttal on the balance sheet is also more rigorous and evidence-based: the peer leverage comparison shows DOW is the most stretched name in its group, cash is only $2.40B (14% of current liabilities), and the 'healthy' 1.75x current ratio is inflated by receivables and inventory that carry real risk of write-down/collection problems in a continued downturn. The restructuring-charge argument cuts both ways - it's reasonable capital allocation in theory, but the bear is right that a company still burning cash after adding back a $591M non-cash charge is not really at breakeven; the underlying business is still consuming capital, and restructuring is an admission that near-term demand isn't expected to justify current capacity, not a sign of imminent recovery.

Where the bull is more compelling: the point that cyclicals experience exactly this kind of despair at trough valuations, the moat argument around installed asset base, and the fact that no recession is being priced by markets currently. But the bull never rebuts the debt-to-equity peer comparison or explains why FY2026 consensus of just $1.80 EPS justifies paying today for a $3.50-$4.00 'mid-cycle' number that's several years out. The dividend safety point actually cuts against the bull once you factor in negative operating cash flow — a 4.76% yield is exactly the kind of yield that gets cut when cash flow doesn't cover it, and the bear flags this risk explicitly.

Net: the bear's argument is grounded in currently observable numbers (leverage, cash burn, consensus estimates) while the bull's thesis depends on a multi-year normalization with no clear near-term catalyst and a technical setup that is actively deteriorating, not stabilizing. This tips the debate toward caution. I'm not going full Sell because there is a genuine possibility of a cyclical bottom forming and the asset base / competitive positioning bull points have some validity for the very long term, and a full exit ignores real optionality if the cycle does turn. But the near/medium-term risk-reward clearly favors trimming, not adding or holding steady, given cash burn, leverage stress relative to peers, negative technicals, and a valuation call that requires assuming numbers well beyond what consensus currently supports.

5. Retain a small residual position (rather than a full exit) to preserve optionality on a genuine cyclical inflection, but size it so it does not represent an outsized bet on a recovery timeline that current consensus does not yet support.
6. Reassess at Q3/Q4 2025 earnings for concrete evidence of margin inflection (not just narrative) - specifically watch capacity utilization, input cost spread, and whether operating cash flow turns positive before adding exposure back.
