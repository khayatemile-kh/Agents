**Rationale**: Both sides make legitimate points, but weighing them against each other, the bull case rests on firmer, more verifiable ground while the bear case leans heavily on speculative modeling and unproven competitive inferiority claims.

On fundamentals: the bull cites hard, reported numbers—margin expansion from 19.9% to 37.9% net margin over 2022-2024, FCF growing to $54B, and capex being fully covered by $90B+ OCF. The bear's rebuttal is that this margin expansion is a one-time artifact of 2022 layoffs and will mean-revert as hiring resumes for AI talent. The bear's forward model (assuming margin compression to 33-35% and FCF collapsing to $9-21B) is a hypothetical projection, not an observed fact, and appears to substantially double-count D&A and capex in a way that isn't how FCF accounting actually nets out (capex is already subtracted from OCF to get FCF; the bear's model conflates operating income minus capex minus D&A, which double-counts depreciation that's already embedded in operating income). This makes the bear's quantitative critique less credible on close inspection.

On AI positioning: the bear raises a fair point that Meta doesn't have a proprietary chip advantage like Google's TPUs and that Llama being open-source means competitors benefit too. However, the bear's argument that open-sourcing Llama is purely a disadvantage ignores the ecosystem/distribution benefits (developer mindshare, ubiquity in Meta's own ad/recommendation stack) — and critically, the bear never actually rebuts the bull's strongest and most concrete point: Meta's ad-targeting AI is already driving measurable revenue acceleration and margin gains for the core $155B+ ad business. The bear's competitive table is mostly qualitative benchmarking rhetoric (whose model is 'better') without hard revenue/share evidence, whereas the bull ties AI investment directly to observed ad revenue and CPM performance.

On technicals: the bull's framing (RSI persistently overbought during an uptrend with rising 10/50/200 EMA structure, positive MACD, declining ATR) is a legitimate technical read that overbought conditions in strong trends often signal continuation, not reversal. The bear didn't really engage with technicals in the excerpt provided, so this pillar goes unchallenged in the debate as given.

Net assessment: the bull's case is grounded in trailing, already-realized profitability and cash generation trends plus a coherent technical picture, while the bear's strongest argument (margin/FCF compression) rests on a forward projection that both contradicts the bear's own data (still-improving OpEx ratio) and appears to misapply capex/D&A accounting. The AI-moat argument is a real long-term risk worth monitoring but is not yet supported by revenue-share erosion. Given the stronger evidentiary footing of the bull thesis and the speculative/methodologically shaky nature of the bear's main quantitative rebuttal, I'm siding with the bull, but not with unreserved conviction — the legitimate risks (capex intensity rising to ~29% of revenue, potential hiring-driven OpEx reversal, AI competitive intensity) argue against a maximum-conviction Buy and instead support a constructive Overweight: add exposure opportunistically rather than chase the extended, overbought move.
