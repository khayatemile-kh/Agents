**Rationale**: Both sides agree on the core secular tailwind (AI/cloud capex driving data center demand) and on EQIX's operational quality (52% gross margins, 46% EBITDA margins, interconnection moat). Where they diverge is on how to weigh the rate/valuation risk against that growth story, and that's where the bear case is more rigorous.

The bull's central claim - that 55x forward P/E 'already prices in' rate pessimism - is contradicted by the bear's own evidence: the stock just fell 18.5% on macro/rate repricing, which is direct proof the multiple was NOT fully discounting further tightening. You can't simultaneously argue 'it's priced in' and point to a rate-driven crash as the cause of the crash - that's an internal contradiction the bull never resolves. The bear's sensitivity table showing that even a robust 12% EBITDA growth scenario gets fully offset (or worse) by a 15-30% multiple compression is the more decision-useful analysis here, because EQIX genuinely is a long-duration asset (30+ year leases) whose valuation is structurally more discount-rate-sensitive than the comparison set the bull chose (AAPL/MSFT/NVDA are poor comps for exactly this reason - shorter effective duration).

The bear also lands solid hits on competitive/oversupply risk (hyperscalers increasingly self-building, specialized AI infra players like CoreWeave fragmenting demand) which directly undercuts the bull's assumption that EQIX's $2.2B pipeline will be absorbed at premium margins. The bull's rebuttal ("selectivity" implies pricing power) doesn't really address the risk that even selective builds can face pricing pressure if enough competing supply comes online in the same key metros.

On leverage, the bull's 4.8x coverage looks fine in isolation, but the bear correctly notes this is a static snapshot - refinancing $5B of maturing debt at higher rates would mechanically erode coverage, and this is happening in real time given the 62% market-implied probability of continued tightening through Sept 2026. That's a live, dated risk, not a hypothetical.

Technically, both sides use the same data (RSI 21→36, MACD negative crossover, price below all major SMAs) but the bear's read - weak bounce on declining volume rather than a durable reversal - is the more conservative and probably correct interpretation absent confirming price/volume follow-through. The bull's 'capitulation floor' claim is really a prediction about future Fed pivot timing, which is speculative and not evidenced in the current data.

Where I think the bull still has a point: this is a high-quality, moat-protected asset (irreplicable interconnection density, multi-decade contracts, investment-grade-adequate coverage today) and the selloff is not driven by any company-specific fundamental impairment (no earnings miss, no covenant breach, no customer loss). That argues against an aggressive Sell/exit call and toward trimming rather than panic-dumping.

Net: the market-implied 62% odds of continued tightening through Sept 2026, the demonstrated rate-sensitivity of the multiple (the crash itself is the proof), the live refinancing risk on an unusually long-duration asset, and the emerging competitive/oversupply risk in key metros collectively outweigh the bull's 'multiple compression is reversible' and 'capitulation floor' arguments, which rest on timing a Fed pivot that isn't evidenced in the current data. This tips the balance to a cautious stance - not an outright Sell (because there's no fundamental impairment and the long-term secular demand story is real), but a clear Underweight: trim exposure now, protect capital against further multiple compression, and revisit sizing once there's concrete evidence of rate stabilization or a clean technical reversal on strong volume.
