## THE "TIGHT BUT SERVICEABLE" INTEREST COVERAGE IS ACTUALLY A SYSTEMIC RISK

For context, a healthy leverage ratio is typically 40-60% debt-to-equity. CVS is at 95%. More concerning, the company has negative working capital of $18.51 billion, meaning it's dependent on continuous refinancing and operational cash flow to service this debt.

Interest coverage of 3.2x means the company is only generating $3.20 in operating earnings for every $1 of interest expense. That leaves minimal room for error. If:

- Operating income declines even 20% (to $1.90 billion), interest coverage drops to 2.5x—now you're in territory where debt covenants may be triggered.
- Interest rates rise another 100-150 basis points, the annual interest bill climbs by $800 million to $1.5+ billion, collapsing coverage below 2.0x.
- Healthcare utilization slows or PBM margins compress further, operating income contracts.

The Aggressive Analyst says CVS has "optionality"—they can de-lever or reduce debt. With $5.16 billion in free cash flow, that's theoretically true. But here's the reality: CVS currently pays $866 million in dividends annually (on a quarterly run rate, annualized to ~$3.5 billion). That's 68% of free cash flow going to dividend payments *right now*. If the company maintains or grows the dividend (which it typically does as a mature healthcare firm), de-levering becomes secondary to capital allocation.

Add in $607 million in quarterly capex ($2.4 billion annualized), and you're at $6 billion in committed cash outflows annually just for dividends and maintenance capex. That leaves minimal room to service debt reduction *and* absorb operational shocks.

In a recession, if healthcare utilization drops and margins compress, CVS could find itself scrambling to maintain dividend coverage while servicing nearly $83 billion in debt. That's not strength—that's a fragile capital structure.
