## WHERE THE AGGRESSIVE CASE MISSES THE FOREST FOR THE TREES

Let me challenge the Aggressive Analyst on three specific points where their analysis prioritizes tactical gains over risk mitigation:

### 1. The "Redeploy Into Better Utilities" Strategy Is Itself A Risk

The Aggressive Analyst recommends rotating proceeds into utilities with Net Debt/EBITDA below 4.5x—names like Duke Energy, American Electric Power, or Southern Company. This sounds prudent on the surface. But consider what we're actually doing here:

We're taking the proceeds from selling an overleveraged utility and reinvesting into *other utilities*, which are themselves capital-intensive, interest-rate sensitive, and dependent on regulatory cooperation. Yes, Duke Energy's leverage is better. But Duke Energy is *still* a regulated utility operating in an environment where:

- Interest rates remain elevated relative to historical norms
- Regulatory rate-setting timelines are lengthening, not shortening
- Grid modernization costs continue to escalate
- Refinancing risk persists across the entire sector

The Aggressive Analyst is essentially saying, "EXC is too risky, so let's buy a slightly less risky utility instead." But that's substituting *one leverage risk for another*. It's not reducing risk; it's redistributing it.

From a conservative standpoint, if the underlying thesis is that utilities are facing refinancing headwinds and that negative FCF profiles are becoming endemic to the sector, then the intelligent response isn't tactical rotation within utilities. It's sector rotation entirely—or at minimum, a rebalancing into non-leveraged income alternatives.

### 2. The Dividend Yield Trap Argument Cuts Both Ways

The Aggressive Analyst correctly identifies that EXC's 3.84% dividend is being funded partially by debt issuance, not purely by cash flow. This is accurate and concerning. But then they suggest redeploying into "better utilities" which—and here's the critical point—are *also* funding dividends through debt issuance, albeit at lower leverage multiples.

Duke Energy yields approximately 3.2-3.5%. American Electric Power yields around 3.4-3.8%. They're not.

If capital expenditure requirements are rising across the utility sector due to grid modernization and renewable integration, and if operating cash flows are struggling to keep pace, then *all utilities are playing the debt issuance game*. Some are just earlier in the cycle than others. Selling EXC to buy Duke is not de-risking; it's lateral movement with the false comfort of a better balance sheet.

**From a conservative perspective, this matters enormously.** We're not just evaluating EXC in isolation. We're asking: what does EXC's deterioration tell us about the broader utility sector's ability to deliver sustainable income and capital appreciation? If the answer is "these are refinancing-dependent entities facing headwinds," then rotating into marginally better names doesn't solve the problem. It delays it.
