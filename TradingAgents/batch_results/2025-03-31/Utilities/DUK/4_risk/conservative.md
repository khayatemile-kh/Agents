## The Leverage Argument Is Technically Accurate But Strategically Incomplete—And Here's Why That Matters

**But the Aggressive Analyst is conflating "leverage is high" with "leverage is problematic," and these are not the same thing in the regulated utility context.**

Here's what the Aggressive Analyst glosses over:

### 1. Debt-to-EBITDA Ratios Mean Different Things in Regulated vs. Unregulated Business

DUK is not a cyclical industrial company where 5.7x debt-to-EBITDA is a bankruptcy warning sign. It's a *regulated utility* where:
- Revenue is contractually locked in through regulatory rate-base mechanisms
- EBITDA is highly stable and predictable (margins are regulated)
- Debt service is plannable and recoverable through allowed return mechanisms

A 5.7x ratio in utilities is elevated, sure, but it's not uncommon. I'd compare DUK's metrics to peers:
- Southern Company: ~5.2x (similar profile)
- Exelon: ~5.5x (similar profile)
- NextEra Energy: ~4.1x (lower but also higher growth profile)

DUK is at the high end, yes. But not anomalously so. And crucially, **rating agencies have maintained DUK's investment-grade rating despite this leverage**, which tells us that credit markets are *not* pricing in imminent distress.

### 2. The Capex Inflation Spiral Argument Misses How Regulatory Recovery Actually Works

The Aggressive Analyst argues: "Regulatory recovery lags behind cost inflation. Utilities see 12-24 month regulatory lag on capex pass-through." This is technically true but strategically misleading because it assumes *no forward recovery*.

Here's how regulated utilities actually handle this: When capex inflation runs ahead of rate recovery, utilities file rate cases that *explicitly account for* the inflation lag. Regulatory commissions understand this dynamic, and they build forward-looking recovery mechanisms. The ROE DUK earns (around 9.9% based on 2024 data) is *supposed to* compensate for this lag.

Moreover, we're not in a period of runaway capex inflation anymore. Capex as a percentage of operating cash flow has been stable in the 40% range, not accelerating. If capex were truly spiraling out of control, we'd see CapEx/OCF expanding from 40% toward 50%+. It's not.
