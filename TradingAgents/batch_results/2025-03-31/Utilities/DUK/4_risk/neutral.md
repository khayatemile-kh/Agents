## Where the Aggressive Analyst Is Right (And Where They Overreach)

Let me start with the technical case because there's legitimate signal here that the Conservative Analyst is too quick to dismiss.

The Aggressive Analyst is absolutely correct that DUK is displaying classic bearish divergence characteristics: MACD declining 34% from its March 20 peak while price continues climbing. This is real. This is observable. And yes, it's a warning sign that institutional positioning may be shifting.

But—and this is critical—the Aggressive Analyst commits a cardinal sin of technical analysis: they mistake "warning sign" for "directional certainty." Here's the reality check: MACD divergences identify *periods of caution*, not timing mechanisms. The Conservative Analyst is right that divergences appear in 30-40% of healthy uptrends. That's not a weakness of their argument—that's the *confirmation* that divergences are necessary but not sufficient for predicting reversals.

The Aggressive Analyst also makes an intellectually honest point about the Bollinger Band: yes, 0.15% headroom to the 2-SD threshold is objectively tight. But then they make the leap that this *must* mean mean reversion is imminent. The Conservative Analyst correctly pushes back: Bollinger Bands define probability boundaries, not destiny. A stock can and does break through bands on continued momentum. The fact that DUK broke through on elevated volume on March 31 could be exactly what you'd expect in the *beginning* of a new leg higher, not the end.

Here's what the Aggressive Analyst gets right that deserves weight: **the risk-reward at $115.77 for a 15-25% trim makes intuitive sense regardless of which direction the stock goes next.** If it pulls back to $110-112, you've locked in gains and reset your entry point. If it continues to $120-125, you've kept 75-85% of your position to participate in the move. That's not a bet on direction—that's just position sizing hygiene. And that's where they're strongest.

DUK added $10.65B in debt over three years while operating cash flow grew only 24.6% in the most recent year. That *is* a slower-growth problem. You can say "it's normal for utilities," and you'd be right. But you cannot say it's not a constraint.
