## **On the "EBITDA Decline is Just Accounting" Argument**

The aggressive analyst argues that the 16.7% EBITDA decline is non-structural because operating cash flow rose 17.4%. This is a dangerous misreading of what the data actually shows.

Here's what they're missing: **OCF growth and EBITDA decline happening simultaneously is not a positive signal—it's a red flag that earnings quality is deteriorating.**

Let me explain the distinction carefully. Operating cash flow can grow while EBITDA falls when a company is:

1. **Pulling forward collections** (working capital timing, not operational strength)
2. **Deferring payments** (extending payables artificially inflates cash)
3. **Capitalizing costs** that should be expensed (making the P&L look worse while the cash statement looks better)
4. **Converting low-quality earnings into cash** through aggressive collection practices

The fact that EBITDA—which strips out non-cash items—fell 16.7% while OCF rose tells us something critical: **the underlying business is generating less operational profit, but the company is managing the cash conversion cycle aggressively to maintain the appearance of cash generation.**

For a utility, this is particularly concerning because utilities operate on predictable, regulated cash flows. When you see deteriorating EBITDA *in a regulated utility*, you're not looking at temporary accounting noise—you're looking at real operational pressure. Utilities don't have significant working capital swings. Their revenue is stable. Their expenses are largely fixed. A 16.7% EBITDA decline reflects **actual margin compression**, likely driven by higher fuel costs, labor inflation, or insufficient rate case victories to offset cost pressures.

The aggressive analyst wants us to believe this is a "cost restructuring story" with "exceptional cash conversion efficiency." But they haven't explained *why* EBITDA fell. And that omission is telling. The answer is probably less exciting: operational headwinds that the company is managing through tighter working capital and delayed discretionary spending—which is exactly when you should be cautious, not aggressive.

## **On the Debt/EBITDA "Recovery Narrative"**

The aggressive analyst argues that 5.87x Debt/EBITDA is a "temporary inflection point" because CapEx is declining and FCF is recovering. This fundamentally misunderstands the mechanics of leverage in a rising rate environment.

But here's the problem: **that $2.9 billion FCF improvement is not sustainable if it came from deferrals and working capital timing rather than structural operational improvement.** If OCF was artificially inflated by aggressive collections and payment deferrals (as I outlined above), then that $2.9 billion swing disappears as soon as the company normalizes its cash management practices.

In a rising rate environment, here's what happens to utilities:

- Refinancing existing debt becomes more expensive
- New capital projects face higher financing costs, reducing their economic returns
- Rate base returns authorized by regulators struggle to keep pace with cost of capital increases
- The delta between allowed return on equity (typically 9-10%) and weighted average cost of capital (rising toward 7-8%+) narrows

The aggressive analyst assumes that rate case victories will "automatically" expand EBITDA to de-lever. But regulated utilities don't work that way. Rate increases lag cost pressures. The company files a rate case, waits 12-18 months for regulatory approval, and *then* sees revenue lift. Meanwhile, debt service obligations on that $82.3 billion debt pile continue whether rates increase or not.

**With 5.87x leverage and 16.7% EBITDA decline already recorded, NEE is in a vulnerable position if rates stay elevated.** The company is refinancing debt at higher costs while EBITDA contracts. The aggressive analyst frames the 89% FCF payout ratio as evidence of a maturing utility transitioning to higher cash generation. They're fundamentally wrong about what this metric signals.

Here's the key distinction: **An 89% payout ratio on stable or growing FCF is defensible. An 89% payout ratio on FCF that surged from $1.8B to $4.7B in a single year—driven by working capital timing and CapEx deferrals—is a dividend trap waiting to happen.**

The company paid out $4.2 billion in dividends against $4.7 billion of FCF. That works mathematically in 2024. But look at the fundamental assumptions baked into that math:

- FCF growth was 169% in one year (2023-2024). The aggressive analyst treats this as evidence of "controlled capital allocation." I see it as unsustainable normalization after a heavily negative FCF year in 2022.
- The dividend grew 12% YoY ($3.8B to $4.2B). If management committed to "mid-to-high single digit growth" and FCF doesn't replicate that 169% surge, the payout ratio will exceed 100% *very quickly*.
- The trailing 12-month payout ratio may be 89%, but forward payout ratios are what matter. If FCF normalizes to $3-4B annually (which is more reasonable than assuming continued 169% growth), and management continues mid-to-high single digit dividend increases, we're looking at payout ratios approaching 110-120% within 2-3 years.

That's the dividend trap. The aggressive analyst is pointing at a single year's metric and declaring it "sustainable" without modeling forward scenarios. **Any competent risk manager should be asking: "What happens to the dividend if FCF reverts to $2-3 billion?" And the answer is uncomfortable: either the dividend gets cut, or it gets funded from debt.**

Neither outcome is attractive for equity holders. Dividend cuts destroy shareholder value. Debt-funded dividends increase leverage precisely when leverage is already elevated. This is exactly the kind of hidden risk that conservative positioning protects against.
