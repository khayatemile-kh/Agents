## The Fortress Myth: When Cash Flow Numbers Hide Structural Rot

GM generated $1.86 billion in net income last year while producing $21.9 billion in free cash flow. How is that possible? The gap comes from non-cash items and working capital management—primarily depreciation and amortization running at roughly $16-17 billion annually. That depreciation is real wear on the fleet. Those assets need replacement. When you strip out that non-cash cushion, the actual *earnings* the company generated were appallingly weak.

Here's the critical point the Aggressive Analyst glossed over: **that free cash flow is not sustainable at current margin levels.** Net profit margin fell from 6.3% to 3.2% in two years. Operating margin is a pathetic 3.20%. If those margins continue deteriorating—and the data suggests they will—that $21.9B figure evaporates. We're not looking at a temporary dip; we're looking at a company that's cannibalizing its asset base just to generate positive cash flow numbers.

The fortress argument also conveniently ignores capital expenditure requirements. GM is in the middle of an EV transition that demands massive capex. If the company is forced to maintain those investments while margins compress further, that $21.9B free cash flow becomes $15B becomes $10B. Fast. We've seen this movie before in the telecom industry, the old media industry—companies that looked like cash machines until the structural transition hit and they suddenly couldn't afford their capital programs.
