Here's what the data actually shows. The MACD collapsed to -2.55 on March 13 after V peaked at $358.60 on February 28. That wasn't a minor blip—that was a -9.4% drawdown accompanied by momentum breakdown. Now, three weeks later, the MACD has recovered to +0.71. But what does that tell us? It tells us momentum is recovering from an oversold extreme. It does *not* tell us that the underlying business has become more valuable, nor does it tell us institutional capital is entering at scale.

In fact, I would argue the opposite. The volume on March 31 was 9.95 million shares—elevated, yes, but this is hardly extraordinary for a mega-cap stock. For context, Visa trades approximately 30-40 million shares daily at normal volumes. A 9.95 million day is roughly 25-30% above average. That's noteworthy but not exceptional. The aggressive analyst is reading conviction into what is essentially normal volatility.

More critically, the macro report explicitly states: "No news headlines were returned for V during the 7-day analysis window." Zero institutional commentary. Zero analyst upgrades. Zero sector momentum. If this were genuine institutional accumulation, we would expect to see contemporaneous news flow, analyst notes, or sector rotation into payments. We see none of this. The MACD bounce could just as easily reflect short-covering or algorithmic mean reversion after an oversold condition. It does *not* constitute evidence of a durable reversal.

Now, I'm not opposed to buybacks—they're tax-efficient. Revenue grew only 9.9% in FY2024, while net income grew 14.3%. That spread—operating leverage expansion—is real but ultimately finite. The company can't indefinitely grow earnings 4-5% faster than revenue without hitting some ceiling. And here's the critical question: what happens to that spread during an economic slowdown?

Visa's exposure is direct: lower transaction volumes equal lower service fees. The company's 50.8% net margins assume stable payment volumes. Strip out 15-20% of transaction volumes in a recession, and those margins compress rapidly because the fixed-cost base doesn't decline proportionally.

The forward PE of 24.44x implies the market is pricing in 27.6% EPS growth. That's not sustainable. We're being asked to buy a stock near 52-week highs on the assumption that exceptional growth continues indefinitely. That's not conservative investing—that's gambling on perpetual acceleration.
