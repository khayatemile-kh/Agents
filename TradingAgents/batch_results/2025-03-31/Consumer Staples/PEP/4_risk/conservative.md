In other words, the company is running faster just to stay in place. The aggressive analyst celebrates "operating income still growing 0.08%" as if that's acceptable. Let me translate: that's essentially no growth. That's a company where pricing power is being fully offset by cost pressures downstream.

## **The Buyback Defense Falls Apart Under Scrutiny**

But here's what the aggressive analyst conveniently glosses over: **the company's net debt is $35.80B, and it increased from $34.39B in 2023 and $34.12B in 2022.**

This is the smoking gun. The company is executing share buybacks *while increasing net debt.* That's not efficient capital allocation—that's financial engineering. What should be happening in a mature, cash-generative business with elevated leverage is debt reduction to improve balance sheet flexibility and reduce refinancing risk. Instead, PepsiCo is returning capital to equity holders through buybacks while the debt load inches higher.

Why does this matter? Because it reveals the true nature of the buyback dependency. The aggressive analyst wants to treat buybacks as a neutral form of capital allocation—"return excess cash or waste it elsewhere." But when you're executing buybacks while your leverage ratio is climbing, you're making an implicit bet that:

1. Interest rates stay manageable (not guaranteed in 2025)
2. The company maintains access to capital markets at current spreads (not guaranteed if there's economic deterioration)
3. Revenue growth reignites organically (not evidenced by the data)

**Here's the risk:** If any of those assumptions break, the company discovers it prioritized returning cash to shareholders over building balance sheet resilience. That's not discipline—that's complacency. And it's exactly the pattern we see in companies that get into trouble during macro downturns.

The aggressive analyst cites a 3.8% FCF yield as "attractive," but they're not adjusting for the fact that this FCF is already partially committed to dividends. The *unlevered* FCF yield—the cash actually available for discretionary allocation—is only about 1.8% after dividend payments. That's not a strong margin of safety.
