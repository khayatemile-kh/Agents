That's not a "false" bounce—that's a *pattern of structural support*.

The Aggressive Analyst wants to interpret this as "proof that the bounces fail," but the data shows something different: they're stabilizing, and each bounce is finding a floor.

## The Aggressive Analyst's Debt Analysis Misses the Critical Context: FCF Stabilization

Now let me address the debt argument, because this is where things get really interesting. The Aggressive Analyst says EL's 4.96x leverage (debt-to-EBITDA) is a "trap" that could "spring" if revenues continue declining. They frame the 72% CapEx cut as "distress management" and "capitulation."

The Estée Lauder business model doesn't require $3+ billion annual CapEx to function. It's not building semiconductor fabs or oil platforms. It's a prestige beauty company with established retail channels, DTC infrastructure, and global brand recognition. A $900 million run rate for CapEx is *reasonable* for maintaining and optimizing existing assets, not reinvesting in growth from zero.

Here's what this means for the debt picture: If FY2024 FCF of $1.44 billion becomes a sustainable baseline—and the inventory reduction suggests management believes it can—then the 4.96x leverage multiple is actually *on a downward trajectory*. The company is generating $1.44 billion in free cash flow against $9.83 billion in debt. That's a 6.8-year paydown horizon if they hold CapEx steady and leverage starts compressing.
