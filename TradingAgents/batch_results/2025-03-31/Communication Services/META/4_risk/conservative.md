## The PEG Ratio Trap: Why 0.82 Doesn't Mean What the Aggressive Thesis Claims

The aggressive analyst treats the 0.82 PEG ratio as a "screaming undervaluation signal." Let me examine that claim more carefully, because it hinges on a critical assumption that I believe is flawed.

A PEG below 1.0 typically indicates *historical* undervaluation. It compares the P/E ratio to the historical or expected earnings growth rate. The calculation: META's forward P/E of 17.55x divided by expected forward earnings growth of 31.7% equals 0.82.

But here's the problem: **that 31.7% forward growth rate is consensus analyst expectation, not reality.** And consensus expectations are frequently wrong, especially during market dislocations. More importantly, that forward growth rate was *priced in* when META was trading at $712.85 in mid-February. The market was paying 17.55x forward earnings *plus a cyclical premium* when sentiment was positive.

Now, the market has re-rated META lower. It's still at 17.55x forward earnings (the stock fell, but so did near-term sentiment about growth sustainability). But the *cyclical premium* has evaporated. This is the critical distinction: **a low PEG in a deteriorating momentum environment often signals that the market doubts the growth trajectory is achievable at *current prices*, not that the stock is a bargain.**

Consider the macro context more carefully. The world affairs report acknowledges a data void on Fed policy, inflation trajectory, and macro volatility indicators. We cannot assess whether interest rate policy will support or undermine META's growth. The 31.7% forward earnings growth assumption might be perfectly valid if rates stabilize at 4.5%. But if the Fed holds rates elevated at 5.5% due to persistent inflation, that growth rate becomes much harder to achieve—and suddenly 17.55x forward earnings *is* expensive.

The aggressive thesis treats the fundamental metrics as if they're disconnected from the macro regime. They're not. And right now, we're in a regime of uncertainty.
