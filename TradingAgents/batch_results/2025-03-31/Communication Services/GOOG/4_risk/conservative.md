## **On Valuation: 7-8x Earnings Is Only Cheap If Growth Is Certain**

Now to the valuation asymmetry argument, which is the emotional centerpiece of the aggressive case. The logic is seductive: Alphabet grows 25-28% but trades at 7-8x earnings, which is "distressed." Therefore, mispricing. Therefore, buy.

But this analysis makes a hidden assumption that I believe is dangerous: **it assumes that the 25-28% growth rate will persist or decelerate gradually rather than cliff off suddenly.** And there is material evidence that this assumption may be precisely wrong.

Let me be concrete. The trader's reasoning specifically mentions that bears raise legitimate concerns about "ad-deceleration and search-disruption." The response is to dismiss these as insufficient to justify current valuations. But I need to ask: have we actually modeled what happens if these concerns prove prescient?

Here's what I see in the fundamentals that the aggressive analysis does not adequately stress:

**First, advertising is cyclical, and we are in a macro environment of rising interest rates and policy uncertainty.** The Fed funds rate has been elevated throughout 2024 and into 2025. The 10-year Treasury yield has been volatile. The world affairs report notes data collection gaps, but the absence of positive macro news is itself a risk signal—silence on Fed policy, silence on CPI, silence on labor market trends. When macro data goes silent and equity markets are volatile, what typically happens? Advertisers cut spending. CPM bids decline. YouTube performance softens.

Alphabet's advertising business generates roughly 80% of revenue. A 10-15% deceleration in ad growth isn't a "haircut to growth" that still leaves 10-15% expansion. It's a potential cliff. And the market's -15.5% month-to-date decline suggests institutional investors are pricing in precisely that scenario.

**Second, the 25-28% growth figure appears to be operating income growth, not revenue growth.** Looking at the fundamentals report, total revenue grew 13.9% YoY (2024 vs 2023). Operating income grew 33.3%. That's not 25-28% revenue growth—that's margin expansion on more modest top-line growth. If ad revenue decelerates from 13.9% to, say, 8-10%, and margins compress under competitive pressure from AI-native search alternatives (OpenAI, Perplexity, etc.), then operating income doesn't grow 25%—it potentially contracts.

The aggressive analyst says "even if advertising growth slows from 25-28% to 15% or 10%, Alphabet still generates extraordinary returns." But that's the wrong comparison. The question isn't whether growth from 25% to 15% is still strong—of course it is. The question is: what if revenue growth goes from 14% to 5-7%, and margins compress from 32% operating margin to 28-30%? Then operating income is flat to negative. At that point, 7-8x earnings is not cheap—it's fair value or even expensive.

And here's the kicker: **the market doesn't reprrice on consensus about gradual deceleration. It reprices on fear of structural disruption.** The sharp March decline suggests the market has moved from "Alphabet will slow but remain strong" to "Alphabet faces a real disruption problem in search and advertising." Whether that fear is justified, I cannot know. But I can know this: the market's repricing suggests it believes the risk is material. And institutional capital is exiting. When institutions exit, retail typically follows, and the selling accelerates.

## **On the Balance Sheet: Financial Fortress Does Not Protect Against Revenue Collapse**

The aggressive analyst emphasizes the fortress balance sheet—$73B net cash, 0.069 debt-to-equity, $72.76B free cash flow. The implication is that this financial strength eliminates downside risk because the company can "wait out cycles."

I need to respectfully challenge this framing. A strong balance sheet protects a company from *financial distress* when revenues decline. It does not protect shareholders from *equity value destruction* when revenues decline.

Let me be explicit. If Alphabet's ad revenue growth decelerates from 14% to 5%, and the market reprices the stock from 7-8x earnings to 5-6x earnings (which is what happens in a secular disruption scenario—think early Microsoft post-internet, or Yahoo post-Google), then the equity value per share declines materially, regardless of how much cash is on the balance sheet.

The balance sheet strength means the company survives. It does not mean your equity position survives intact.

Moreover, I want to flag a specific risk that the fortress balance sheet actually *enables rather than prevents*: shareholder capital destruction through poor strategic decisions made from a position of strength. When a company has $95B in cash and investments, there is enormous temptation to deploy it inefficiently—overpriced acquisitions, poorly timed buybacks at high valuations, CapEx splurges that don't generate returns.

Looking at the fundamentals, Alphabet increased CapEx from $31.5B (2022) to $32.3B (2023) to $52.5B (2024). That's a 67% increase in capital expenditure in two years. For what? The data center investments for AI infrastructure. But here's my question: does the market believe these CapEx investments will generate adequate returns? Or is the market concerned that Alphabet is burning massive capital to compete in AI commoditization where returns are uncertain?

The sharp March decline and the exit of institutional money suggests the market is skeptical. And I think the market has a point. When a software company with 60% gross margins is suddenly spending $52B annually on CapEx, that's a structural shift in the business model. That's closer to a capital-intensive infrastructure play than a high-margin software franchise. And capital-intensive infrastructure plays don't trade at premium multiples.
